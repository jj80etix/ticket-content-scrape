## Q8 — Where's the next big opportunity?

The clearest convergent signal this month is that **open, neutral primary-distribution infrastructure is becoming a structural battleground, not just a vendor nicety**. StubHub is scaling "Open Distribution" to roughly 10 live primary-ticketing integrations — recent additions are an expanded AudienceView Unlimited deal and a Spektrix integration spanning 600+ arts organizations — reporting 84% YoY growth in directly-integrated sales and one MLB team generating $40M+ through the program ([[articles/2026-09-01-stubhub-bets-on-open-distribution-as-ticketing-industry-rethinks-the-walled-gard|StubHub's Open Distribution]]). vivenu, TicketNetwork and TicketSocket are building the same layer independently, and the pending DOJ/Live Nation settlement would force Ticketmaster to build a mirror-image standardized third-party distribution interface — regulation chasing the same model StubHub is building commercially. But two of the vendors building toward this future are also warning the mandated version could be hollow: SeatGeek's Tunney Act filing documents venues (BSE Global/Barclays Center, a 2024 NBA deal, a 2025 NBA/NHL venue that called SeatGeek's tech "far superior") staying with or reverting to Ticketmaster for fear of losing Live Nation-promoted shows regardless of open-distribution rules ([[articles/2026-09-04-seatgeek-urges-rejection-of-live-nation-settlement-warns-it-could-entrench-ticke|SeatGeek's Tunney Act filing]]), while TicketNetwork's own filing argues the settlement's "eligible primary provider" definition still lets Ticketmaster gate access based on a rival's *resale* conduct, not just security ([[articles/2026-09-22-ticketnetwork-targets-resale-conditions-in-doj-s-live-nation-ticketmaster-settle|TicketNetwork's resale-conditions filing]]). What this means: the open-distribution opportunity is real and multiple independent vendors are racing to build it commercially ahead of any court order — but the fine print of eligibility/access rules, not the plumbing, will decide who actually benefits.

A second thread is **international and non-English-language growth continuing to outrun capacity**, exactly as it did in August. Ticketmaster folded its new Uruguay operation (from the August Red UTS acquisition) into its existing Chile team rather than standing up a local entity — a cheap, repeatable regional-rollup pattern ([[articles/2026-09-07-ticketmaster-s-new-uruguay-business-to-be-run-from-chile|Ticketmaster's Uruguay/Chile structure]]). In São Paulo, Ticketmaster's winning bid for the MorumBIS stadium contract and rival NewC's attempt to match it turned almost entirely on financial terms, not product (more below under Q20) ([[articles/2026-09-18-newc-moves-to-match-ticketmaster-offer-as-s-o-paulo-ticketing-fight-escalates|NewC/Ticketmaster São Paulo fight]]). And at the Goldman Sachs Communacopia conference, Live Nation CFO Joe Berchtold said non-English-language shows are now 30% of the company's top 50 global tours (up from 8% pre-pandemic) and that arena infrastructure is a binding constraint in roughly two-thirds of its top 75 touring markets ([[articles/2026-09-11-non-english-language-shows-now-30-of-live-nation-s-top-50-tours|Non-English shows at 30% of LN's top tours]], [[articles/2026-09-22-the-biz-berchtold-talks-globally-at-goldman-sachs-ln-competitors-object-to-settl|Berchtold at Goldman Sachs]]). Read together: the growth frontier keeps being capacity-constrained international markets, and whoever's infrastructure (venues, ticketing, financing) gets there first keeps the edge.

A third thread complicates last month's AI euphoria rather than extending it: **AI in ticketing is maturing from a discovery feature to a data-foundation and trust problem**. At vivenu's AI Summit, speakers from StubHub, OVG360 and Playfly Sports agreed fragmented customer data — not AI itself — is the real obstacle to practical AI use, and some large-market teams still manage parts of their data in spreadsheets ([[articles/2026-09-28-vivenu-summit-puts-fragmented-fan-data-at-center-of-ticketing-s-ai-push|vivenu's AI Summit]]) — directly echoing vivenu's own promotional framing two weeks earlier that "owning your ticketing data was the easy part" and the hard part is connecting and activating it ([[articles/2026-09-02-mastering-ai-in-ticketing-vivenu-on-connecting-and-activating-data|vivenu on activating ticketing data]]). Meanwhile a Tixel survey found 68% of ticket buyers say AI/algorithmic recommendations make it *harder* to trust which events to attend, even as major platforms keep shipping AI discovery ([[articles/2026-09-18-two-thirds-of-fans-say-ai-and-algorithms-confuse-event-discovery-report-finds|Tixel's AI-discovery trust survey]]). What this means: the next AI build bet for a primary platform isn't another consumer-facing recommendation widget — it's the unglamorous data-unification layer underneath, paired with explainability, since the patron-trust data point suggests shipping more opaque AI discovery right now could backfire.

A fourth, smaller but notable thread: **fan-data-owning platforms are building their own registration-gated access/loyalty layer around the ticket transaction**. Spotify expanded its Reserved program (personalized share cards, setlist.fm playlists, an in-app event hub) and reports nearly 100,000 tickets reserved since its June launch ([[articles/2026-09-24-spotify-adds-setlists-venue-info-to-reserved-concert-ticket-program|Spotify Reserved expansion]]), while also co-presenting a curated Live Nation Urban festival in Dallas ([[articles/2026-09-10-spotify-augments-live-activity-in-us-with-live-nation-urban-event|Spotify/Live Nation Urban]]). The Rangers launched a $150/month "Rangers Pass" subscription guaranteeing lower-bowl seats at roughly $75/game — undercutting what many existing season-ticket holders pay — while barring resale ([[articles/2026-09-23-rangers-launch-75-lower-bowl-ticket-pass-undercutting-prices-paid-by-season-memb|Rangers Pass]]). And registration/ballot-gated onsales (AXS's Fair AXS, Oasis's Live '27 registration model) kept spreading as the default anti-scalping mechanism for top-tier onsales (more under Q18 below). Taken together with Q17's loyalty signal, this is a credible build bet: the next differentiator isn't just who sells the ticket, but who owns the registration/engagement layer around it.

## Q19 — What are clients asking for that no vendor serves well? (whitespace)

**Verifiable original-ticket-price data remains unbuilt, and the compliance cost of that gap is now stacking up across three jurisdictions in a single month.** Ontario fined SeatGeek Canada C$25,000 (including C$10,000 specifically for facilitating a resale without proof of original price) and then fined StubHub Canada C$20,000 under the same framework ([[articles/2026-09-03-ontario-fines-seatgeek-over-ticket-resale-rules-company-says-remain-unclear|Ontario fines SeatGeek]], [[articles/2026-09-22-stubhub-fined-under-ontario-ticket-resale-cap-verification-dispute-continues|StubHub fined, appeals]]); SeatGeek's VP of Government Affairs calls the requirement "unworkable" because many sellers, including season-ticket holders, simply have no way to produce their ticket's original face value. The same undefined "original ticket price" term is now live in Massachusetts' competing resale-cap bills ([[articles/2026-09-25-healey-doubles-down-on-ticket-resale-price-cap-in-massachusetts-supplemental-bud|Healey's H.5679]]) and in the UK's draft resale-cap legislation ([[articles/2026-09-28-uk-steps-up-work-on-ticket-resale-cap-bill-as-officials-consider-black-market-ri|UK resale-cap bill]]). No vendor — primary or secondary — has shipped a verifiable, exportable original-price record tied to ticket issuance; whoever builds one first turns a recurring regulatory liability into a feature regulators and resale partners alike would pay for.

**Fragmented fan data is blocking AI for everyone, including the most sophisticated operators.** vivenu's own AI Summit heard from StubHub, OVG360 and Playfly Sports that data spread across ticketing, CRM, premium seating, sponsorship and F&B systems — not AI capability — is the actual bottleneck, with some large-market teams still tracking parts of it in spreadsheets ([[articles/2026-09-28-vivenu-summit-puts-fragmented-fan-data-at-center-of-ticketing-s-ai-push|vivenu's AI Summit]]). No vendor in this month's corpus claims to have solved cross-system fan-data unification as a packaged product rather than a bespoke integration project.

**Cancellation/escrow protection and cross-channel listing sync are still manual and inconsistent.** New York's AG is suing Evolutions Festival's promoters for allegedly spending $243,158 in advance ticket revenue instead of escrowing it as state law requires, after which only 11 of 521 buyers recovered any money — and only via card-issuer chargebacks ([[articles/2026-09-30-new-york-ag-sues-evolutions-festival-promoters-for-allegedly-scamming-ticket-buy|NY AG v. Evolutions Festival]]). Separately, UK promoter Xfire Live canceled four shows in weeks while ticketholders went days without direct cancellation notice and tickets for one show stayed listed for sale even as the cancellation was being finalized ([[articles/2026-09-30-promoter-xfire-under-fire-from-artists-for-edinburgh-concert-cancellations|Xfire Live cancellations]]); the Beach Boys' September cancellations showed the identical lag, with Ticketmaster and AXS still listing canceled dates as on sale after venues had announced them ([[articles/2026-09-24-beach-boys-cancel-september-comeback-shows-leaving-october-tour-return-uncertain|Beach Boys cancellation lag]]). No vendor has shipped real-time, cross-channel cancellation-status sync or promoter-side fund verification as a standard feature — clients are left to notice stale listings themselves.

**A published, auto-triggering "material lineup change" refund threshold still doesn't exist, and the Ed Sheeran/Macklemore saga shows the cost of that gap at scale.** Massachusetts AG complaints over Gillette Stadium refunds grew from 9 to roughly 200 before Ticketmaster agreed to a formal, promoter-authorized 24-hour refund window ([[articles/2026-09-24-ticketmaster-commits-to-ed-sheeran-refunds-after-massachusetts-ag-demand|MA AG forces Sheeran refund window]]), and even after that, fans reported refunds were granted only on request with no public policy change announced ([[articles/2026-09-23-ed-sheeran-gillette-refunds-reported-but-fans-still-haven-t-been-told-they-re-av|Gillette refunds, unannounced]]) while resale prices softened amid the uncertainty ([[articles/2026-09-18-ed-sheeran-refunds-begin-in-philadelphia-as-softening-ticket-market-puts-pressur|Philadelphia refunds, softening market]]). This is the same gap August's Bon Jovi refund case flagged on a single-show scale — September shows what happens when it plays out at stadium-tour scale with regulatory intervention required to force a response.

**Reconciling upstream identity vetting with a platform's own fraud screening remains unsolved, producing false positives for already-verified fans.** Oasis's Live '27 registration-only sale used Openstage's "Ticket Unlocks" identity/behavior screening before fans ever reached checkout — yet some verified, code-holding fans still triggered Ticketmaster's own independent bot-detection mid-purchase, hitting error loops and payment failures ([[articles/2026-09-28-oasis-built-a-registration-only-ticket-sale-to-avoid-chaos-fans-still-hit-ticket|Oasis registration sale, Ticketmaster friction]]). No vendor has a single source-of-truth for "this buyer is already cleared" that upstream vetting partners and platform-side fraud systems both honor.

## Q20 — Why do clients churn or choose competitors? (win/loss)

**Financial terms, not product quality, decided this month's clearest head-to-head contract fight — and a vertically integrated bidder's affiliate leverage is the live complaint.** Ticketmaster's winning bid for São Paulo FC's MorumBIS stadium offered R$110M in advance revenue and R$30M for a five-year suite deal versus rival NewC's R$90M and R$13M, with financial terms controlling 75% of the tender's scoring weight; NewC is now trying to match those numbers and separately alleges Ticketmaster's parent Live Nation's exclusive MorumBIS concert-promotion rights (through 2031) gave it an unfair vertically-integrated edge on the suite/concert-inventory portion of the bid — language that explicitly echoes the US DOJ v. Live Nation/Ticketmaster conditioning allegations ([[articles/2026-09-18-newc-moves-to-match-ticketmaster-offer-as-s-o-paulo-ticketing-fight-escalates|NewC vs. Ticketmaster, São Paulo]]). What this means: this is the same lesson as August's Connecticut/OVG bidding-bypass case — when a dominant, vertically integrated bidder competes for a major venue contract, cash advances and affiliate leverage can out-weigh technology differences entirely, and procurement-process fairness (not feature parity) is what actually determines whether an independent can win.

**StubHub's reputational and legal drumbeat kept compounding through the month, with independent venues now suing over the same alleged tactic in back-to-back months.** New Orleans' Howlin' Wolf sued StubHub over "false scarcity" marketing — the venue had 100 GA tickets available directly at $20 while StubHub advertised "Only 1% of tickets left!" at $76 with zero actual sales — explicitly following Vermont's Stone Church lawsuit against StubHub in August ([[articles/2026-09-18-new-orleans-howlin-wolf-latest-venue-to-sue-stubhub-citing-false-scarcity-tactic|Howlin' Wolf sues StubHub]]). In the same month, StubHub absorbed a public dispute with "Marlins Man" over a denied refund amid ongoing World Cup ticket-delivery class actions ([[articles/2026-09-01-marlins-man-ticket-dispute-adds-to-stubhub-s-mounting-consumer-scrutiny|Marlins Man/StubHub dispute]]) and was fined C$20,000 by Ontario over resale-cap and price-verification violations ([[articles/2026-09-22-stubhub-fined-under-ontario-ticket-resale-cap-verification-dispute-continues|StubHub's Ontario fine]]). What this means: two independent venues in two different cities suing over the identical "false scarcity" allegation inside two months is a pattern, not noise — a plausible leading indicator that venues wary of reputational contagion will favor marketplaces that can show verified, real-time inventory over one accumulating this many distinct trust failures at once.

**A second platform's fee-opacity legal exposure is broadening in parallel.** Vivid Seats' Pennsylvania hidden-fee lawsuit grew from one county to seven in September, with the amended complaint alleging dark-pattern checkout design and misleading fee descriptions under the state's consumer-protection law ([[articles/2026-09-10-seven-pennsylvania-das-now-pursuing-vivid-seats-hidden-fee-lawsuit|Seven PA DAs vs. Vivid Seats]]) — reinforcing that fee-transparency litigation risk is a real, escalating factor in which platforms clients and regulators are willing to keep doing business with, not a StubHub-only story.

**Where clients did make an active, positive choice this month, data and brand ownership — not price — was the stated reason.** vivenu won AC Grand Rapids' inaugural ticketing contract on an explicit promise that the club keeps its fan data, branding and customer relationship from day one ([[articles/2026-09-23-ac-grand-rapids-taps-vivenu-for-ticketing-ahead-of-inaugural-2027-season|AC Grand Rapids picks vivenu]]), and Tixr's new UK deal with grassroots operator Propaganda Independent Venues leads with the identical guarantee — PIV keeps full ownership of its fan data ([[articles/2026-09-15-tixr-strikes-partnership-with-propaganda-independent-venues|Tixr/Propaganda Independent Venues]]). This continues August's pattern (vivenu/TicketNetwork) with two more concrete wins: for a certain segment of clients, "who owns my data" is now a bigger deciding factor than fee percentage.
