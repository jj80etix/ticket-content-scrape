# Research Explains Why Ticket Sellers Embrace Resale Controls That Can Reduce Consumer Benefits

## Summary

New academic research from UVA Darden's Pnina Feldman and Wharton's Gérard Cachon explains why ticket sellers have reversed decades of opposing resale to instead operate or partner with resale exchanges: capturing a commission on every resale transaction can out-earn any other selling strategy, especially for high-demand events. But the model finds authorized, seller-controlled resale produces less consumer surplus than unrestricted resale, creating a financial incentive for incumbents' resale-restriction advocacy that is separate from stated fraud/scalping rationales. The piece ties the findings directly to Live Nation/Ticketmaster's Face Value Exchange push, the DOJ settlement's resale-eligibility conditions (contested by SeatGeek and TicketNetwork), and pending state resale-cap legislation in Massachusetts.

### Highlights
- The model: authorized resale with revenue-maximizing commissions can out-earn advance-selling or unrestricted resale, but produces lower consumer surplus than either alternative.
- Feldman: "If the goal is to limit how much is extracted from fans, the better tool is a cap on the fee, not on the price" — relevant to Massachusetts's proposed 110%-of-face resale cap (reported Oct. 22).
- Live Nation/Ticketmaster's Face Value Exchange and Music Artists Coalition's resale-restriction lobbying overlap with the paper's identified financial incentives for original sellers to control resale.
- DOJ's proposed antitrust settlement would require competitors with primary+secondary businesses to honor rights-holders' resale restrictions to gain back-end access — a provision SeatGeek and TicketNetwork have objected to.

### Industry Problem
Original ticket sellers have a structural financial incentive to restrict independent resale and capture a share of secondary-market value — even though the research shows unrestricted resale delivers greater consumer surplus — creating tension between seller revenue optimization and patron/fan economic benefit, and shaping how resale-regulation and antitrust-remedy debates play out.

### Proposed Solution
The paper itself proposes no product, but argues policymakers should prefer commission caps over price caps to protect consumers without destroying resale's allocative benefits. For an independent primary platform, the signal is to watch how DOJ settlement resale-eligibility terms and state commission/price-cap legislation evolve, since either could reshape what resale terms a primary platform can offer or must honor to gain back-end access to incumbent infrastructure.

For decades, concert promoters, sports leagues and other ticket issuers fought against ticket resale, viewing fans and professional resellers as competitors who threatened their ability to control pricing and maximize revenue. Today, many of those same businesses operate official resale exchanges, partner with secondary marketplaces and collect fees when tickets change hands.

New academic research offers an explanation for that reversal: Advances in digital ticketing have allowed original sellers to turn resale from a source of competition into a potentially more profitable extension of their own businesses.

But the findings also expose an important economic trade-off. The resale arrangements that maximize revenue for original ticket sellers do not necessarily maximize benefits for consumers, with unrestricted resale producing greater consumer surplus than seller-controlled exchanges under the researchers’ model.

The conclusions come from [*The Enigma of Ticket Exchanges (and Other Reselling Markets) and the Design of Commission Fees*](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6899618&utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), a working paper by University of Virginia Darden School of Business professor Pnina Feldman and University of Pennsylvania Wharton School professor Gérard P. Cachon.

In detailed written responses to TicketNews, Feldman explained that the crucial development is not necessarily ownership of a resale marketplace, but the ability to collect revenue from transactions occurring after the original ticket sale.

“In our paper, the critical feature is the seller’s ability to earn a share of every resale transaction,” Feldman said. “Control over the marketplace matters only because it is what makes that fee collectible.”

That distinction is particularly relevant as the live entertainment industry debates how much authority artists, promoters, venues and primary ticketing companies should retain over tickets after consumers purchase them.

## How Resale Became a Revenue Opportunity

The researchers examined four approaches to ticket sales: selling exclusively shortly before an event, selling tickets in advance without permitting resale, allowing unrestricted resale, and allowing resale through an authorized system where the original seller collects commissions.

Advance selling has substantial advantages for event organizers. It allows them to sell inventory months before a concert or sporting event, when consumers may still be uncertain about whether they can attend or how much they ultimately value the experience.

That uncertainty creates opportunities for resale. Some early purchasers eventually discover they no longer want their tickets, while other consumers become more interested in attending as the event approaches.

An unrestricted resale marketplace allows those buyers and sellers to trade, moving tickets toward consumers who value them more.

But from the original seller’s perspective, those transactions also create competition.

Consumers who purchase tickets early can subsequently offer them for sale alongside inventory the original issuer may still be trying to sell.

In the researchers’ frictionless model, that competition eliminates the financial advantage of advance selling. When unrestricted resale is available, the original issuer cannot earn more than it would by waiting until shortly before the event to sell its inventory.

The economics change dramatically when the issuer can collect a commission on subsequent transactions.

Rather than simply competing against tickets already sold, the original seller participates financially in their resale. It receives revenue from the initial purchase and can earn additional revenue when that ticket changes hands.

Commissions also influence how many ticket holders participate in the secondary market, allowing the issuer to regulate the amount of competing resale inventory available.

“A higher fee means fewer ticket holders choose to sell, which keeps the resale price from collapsing when many people want out and few want in,” Feldman explained.

Under the model’s assumptions, properly designed authorized resale arrangements can produce more revenue for the original seller than any other selling mechanism, particularly when demand substantially exceeds available ticket inventory.

The researchers also found that the optimal percentage commission can decline for especially popular events because strong demand already supports higher resale prices, even as resale commissions become a larger share of the seller’s overall revenue.

Importantly, these financial benefits do not require the original issuer to operate the resale marketplace itself.

Feldman said a contractual agreement with a third-party platform that provides the original seller with a share of resale transactions can produce the same outcome.

An authorized exchange charging no commission to benefit the original seller would, in the model, produce the same economic result as unrestricted resale.

The research therefore identifies a financial incentive for original sellers to participate in secondary transactions, but does not establish that consumers must be restricted to one particular marketplace for those benefits to exist.

## Who Benefits When Original Sellers Control Resale?

The distinction between allowing resale and controlling its economic terms becomes especially important when examining consumer welfare.

Feldman and Cachon’s model finds that authorized resale with revenue-maximizing commissions produces less consumer surplus than unrestricted resale or selling tickets only shortly before an event.

Consumer surplus represents the economic benefit consumers receive beyond what they pay. It differs from total social welfare, which considers the overall value generated by transactions.

“In our model, consumer welfare overall is always lower under a seller-controlled exchange than under an open resale market or spot selling,” Feldman told TicketNews.

The research identifies two mechanisms behind that result.

Consumers directly pay commissions on resale transactions, while the commissions also influence the effective prices and resale activity within the marketplace.

Higher fees can discourage ticket holders from offering inventory, reducing competitive pressure that might otherwise push prices downward.

That creates a potential conflict between the interests of original ticket issuers and consumers. An issuer seeking to maximize revenue may benefit from limiting resale competition, while fans shopping for tickets may benefit from more sellers competing to offer lower prices.

However, the researchers do not conclude that authorized resale is inherently harmful.

Compared with prohibiting ticket transfers altogether, an authorized exchange allows unwanted tickets to move to people who value them more. That can improve ticket allocation, increase overall economic welfare and, in many circumstances, generate greater consumer surplus than a system of nontransferable advance tickets.

The model also predicts that authorized resale can encourage original sellers to release more inventory earlier and may strengthen incentives to expand event capacity.

Those are meaningful potential benefits, even if the original seller captures a greater portion of the value generated by resale.

The findings also have limitations. The model assumes reliable, efficient transactions and does not account for differences in fraud prevention, ticket authentication or delivery risk between marketplaces. Nor does it analyze competition among independent resale platforms.

Feldman cautioned against extending the findings too far into that territory.

“We do not model competition among resale platforms, so I want to be careful predicting what would happen,” she said.

The analysis also treats artists, promoters and ticketing providers as a single seller, rather than examining how actual commercial agreements divide resale revenue among those parties.

Consequently, the research does not establish how any individual ticketing company profits from a particular arrangement. It does, however, identify why businesses involved in the original sale may have an economic interest in retaining influence over subsequent transactions.

## Live Nation’s Push for Greater Control Over Resale

Those incentives provide an important framework for examining how Live Nation Entertainment, Ticketmaster and influential music-industry organizations have approached ticket resale regulation.

Live Nation has increasingly advocated policies that allow artists and other rights holders to dictate the conditions under which tickets can be resold, including restrictions on prices and transfers.

In a [February 2026 public statement](https://news.livenationentertainment.com/news/live-nation-ticketmaster-stand-with-artists-calling-for-meaningful-ticketing-reform?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), the company promoted Ticketmaster’s Face Value Exchange, which allows artists to restrict transfers and require resale at the original purchase price.

Live Nation argues such measures protect fans from speculative purchasing, inflated resale prices and other abuses. It has advocated stronger federal enforcement against ticket-buying bots, prohibitions on speculative listings and broader artist authority over resale conditions.

Those concerns are not inherently illegitimate. Ticket fraud, misleading listings and automated purchasing have created genuine problems for consumers, and artists may have legitimate reasons for seeking to make tickets available at prices they consider affordable.

But the economic research introduces another consideration: The ability to regulate resale transactions can also provide substantial financial advantages to original ticket sellers.

The overlap between artist-control arguments and commercial interests has become increasingly relevant as concert-industry organizations seek legislative changes governing secondary ticket markets.

The Music Artists Coalition, founded with the involvement of influential manager Irving Azoff, has become a prominent advocate for tighter resale restrictions.

Azoff, a former Ticketmaster CEO and Live Nation executive chairman, remains on the organization’s board.

In May, MAC [promoted polling supporting artist authority over resale prices and stronger restrictions on secondary-market transactions](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/05/irving-azoff-aligned-music-artists-coalition-pushes-survey-backing-resale-caps-at-core-of-industry-lobbying-efforts/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content).

The organization is also part of the broader Fix the Tix coalition, which has advocated resale-price limits, restrictions on speculative listings and other legislation intended to strengthen protections for fans.

These organizations do not share identical interests or positions on every issue. The National Independent Venue Association, which leads Fix the Tix, has separately been a vocal critic of Live Nation’s market power and the Justice Department’s proposed antitrust settlement.

Nevertheless, their alignment on certain resale restrictions demonstrates the political influence behind proposals that could expand the authority of artists and original ticket issuers over secondary transactions.

Live Nation has also maintained a substantial direct lobbying operation. [Federal lobbying disclosures compiled by Open America](https://openamerica.io/lobbying/client/53910/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) show approximately $2.7 million in reported federal lobbying spending during 2025 and another $1.16 million in filings covering the first three quarters of 2026.

Those expenditures span numerous issues, including ticketing, competition, venue security and other live-entertainment matters. They cannot all be attributed to resale regulation.

Still, the policy debate raises a question closely related to Feldman and Cachon’s findings: When an original ticket seller advocates restrictions on independent resale, how much of the proposed arrangement protects consumers, and how much strengthens the seller’s control over the economic value of tickets after purchase?

The research cannot establish the motivations of any particular company or lobbying organization. It does demonstrate that original sellers have financial incentives to seek that control, even when consumers might receive greater economic benefits under less restrictive arrangements.

## Antitrust Battles Put Ticketing Control Under Scrutiny

The tension between marketplace competition and control over ticket distribution is also central to Live Nation and Ticketmaster’s ongoing legal challenges.

On April 15, a federal jury [found Live Nation and Ticketmaster liable for anticompetitive conduct](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-and-coalition-states-win-trial-against-live-nation-and?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), concluding that Ticketmaster unlawfully maintained monopoly power in primary ticketing services at major concert venues and that Live Nation violated antitrust laws involving its amphitheater and concert-promotion businesses.

The verdict followed the Justice Department’s decision to settle its portion of the case in March. The states that continued litigating subsequently won their jury verdict and are pursuing remedies, while Live Nation and Ticketmaster have challenged the findings.

The proposed federal settlement remains subject to judicial review.

Although that antitrust verdict did not determine whether particular resale-transfer restrictions are unlawful, the broader proceedings have highlighted the competitive significance of controlling ticketing infrastructure.

Under the Justice Department’s proposed judgment, Ticketmaster would be required to create opportunities for eligible competing primary marketplaces to distribute tickets through its back-end technology.

But the [proposed settlement also contains resale-related eligibility conditions](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/09/ticketnetwork-live-nation-ticketmaster-resale-settlement/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content).

Certain competitors that operate both primary and secondary ticketing businesses would have to honor qualifying artist and rights-holder requests concerning resale-price limits, geographic restrictions and other secondary-market conditions to receive access under the new system.

Competitors including TicketNetwork and SeatGeek have objected to those provisions, arguing that access to primary ticketing infrastructure should not depend on a marketplace’s willingness to enforce private resale restrictions unrelated to fraud prevention or ticket security.

The concern illustrates a practical distinction that Feldman’s research helps illuminate.

An original seller’s financial participation in resale does not necessarily require restricting consumers to a single marketplace. But when ticket credentials, transfer permissions and access to primary inventory remain controlled by an incumbent provider, restrictions on those systems can affect the ability of independent platforms to compete.

The paper does not resolve those antitrust questions. Its contribution is identifying the underlying financial incentives that make control over resale transactions attractive to original sellers in the first place.

## What the Research Means for Resale Regulation

The policy implications extend beyond the question of which marketplace consumers can use.

Feldman’s research also examines the difference between limiting resale prices and limiting the commissions collected on those transactions.

A resale-price cap below the market-clearing price can leave more buyers seeking tickets than sellers willing to offer them at the permitted price.

A cap on commissions, by contrast, can limit the amount collected from resale transactions while allowing prices to respond to supply and demand.

Feldman argues the distinction is important for policymakers seeking to protect consumers without undermining the resale market’s ability to allocate tickets.

“If the goal is to limit how much is extracted from fans, the better tool is a cap on the fee, not on the price,” she told TicketNews.

The researchers found that percentage-based commissions combined with a maximum dollar fee per transaction can achieve nearly as much original-seller revenue as more complicated fee arrangements.

That does not establish precisely how much consumers would save under such a policy. The numerical results principally measure seller revenue, and actual savings would depend on the structure of the market and how fees are distributed.

Nevertheless, the distinction has immediate relevance to legislation such as the proposals under consideration in Massachusetts.

As [TicketNews reported October 22](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/10/massachusetts-house-ticket-resale-cap-consumer-concerns/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), lawmakers are weighing measures that would generally limit covered ticket resales to 110% of their original price, while differing on whether additional limits should apply to marketplace fees.

Those restrictions would regulate the amount individual ticket holders can receive when reselling, without imposing comparable limits on the original prices established by artists, promoters and primary ticketing platforms.

Feldman’s research identifies a broader relationship between those initial prices and the conditions imposed on subsequent transactions.

“The seller is really selling a bundle: a ticket plus the terms on which it can be resold,” she said. “The fee is already priced into what fans will pay at the start.”

The finding suggests that resale rights have economic value to consumers from the moment they initially purchase a ticket.

Whether a buyer can freely transfer a ticket, must use an authorized marketplace, or faces substantial commissions when reselling can all influence the value of the original purchase.

For policymakers, that makes the distinction between protecting legitimate transactions and giving original ticket issuers greater control over the secondary market especially consequential.

The researchers acknowledge that their working paper is theoretical, remains subject to peer review and does not measure the actual effects of existing ticketing policies. Feldman said transaction-level data from primary sales and authorized resale exchanges would be particularly valuable for testing the model’s predictions.

But the central economic insight offers a different way to evaluate the industry’s longstanding fight over resale.

The question is not simply whether consumers should be permitted to resell unwanted tickets. Resale itself can create value by moving tickets to people who want them more.

The question is who gets to control that activity and how much of the value created by those transactions should return to the original issuer.

“So, the policy question is not whether resale should exist,” Feldman said. “It is how much of the value that resale creates the seller should be allowed to keep.”
