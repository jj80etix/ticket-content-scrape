# A festival that can’t be franchised: The secret to Outside Lands’ longevity

## Summary

Ahead of Outside Lands' 18th edition, Another Planet Entertainment co-founder Allen Scott told IQ how the independent San Francisco festival (225,000 tickets, sold out) stays true to its Bay Area identity despite production costs up 75% since 2021 and growing consolidation across the live-events industry — offsetting rising costs with a GA+ upsell tier and treating its cannabis area (Grass Lands) as an experiential cost center rather than a profit driver.

### Key Points
- Production costs up 75% since 2021 ("mid-eight figures" to produce); California's high cost-of-living compounds the pressure.
- GA+ tier introduced specifically to subsidize/hold down base GA pricing (3-day GA ~$539; GA+ ~$799 this year).
- Grass Lands cannabis area has shifted from a sponsor-funded profit center to a cost center as sponsorship dollars dried up, but is kept for the on-brand experience.
- Scott frames staying independent and hyper-local (non-franchisable) as a deliberate strategy against industry consolidation, citing difficulty securing unique lineups amid fewer, larger promoters controlling festival bookings.

### Highlights
- 75% cost inflation since 2021 for a major independent festival is a concrete, quantified data point on organizer-side cost pressure.
- Explicit acknowledgment that industry consolidation is making exclusive/unique festival lineups harder to secure for independents.

### Industry Problem
Independent promoters/organizers face compounding cost inflation and a consolidating live-events landscape (fewer large promoters controlling artist access), squeezing margins and forcing tiered-pricing workarounds (GA+ upsells) just to keep base ticket prices accessible.

### Proposed Solution
The organizer's own response (tiered GA/GA+ pricing to cross-subsidize affordability) is a pattern worth watching for independent/mid-market clients on Etix's platform — flexible tiered-pricing and add-on/upsell tooling that lets organizers protect base-ticket affordability while capturing premium demand could be a relevant product angle for cost-squeezed independent promoters.

## A festival that can’t be franchised: The secret to Outside Lands’ longevity

Co-founder Allen Scott of Another Planet Entertainment tells IQ how the festival's California roots are fuelling its innovation

![Outside Lands](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Outside-Lands-©-Alive-Coverage-2.png)

Outside Lands 2025 © Alive Coverage

Ahead of the 18th edition of San Francisco’s Outside Lands, festival co-founder Allen Scott explains how the independent organiser is doubling down on its local identity despite soaring costs and increasing industry consolidation.

Organised by Another Planet Entertainment (APE), the multi-stage festival launches today (7 August) and runs until Sunday (9 August), with Charli xcx, The Strokes, and Rüfüs du Sol topping this year’s bill. It is another sell-out success for the indie organisers, with 225,000 tickets sold and a slate of fresh and local offerings on tap.

“It’s all about San Francisco,” Scott tells *IQ.* “The festival is not really something that we can franchise, because it’s so Bay Area and San Francisco – but we love that.”

Taking place at the city’s famed Golden Gate Park, the festival, co-founded by APE and Superfly, will bring tens of thousands of fans to see more than 140 artists playing on locally themed stages, with access to more than 100 local restaurants, over 30 regional wineries, two dozen breweries, local cannabis distributors, and, new for this year, a dozen vintage sellers for on-site shopping.

But the festival’s progression has not come without challenges, as rising costs weigh particularly heavily in its home market. California boasts the US’s second-highest cost-of-living rate, with both organisers’ and fans’ wallets taking a hit in recent years.

“Since 2021, our costs have gone up 75% – it is staggering how much it costs to produce the festival now,” he explains. “Festivals in general around the country, everyone’s experiencing inflated costs that we’re seeing through a bunch of different industries, but California itself and Golden Gate Park are super expensive. I don’t think people realise how much it costs to produce this. I mean, it’s mid-eight figures just to produce it.”

> “Cannabis culture up in Northern California has always been big”

Conscious of the cost increases, Scott says keeping it affordable and high-value for fans is at the top of the priority list. This year, three-day GA passes started around $539 (€468), with the GA+ tier climbing to $799 (€693).

“We try our best to keep the ticket prices as low as we can, certainly at the GA level. One way we’ve really offset that is with VIP offerings. We added GA+ a couple years ago, and what that allows us to do is help keep the GA price down to make it as accessible as it can because it’s an expensive ticket,” Scott says.

Even as costs have ballooned, organisers are focused on staying true to the mission of delivering world-class entertainment to their home market, with all but one of its stages named after local landmarks instead of branded activations. This ethos continues through the event’s food & beverage and recreational choices, with organisers adding a vintage shopping area this year.

“Vintage and thrifting have become really big in the Bay Area. We’ve partnered with 12 local vintage shops to be out there and sell their gear,” he says.

Outside Lands has also carved out a niche with Grass Lands, its dedicated cannabis area, making it the first – and perhaps only – major festival in the world to offer legal sales and on-site consumption.

“That’s been a home run. Cannabis culture up in Northern California has always been big,” he says. But delivering the experience, however, has come with significant logistical challenges.

> “Our whole business plan has shifted, going from a profit centre to a cost to us”

“It’s a heavily regulated industry, and there were a lot of hoops that we had to jump through to get the permit. You can’t serve any alcohol in that area, and the rest of the audience can’t see into the place where you have consumption and sales,” he says, pointing out an area in the site’s natural formation that works in their favour. “It’s a really magical spot, but it is still difficult to put on.”

Scott adds that the economics have shifted dramatically since Grass Lands launched.

“Early on, the sponsors had a lot of money to spend. It was a little like the Wild West, with the money that was flowing. As the years have gone on, that has really dried up. So our whole business plan has shifted, going from a profit centre to a cost to us, but we see it as a festival element. It’s not about making money; it’s part of the attraction to coming to the festival and having something different,” he says.

Like many event organisers today, Scott cites rising costs as the biggest challenge for the APE team. But another notable hurdle for the independent powerhouse is operating in an increasingly consolidated live business.

“As the concert industry has become more consolidated, we’ve stuck with and stayed focused on California,” he says. “We’re the largest independently run festival in the country, and that independence matters.”

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/smush-webp/2026/08/Outside-Lands-©-Alive-Coverage-430x269.png.webp)

*© Alive Coverage*

He says navigating global touring deals and securing exclusive bookings has become an increasingly nuanced path to walk.

“It’s hard when there are single promoters with different festivals. It’s very difficult to make a unique lineup, something you don’t see anywhere else,” he says. “Our number one is to try to book for the Bay Area, and the Bay Area has an eclectic taste. We have a wide brush to paint with because there are so many artists that you know sell tons of tickets in the Bay Area.”

That eccentricity is reflected in this year’s lineup, which blends rock, pop, electronic, alternative, and rap, with artists including Turnstile, Baby Keem, Djo, Dijon, Empire of the Sun, PinkPantheress, Glorilla, Ethel Cain, and Disco Lines also on board.

“We try to make it as unique as we can, particularly at the headliners, and that’s so difficult. For this year, we went really hard after Charli xcx. It was really important to have an artist like that, one that had not really played the market very much, so there was a hunger for her. But she has an edge and a coolness, and certainly a pop culture relevance that is hard to get,” Scott says.

That singularity also shows with its pull of rock pioneers The Strokes and electronic mega act Rufus du Sol, reflecting the breadth that organisers are trying to achieve.

They’ve recently expanded their electronic programming and stage in response to the “on fire” scene, Scott says, along with their LGBTQ-oriented Dolores stage.

> “It’s so much more than a music festival”

“We partner with three different queer party promoters in San Francisco, and they each get a day to curate it. We’ll have a lot of local artists, drag DJs, performance artists, but then we’ll also have like Romy from the xx doing a DJ set, or Trixie Mattel will pop over – that’s been really popular the last few years,” he says.

In years past, the party has continued outside of the Outside Lands weekend, with a municipal-approved [standalone concert series](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2024/04/historic-golden-gate-concert-series-confirmed/) bringing [Dead & Company](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2025/08/legendary-golden-gate-shows-reviving-san-francisco/), Zach Bryan, and System of a Down & Deftones into the market in recent years. Though it has been a running success, the team said they are taking a year off this year.

“We really didn’t find the appropriate artist or artists to do it. But we just renewed with the park and the city, so nine more years of doing the concerts before and after. We’ll have three concerts next year in one of the weekends surrounding Outside Lands. They’re fun for us, because it’s one stage with four-five artists, and is very genre-focused, so it’s a lot easier to book but just as fun.”

As the festival prepares to open its gates to another sold-out crowd, Scott says the ambition remains unchanged despite mounting pressures.

“It’s so much more than a music festival, and it’s not just a concert focused on one stage. There are millions of little moments throughout the festival, and we want to help create and enhance those experiences,” Scott concludes.
