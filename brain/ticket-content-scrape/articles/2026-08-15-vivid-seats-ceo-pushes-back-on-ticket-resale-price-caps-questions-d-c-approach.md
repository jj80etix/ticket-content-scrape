# Vivid Seats CEO Pushes Back on Ticket Resale Price Caps, Questions D.C. Approach

## Summary

On Vivid Seats' Q2 earnings call, CEO Lawrence Fey pushed back on the spreading wave of state/local resale price-cap laws, arguing they selectively target secondary-market concert tickets while leaving primary-market pricing and sports tickets untouched. He pointed to D.C.'s newly passed RESALE Act, Vermont's Act 109 and Maine's 2025 package as the active regulatory front, while California's comparable AB 1720 was shelved this week after lobbying pressure. Fey framed the caps as a scarcity-allocation problem: restrict legitimate resale and buyers/sellers will move to "shadow markets" instead.

### Highlights
- D.C.'s RESALE Act (unanimous Council passage, effective Jan. 1, 2027): caps concert/theater resale at 110% of face value (10% markup + up to 10% fees, ~120% consumer max), bans speculative sales, requires all-in pricing, bars certain surveillance pricing, and requires resellers moving 50+ tickets/year to register with a $25,000 surety bond. Sports, movies, and primary-market pricing are excluded.
- Vermont's Act 109 (signed May 26, effective July 1, 2026): caps resale at 10% above original price, plus speculative-sale and deceptive-ticketing restrictions.
- Maine's 2025 package: all-in pricing, speculative-ticket ban, bot restrictions, resale-marketplace fee limits.
- California's AB 1720 (10% resale cap) was shelved this week in the Senate — the same bill covered in TicketNews' companion piece today — after StubHub-led lobbying opposition.
- Fey: "If you take D.C. specifically, they excluded primary, they excluded sports" — calling the asymmetry (secondary capped, primary uncapped) the core flaw; warns caps push demand into unregulated "shadow markets."

### Industry Problem
State-by-state resale price-cap legislation is expanding unevenly and asymmetrically — it regulates secondary-market markups while leaving primary-market pricing and inventory practices untouched — creating a fragmented compliance landscape for any platform touching ticket resale, and a recurring "all-in pricing" mandate that increasingly shows up regardless of jurisdiction.

### Proposed Solution
Vivid Seats' own posture is advocacy/monitoring, not product change — it's contesting the caps rather than building around them. For Etix as a primary platform, the actionable signal isn't the resale cap itself (out of scope) but the recurring all-in-pricing requirement threading through every state's bill (D.C., Vermont, Maine); getting ahead of that now — transparent, fees-included pricing by default — reduces future compliance risk regardless of how the resale-cap patchwork resolves. Track D.C.'s Jan. 2027 effective date and Massachusetts' expected proposal (per yesterday's harvest) as the next milestones.

## Transcript

![](https://www.ticketnews.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/the-anthem.webp)

Vivid Seats CEO Lawrence Fey pushed back against the growing legislative effort to cap ticket resale prices [during the company’s second-quarter earnings call](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/08/vivid-seats-raises-2026-outlook-after-fifa-world-cup-demand-delivers-strong-q2/), questioning why lawmakers would restrict prices in the secondary concert market while leaving sports tickets and primary-market pricing largely untouched.

Fey addressed the issue after Benchmark analyst Daniel Kurnos asked about increased regulatory activity in Washington, D.C. and the possibility of similar restrictions spreading state by state. The Vivid Seats executive cited recent action in Maine and Vermont alongside the D.C. proposal, but said the company does not currently expect the changes to have a meaningful near-term financial impact because of the relatively small jurisdictions involved and delayed implementation of some measures.

The more significant question, Fey suggested, is whether those regulatory frameworks make sense in a market where demand for certain events routinely exceeds the number of available tickets.

“If you take D.C. specifically, they excluded primary, they excluded sports,” Fey said, calling that an “interesting starting point.”

## D.C. Targets Concert Resale, Not Primary Ticket Prices

Fey’s criticism comes after the D.C. Council gave unanimous final approval in July to the Restricting Egregious Scalping Against Live Entertainment, or RESALE, Act.

The measure would cap secondary ticket prices for concerts, theater and other covered live entertainment at 10% above the original ticket price, while allowing fees of up to another 10%. According to the Council, the maximum final consumer price would therefore be 120% of face value. Sporting events and movies are expressly excluded.

Primary-market ticket prices are also outside the resale cap. An earlier version would have permitted the mayor to regulate certain fees charged by primary ticketing platforms, but that authority was stripped from the final measure before passage. The Council says the legislation is intended to take effect January 1, 2027.

Beyond its price restrictions, the RESALE Act bans speculative ticket sales, requires all-in pricing and prohibits certain forms of personalized “surveillance pricing.” Professional resellers moving more than 50 tickets annually would also have to register with the District and maintain a $25,000 surety bond.

Supporters have presented those restrictions as consumer protections against excessive resale markups and deceptive ticketing practices. The D.C. Council described the legislation as an attempt to address perceived excesses in the secondary market, with independent venue advocates arguing that large markups can undermine prices intentionally established by artists and venues.

The selective nature of the price controls, however, has been a recurring point of contention.

As TicketNews has previously examined, resale-cap proposals generally do nothing to restrict the price an artist, promoter or primary ticketing platform may initially charge for a ticket, including tickets whose prices are increased in response to demand. They also do not directly address primary-market inventory holdbacks that can reduce the number of tickets available during an onsale.

That creates the asymmetry Fey highlighted: a $100 concert ticket could potentially be prohibited from being resold for $150 under the D.C. framework even though there is no corresponding prohibition against that ticket being initially offered for $150 — or considerably more — by the primary market.

## Price Caps Become a National Ticketing Battleground

D.C. is only one front in an increasingly active fight over resale regulation.

Vermont enacted Act 109 this year, limiting covered ticket resale prices to no more than 10% above the original price and adding restrictions on speculative sales and deceptive ticketing practices. Gov. Phil Scott approved the measure May 26, and it took effect July 1.

Maine also adopted a package of ticketing restrictions in 2025 that included all-in pricing requirements, a speculative-ticket ban, bot restrictions and limits on charges imposed by resale marketplaces. Fey grouped both states with D.C. when discussing the emerging regulatory environment facing Vivid Seats.

The campaign has not been successful everywhere. [California lawmakers this week shelved AB 1720](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/08/california-shelves-ticket-resale-price-cap-after-months-of-lawmaker-skepticism/), a closely watched proposal that would have imposed a similar 10% resale cap. The measure had drawn opposition from resale marketplaces and critics who questioned imposing controls exclusively on secondary-market prices while leaving primary-market pricing practices unrestricted.

Resale price restrictions have simultaneously become a priority for Live Nation and allies in the Fix the Tix coalition. Live Nation previously urged federal regulators to adopt a 20% cap above face value for concert ticket resale, while Fix the Tix has promoted model policies limiting or prohibiting resale markups. Those proposals generally focus their price controls on secondary rather than primary transactions.

For Vivid Seats, the issue goes beyond a philosophical disagreement over how tickets should be distributed. The company identifies restrictions on consumers’ ability to resell tickets and unfavorable legislative developments among the risks facing its marketplace business.

Fey’s comments put one of the country’s major ticket resale marketplaces more directly into that policy debate. His position is effectively that lawmakers cannot regulate away scarcity: for the events where demand dramatically exceeds supply, tickets will still ultimately be allocated somehow.

The emerging fight is over whether regulated resale marketplaces and market-driven prices should play that role — or whether governments should increasingly determine how much a ticket holder is permitted to receive when demand rises above the price originally established at the box office.
