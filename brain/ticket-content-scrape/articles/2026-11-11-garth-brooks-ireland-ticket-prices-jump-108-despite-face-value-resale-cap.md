# Garth Brooks Ireland Ticket Prices Jump 108% Despite Face-Value Resale Cap

## Summary

Standing tickets for Garth Brooks' 2027 Ireland concerts will cost 108% more than his 2022 Croke Park shows, even though Ireland's Sale of Tickets Act 2021 bans resale above face value for designated events. The piece uses the case to show that resale price caps do nothing to constrain primary-market prices, and ties the pricing power back to Ticketmaster's historically dominant (90%+) share of Irish primary ticketing, as documented in a 2021 CCPC investigation that ended in commitments rather than a legal finding against the company.

### Key Points
- Standing/pit tickets rise from €65.56 (2022) to €136.25 (2027), a ~108% jump; seated tickets up ~93% (€81 → €156.25), before Ticketmaster service charges
- Promoter confirms no dynamic pricing, no VIP tiers and no presale for the 2027 shows — yet face values are still far higher than 2022
- Ireland's 2021 resale law caps secondary-market resale at face value but leaves primary-market pricing entirely unregulated
- Ireland's CCPC previously found Ticketmaster controlled 90%+ of outsourced primary ticketing (2012–2018) and that exclusive contracts foreclosed 70–90% of the market from competition; case resolved via binding commitments, not an infringement finding
- Echoes a prior pattern seen with Oasis tickets, where high Ticketmaster premium-priced tickets carried elevated prices into the face-value resale marketplace
- Northern Ireland shows (Belfast) aren't covered by the Republic's resale law

### Highlights
- 108% primary-price increase despite a face-value resale cap directly undercuts the "resale caps protect affordability" argument
- Ticketmaster's historical 90%+ primary-market dominance in Ireland is cited as context for why high face-value pricing persists unchecked
- No dynamic pricing or VIP tiers were used here — the price increase is purely a face-value decision by promoter/artist, not an algorithmic pricing tactic

### Industry Problem
Resale/transferability regulation (price caps) is a popular legislative fix for ticket affordability, but it structurally cannot touch primary-market pricing power — especially where one platform has outsized primary-market share, leaving patrons exposed to steep face-value increases regardless of resale rules.

### Proposed Solution
This is a regulatory/market-structure story to monitor, not build against directly: it strengthens the case (AI-context) for positioning independent primary platforms as an alternative to concentrated primary-ticketing markets, and signals that future resale-cap legislation (as being debated elsewhere, e.g. Massachusetts, Texas) will likely face the same "doesn't touch face value" critique.

Standing tickets for Garth Brooks’ 2027 concerts in Ireland will cost more than twice as much as they did during his last Irish performances in 2022, a substantial increase in a country where legislation prohibits ticket resale above face value for designated events.

Fans attending Brooks’ eight concerts in Galway, Kilkenny, Limerick and Cork will pay €136.25 for general standing or pit tickets, compared with €65.56 for his five Croke Park performances in 2022. The €70.69 increase represents a jump of nearly 108%, while seated tickets have climbed from €81 to €156.25, an increase of approximately 93%.

The prices, [highlighted in a report by TheJournal.ie](https://www.thejournal.ie/garth-brooks-ticket-prices-ireland-shows-7220186-Nov2026/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), do not include Ticketmaster service charges, which will increase the final amount consumers pay.

The comparison comes with an important qualification. Prices for Brooks’ 2022 Croke Park concerts were held at the levels originally established for his planned 2014 performances, which were canceled following a dispute over event licensing. That means the 2027 prices represent an increase over rates first established 13 years earlier, rather than a conventional five-year price adjustment.

Speaking in an interview with RTÉ Radio 1, Aiken said there would be no presales or VIP tickets and that fans standing in the pit closest to the stage would pay the same amount as those elsewhere on the pitch.

## Ireland's Resale Cap Does Not Restrict Primary Ticket Prices

Ireland’s [Sale of Tickets Act 2021](https://www.irishstatutebook.ie/eli/2021/act/21/enacted/en/html?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) prohibits the resale of tickets above their original sale price for events at designated venues or events specifically designated under the legislation.

Brooks’ concerts fall directly within those restrictions. The Irish government’s [official designation register](https://enterprise.gov.ie/en/publications/register-for-designation-of-venues-and-events.html?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) lists all four two-night engagements in the Republic of Ireland, with designations effective November 6, 2026.

Consequently, consumers purchasing tickets for those eight performances will face substantially higher initial prices than in 2022, but anyone subsequently reselling those tickets will be prohibited from charging more than the original sale price.

The distinction is central to the debate over whether resale price caps meaningfully improve ticket affordability.

Although such restrictions are frequently presented as consumer protections against excessive resale markups, they do not regulate the initial prices established by event organizers. In practice, the restrictions limit what ticket holders can recover when reselling their purchases while leaving primary-market pricing decisions outside the scope of the cap.

Brooks’ upcoming concerts do not establish that resale restrictions caused ticket prices to increase. They do, however, offer a clear example of a market where resale markups have been outlawed while consumers continue to face substantial increases in the prices established at the initial onsale.

The distinction becomes particularly consequential when the primary ticketing marketplace is itself highly concentrated.

## Ticketmaster's Dominance Adds to Competition Questions

Ireland’s Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC) previously investigated Ticketmaster’s dominance in the country’s primary ticketing industry, raising significant concerns about long-term exclusive agreements that restricted competition.

In its [2021 investigation report](https://assets.ccpc.ie/data/docs/default-source/enforcement-and-regulation/competition-law/competition-cases/ccpc-ticketing-investigation-report.pdf?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), the regulator reported that Ticketmaster controlled more than 90% of the island of Ireland’s outsourced primary ticketing market between 2012 and 2018, measured by either revenue or gross ticket value.

The CCPC also estimated that long-term exclusive ticketing agreements had foreclosed between 70% and 90% of that market from effective competition during the period examined.

Ticketmaster disputed the regulator’s preliminary findings and denied violating competition law. The investigation concluded after the company agreed to legally binding commitments addressing contract exclusivity and duration, rather than a court finding that Ticketmaster had infringed competition law. The historical market-share figures are not a measurement of Ticketmaster’s current market share.

Those findings concerned competition in primary ticketing services, not the intent behind Ireland’s resale legislation. Nevertheless, they provide important context for the broader consequences of restrictions that apply to secondary-market transactions while leaving the structure and pricing of the primary market largely untouched.

For consumers, the distinction is straightforward: A law prohibiting resale above face value does not prevent the original ticket seller from offering tickets at sharply higher prices, nor does it necessarily introduce additional competition into the initial sales process.

The issue has surfaced previously in Ireland. [TicketNews reported last year](https://www.ticketnews.com/2025/08/irish-regulator-probe-of-oasis-ticket-fiasto-ongoing-ticketmaster-resale-double-dips/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) that high-priced Oasis tickets originally sold through Ticketmaster’s premium pricing system were subsequently offered through its face-value resale marketplace at similarly elevated prices. Because those prices reflected the original purchase amounts, the listings illustrated how high primary-market prices can carry directly into a legally capped resale environment.

Brooks’ tour presents a different pricing structure, without dynamic pricing or premium ticket tiers, but raises a related affordability question.

## Garth Brooks 2027 Ireland Ticket Information

As [TicketNews previously reported](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/11/garth-brooks-irish-tour-2027-dates-tickets/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), Brooks will perform 10 concerts across five cities in May and June 2027, beginning with two shows in Belfast before visiting Galway, Kilkenny, Limerick and Cork.

The Belfast performances, scheduled for May 21 and 22 at Boucher Road Playing Fields, will cost £115 per ticket before service charges. Those concerts take place in Northern Ireland and are not subject to the Republic of Ireland’s 2021 resale legislation.

Tickets for all 10 performances go on sale Thursday, November 12, at 8 a.m. Irish time through [Ticketmaster Ireland](https://www.ticketmaster.ie/garth-brooks-tickets/artist/732705?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content). There will be no advance presale, and Ticketmaster lists a purchase limit of nine tickets per customer.
