# Viagogo Loses New Zealand Appeal as Court Expands Consumer Protection Ruling

## Summary

New Zealand's Court of Appeal has dismissed Viagogo's appeal of a 2024 High Court ruling that found the resale marketplace breached the Fair Trading Act through misleading ticket-guarantee, pricing, and marketplace-status representations. The court also expanded the earlier ruling, finding that Viagogo's contract clause requiring consumer disputes to be governed by Swiss law (not just heard in Swiss courts) is an unfair contract term. Viagogo must pay the Commerce Commission's appeal costs and has 28 days to propose wording for a revised declaration.

### Key Points
- Court of Appeal upheld findings that Viagogo's ticket-guarantee and pricing disclosures were misleading; a refund is not equivalent to a valid ticket.
- Ruling rejected Viagogo's argument that late fee disclosure and "secondary marketplace" labeling were adequate.
- Expanded the unfair-contract-term finding to cover both the Swiss-courts clause and the Swiss-law choice-of-law clause — relevant precedent for any international ticketing platform's terms of service.
- Viagogo's current site/practices differ from those at issue (changes made ~2020), but the litigation and cost order still apply.
- Parties have 28 days to submit proposed wording for a revised declaration; litigation dates back to a 2017 investigation.

### Highlights
- New Zealand Court of Appeal decision issued Nov. 23, 2026, publicly released Nov. 24.
- Court found both the "Swiss courts" and "Swiss law" clauses in Viagogo's consumer terms to be unfair contract terms under NZ's Fair Trading Act.
- Commerce Commission's underlying case dates to 2017 investigation / 2018 proceedings; High Court ruled against Viagogo in 2024.
- Viagogo ordered to pay the Commission's appeal costs; 28 days given for revised declaration wording.

### Industry Problem
International ticketing/resale platforms often rely on foreign-law/foreign-forum clauses in consumer contracts, which regulators and courts increasingly view as a device that obscures local consumer-protection rights — a compliance exposure for any platform operating across jurisdictions with varying consumer-protection regimes.

### Proposed Solution
The ruling doesn't bar choice-of-law clauses outright, but requires clear, transparent communication of the local rights and recourse that remain available to consumers. A primary platform like Etix should audit its own terms of service and guarantee language against this standard — ensuring ticket-guarantee and fee-disclosure language can't be read as implying delivery assurance it doesn't provide, and that any choice-of-law clause doesn't obscure local consumer rights.

New Zealand’s Court of Appeal has dismissed an appeal by global ticket resale marketplace Viagogo, upholding a 2024 ruling that the company breached the country’s consumer protection laws while expanding an earlier finding concerning unfair terms in its consumer contracts.

The [66-page judgment](https://www.courtsofnz.govt.nz/assets/cases/2026/2026-NZCA-576.pdf?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), issued Monday and publicly released Tuesday, also sided with New Zealand’s Commerce Commission in its cross-appeal challenging a provision in Viagogo’s terms and conditions requiring consumer disputes to be governed by Swiss law.

The decision marks the latest development in a legal battle stretching back nearly a decade, though Viagogo had already implemented several changes to its website and business practices years before the original High Court ruling.

“The Commission will pursue international companies when they mislead New Zealanders. If you are doing business here, you must comply with the Fair Trading Act,” Rebecca McAtamney, the Commerce Commission’s general manager of competition and fair trading, said in a [statement announcing the decision](https://www.comcom.govt.nz/news-and-media/news-and-events/2026/a-win-for-kiwi-consumers-court-of-appeal-dismisses-viagogo-appeal/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content).

## Court Upholds Findings on Ticket Guarantees, Pricing

The Commerce Commission began investigating Viagogo in 2017 and initiated legal proceedings in August 2018. In [its 2024 ruling](https://www.ticketnews.com/2024/05/new-zealand-court-finds-viagogo-breached-consumer-protection-rules/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), the High Court found that Viagogo had violated New Zealand’s Fair Trading Act in several areas, including its representations concerning ticket availability, pricing, guarantees and its status as a ticket reseller rather than an official ticketing agent.

Viagogo challenged the lower court’s findings concerning the adequacy of its resale marketplace disclosures, ticket guarantees and pricing representations, as well as aspects of the evidence relied upon in reaching those conclusions. The company did not challenge the findings concerning its ticket scarcity representations or advertising as an official ticket seller.

Among the issues examined by the Court of Appeal was the distinction between guaranteeing a valid ticket and providing a financial remedy when a ticket buyer encounters a problem.

Viagogo argued that consumers would understand that a secondary ticketing marketplace could not absolutely guarantee delivery of valid tickets and that its guarantee instead provided a remedy in the event of a failed transaction.

The court rejected that argument, finding that a refund was not equivalent to possessing a valid ticket to an event. It also emphasized that consumers frequently incur expenses beyond the ticket purchase itself, such as travel and accommodations.

“The statements made by Viagogo were misleading on their face,” the court found.

The appellate court also rejected Viagogo’s challenges to the lower court’s reasoning concerning the disclosure of additional ticket fees late in the purchasing process and the identification of its website as a secondary marketplace.

The practices at issue, however, should be distinguished from Viagogo’s current operations. In March 2020, the Commerce Commission [announced that it would no longer pursue an interim injunction](https://www.comcom.govt.nz/news-and-media/news-and-events/2020/viagogo-confirms-website-changes-and-accepts-jurisdiction-of-nz-courts/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) against Viagogo after the company made changes to its website and provided undertakings to the court that it would not reverse them.

At the time, the regulator acknowledged that the changes largely addressed its immediate concerns regarding ticket prices, availability and guarantees. Viagogo also withdrew its challenge to the jurisdiction of New Zealand’s courts in the Commission’s proceedings.

Those changes did not resolve the underlying litigation, which continued through the High Court’s 2024 decision and an appellate hearing in October 2025.

## Ruling Expands Findings on International Consumer Contracts

Beyond upholding the earlier findings, the Court of Appeal expanded the ruling concerning Viagogo’s contractual provisions governing consumer disputes, an issue with potential relevance to other international ticketing businesses.

The provision at issue required disputes to be resolved in the courts of Geneva under Swiss law, while preserving Viagogo’s ability to bring legal proceedings against consumers in their home jurisdictions.

The High Court previously determined that requiring New Zealand consumers to pursue disputes in Switzerland constituted an unfair contract term but stopped short of reaching the same conclusion about the requirement that Swiss law govern those disputes.

The Court of Appeal has now found both elements of the provision unfair under the Fair Trading Act.

During the appeal, Viagogo accepted that the contractual language did not exclude consumers’ statutory rights under New Zealand law. The court nevertheless determined that the provision failed to adequately communicate those rights to consumers, potentially discouraging them from pursuing claims through New Zealand’s own legal system.

The court acknowledged that international businesses have legitimate interests in establishing which laws and courts govern contractual disputes. Its decision focused on whether those interests could be protected through less restrictive and more transparent contract language.

For international ticketing platforms, the distinction is significant. The ruling does not categorically prohibit companies from designating a governing law in their contracts, but it underscores the importance of clearly communicating the local consumer protections and avenues for legal recourse that remain available to customers.

The court ordered Viagogo to pay the Commerce Commission’s appeal costs and gave the parties 28 days to submit proposed wording for a revised declaration addressing the unfair contract provision.

TicketNews has contacted Viagogo for comment on the ruling, whether the company intends to pursue a further appeal, and whether the decision will require additional changes to its website, ticket guarantee or consumer contract terms in New Zealand or other markets.
