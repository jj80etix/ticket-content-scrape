# Raskin Introduces Antitrust Reform Bill Citing Live Nation-Trump Settlement Controversy

## Summary

Rep. Jamie Raskin introduced the Antitrust Accountability and Transparency Act, the House companion to Sen. Amy Klobuchar's March bill, seeking expanded disclosure requirements and judicial oversight of federal antitrust settlements. The bill directly cites Live Nation's October 21 court filing acknowledging President Trump's attendance at a March 5 White House meeting where the DOJ-Live Nation antitrust settlement (which let Live Nation keep Ticketmaster) was finalized, and references TicketNews' reporting that Live Nation omitted Trump's attendance from its original June disclosure.

### Key Points
- Raskin (D-MD, House Judiciary ranking member) announced the bill October 29; it is the House companion to Klobuchar's Senate bill from March
- Live Nation's October 21 filing confirmed CEO Michael Rapino met Trump at a March 5 White House meeting finalizing DOJ settlement terms
- Live Nation had not disclosed Trump's attendance in its original June court filing
- Bill proposes expanded disclosure rules, greater judicial authority, and new protections against politically influenced antitrust settlements

### Highlights
- Settlement allowed Live Nation to retain ownership of Ticketmaster despite DOJ's antitrust suit
- Newly disclosed presidential involvement is now central to the legislative push
- Companion Senate bill from Klobuchar predates the October disclosure (introduced March 2026)

### Industry Problem
The DOJ's abrupt settlement with Live Nation/Ticketmaster, now shadowed by allegations of undisclosed political influence, undercuts confidence that antitrust enforcement against the dominant primary-ticketing incumbent will meaningfully change market structure — a live question for how much competitive room independents like Etix have.

### Proposed Solution
The bill itself is a legislative check (disclosure/oversight), not a product fix; Etix's posture should stay monitor-only, watching whether increased settlement scrutiny revives tougher DOJ/FTC terms (e.g., structural remedies) that could reopen competitive space, and tracking the bill's progress alongside the FTC's separate suit.

Jamie Raskin has introduced legislation aimed at strengthening judicial oversight of federal antitrust settlements, citing the controversial agreement between the Justice Department and Live Nation-Ticketmaster and newly disclosed details about President Donald Trump’s involvement in the negotiations.

The Maryland Democrat, who serves as ranking member of the House Judiciary Committee, announced the Antitrust Accountability and Transparency Act on Thursday, October 29, proposing expanded disclosure requirements, greater authority for federal judges and new protections against politically influenced settlements.

The announcement follows Live Nation’s October 21 court filing acknowledging that Trump briefly appeared at a March 5 White House meeting where company executives and senior administration officials finalized the terms of a settlement that allowed Live Nation to retain ownership of Ticketmaster.

The House Judiciary Committee Democrats’ [announcement](https://democrats-judiciary.house.gov/media-center/press-releases/after-live-nation-reveals-trump-role-in-doj-settlement-ranking-member-raskin-introduces-bill-to-crack-down-on-corruption-in-doj-and-ftc-merger-approvals?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) specifically cites [TicketNews’ reporting on the amended disclosure](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/10/live-nation-trump-settlement-disclosure-amended/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), which revealed that the company had not identified Trump’s attendance in its original June filing.

## Bill Would Expand Disclosure of Political Contacts and Settlement Negotiations

The legislation proposes substantial changes to the Tunney Act, the federal law enacted in 1974 to ensure that antitrust settlements negotiated by the Justice Department receive independent judicial review before taking effect.

Under the [proposed legislation](https://democrats-judiciary.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/democrats-judiciary.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/antitrust-accountability-and-transparency-act.pdf?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), parties would face expanded requirements to disclose communications with government officials concerning proposed settlements, explicitly including contacts involving the Executive Office of the President.

The measure would also authorize judges to demand information about benefits or concessions offered to government officials that may have a reasonable connection to a settlement decision. Those inquiries could encompass payments, political donations or changes in corporate policies and business practices.

That provision has particular relevance to the Live Nation controversy.

The company contributed $500,000 to Trump’s second inaugural committee, retained several politically connected advisers and appointed longtime Trump associate Richard Grenell to its board of directors while facing the federal antitrust lawsuit.

The company’s amended disclosure also confirms that Trump personally contacted Live Nation CEO Michael Rapino in February to discuss the Kennedy Center and asked about the status of the antitrust case.

Live Nation maintains that no substantive settlement terms were discussed during that conversation and that Trump did not participate in substantive negotiations when he briefly appeared at the March 5 meeting.

Neither the amended filing nor the political relationships documented around the settlement establish that improper influence occurred. However, Raskin and other lawmakers have argued that the circumstances warrant substantially greater scrutiny than the existing review process has produced.

The bill would also require disclosure of earlier settlement proposals, the process used to evaluate them and commitments made outside the formal settlement agreement.

Those requirements could address one of the central unresolved questions surrounding the Live Nation agreement: why the Justice Department abandoned its original demand for Ticketmaster’s separation from Live Nation in favor of behavioral restrictions that would leave the companies under common ownership.

## Greater Power for Judges and State Attorneys General

Beyond transparency, the proposal would change the standards federal courts apply when evaluating antitrust settlements.

Judges would be directed to consider whether proposed remedies adequately address the violations alleged by the government and whether the agreement permits conduct creating a material risk of future antitrust violations.

The legislation would also make clear that courts need not defer to the government’s predictions about whether its proposed remedies will work.

Another provision would extend Tunney Act-style judicial review to Federal Trade Commission settlements, which are not currently subject to the same statutory process.

For proposed merger settlements, the bill would require companies to keep relevant assets separate during an initial review period, with courts authorized to extend that restriction under specified circumstances. The goal is to prevent transactions from becoming effectively irreversible before judicial scrutiny is complete.

Perhaps most consequentially for cases resembling the Live Nation litigation, the legislation would expand the role of state attorneys general.

When federal regulators propose voluntarily dismissing an antitrust case, state attorneys general would have an opportunity to seek substitution and continue the litigation rather than allowing the federal government’s withdrawal to end the proceeding.

That provision reflects the unusual circumstances of the Live Nation case, in which a bipartisan coalition of state attorneys general continued pursuing antitrust claims after DOJ reached its settlement.

On April 15, a federal jury found Live Nation and Ticketmaster liable on the antitrust claims pursued by the remaining states. Those states continue seeking remedies that could include separating the two companies, while Live Nation has challenged the verdict and aspects of the damages findings.

The proposed federal settlement, meanwhile, remains subject to review by U.S. District Judge Arun Subramanian, who must determine whether its terms satisfy the Tunney Act’s public-interest requirements.

## Former Antitrust Officials Back Reforms

The legislation has attracted support from former federal antitrust officials who served under administrations of both parties, including former DOJ Antitrust Division leaders Jonathan Kanter, William Baer and Roger Alford, as well as former FTC Chairman William Kovacic.

Alford, a former senior Trump administration antitrust official who has publicly criticized political interference in federal enforcement decisions, endorsed the proposal when Klobuchar introduced the Senate version in March.

“These proposals are welcome additions to strengthen judicial review of merger settlements and ensure voluntary dismissals are not used to circumvent review,” Alford said at the time.

Alford has also been an outspoken critic of the handling of the Live Nation settlement, previously warning congressional lawmakers that politically connected lobbyists had gained inappropriate influence over federal antitrust decisions.

The House legislation follows [additional criticism from Rep. Jerrold Nadler](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/10/nadler-live-nation-settlement-trump-politicized/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), who told TicketNews this week that the disclosure of Trump’s presence at the settlement meeting deepened his concerns about political interference.

“DOJ’s belated admission that President Trump was in the room when DOJ negotiated its proposed settlement with Live Nation Ticketmaster only further deepens my concerns that the deal was grotesquely politicized,” Nadler said.

Nadler is among the original House cosponsors of Raskin’s legislation, alongside Reps. Hank Johnson, Pramila Jayapal, Deborah Ross, Becca Balint, Jesús “Chuy” García, Sydney Kamlager-Dove, Dan Goldman and Summer Lee.

The legislation would not automatically reopen or invalidate the Live Nation settlement. Its immediate significance is that the controversy surrounding the ticketing giant has become a central example in a congressional effort to change how federal antitrust agreements are negotiated, disclosed and reviewed.

For now, Subramanian’s pending review will proceed under existing law, with the court still tasked with determining whether the government’s proposed agreement adequately protects competition despite the jury’s separate findings against Live Nation and Ticketmaster.
