# History Ottawa Brings New 2,000-Cap. Venue To Canadian Capital (Q’s With GM Bronwyn Campbell)

## Summary

Live Nation opened History Ottawa, a 2,000-capacity venue in Ottawa's new Arts, Culture and Entertainment District, on Aug. 20 with a Wallflowers show. GM Bronwyn Campbell discusses the venue's design (a second "History" venue after Toronto, with input from Drake), community relations with a neighboring residence, and plans to scale toward 200 shows/year.

### Key Points

- Built in a former Chapters bookstore in Ottawa's ByWard Market, filling a gap for touring artists between Toronto and Montreal stops.
- Multiple shows sold out before the venue even opened, including Marianas Trench, Pretty Reckless, Kaleo, and Modest Mouse.
- Venue worked proactively with an adjacent residential building on sound/load-in concerns ahead of opening.
- Target is ~50 shows in the first six months, scaling to 120-140 next year and eventually ~200/year, similar to History Toronto and the Commodore Ballroom.

## Transcript

![IMG 8266](https://static.pollstar.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/IMG_8266.jpeg)

Bronwyn Campbell at History Ottawa, which opened in the Canadian capital on Aug. 20 with a concert by The Wallflowers.

*Toronto might seem like the centre of the Canadian universe, but Ottawa is the country’s capital — and now many more artists have a 2,000-capacity venue to play, with the opportunity to squeeze in some touristy activities before soundcheck, like trying the original BeaverTails, the fried sugary treat, and visiting Parliament Hill.*

*History Ottawa, which opened Aug. 20 with The Wallflowers, is the second “History” for Live Nation built with design input from Canadian rapper Drake.*

*Ottawa has had some legendary venues —1,000-capacity Capital Music Hall, 250-capacity Zaphod Beeblebrox and 1,000-capacity Barrymore’s Music Hall — but all have sadly closed. The city is still home to the 900-seat Bronson Centre, the National Arts Centre’s Southam Hall theatre (cap. 2,065) and the 2,200-capacity Hard Rock Hotel & Casino Ottawa in Gloucester. A 20,500-capacity arena, Canadian Tire Centre, is in Kanata.*

*History Ottawa adds a needed two-level, 35,000 square foot room to the venue landscape.*

*Built in the former Chapters bookstore, History Ottawa is part of the city’s newly designated Arts, Culture and Entertainment District (ACE) in the neighborhood known as ByWard Market. The former property at 47–57 Rideau Street was acquired by the Crown corporation National Capital Commission (NCC), which leased the site to Live Nation Canada for the development and operation of the mid-sized entertainment venue.*

*The architectural design was led by Ottawa firm CSV Architects, with principal Darryl Hood and director Rick Kellner among the project leads. Toronto’s Solotech handled the AV integration, with an Adamson sound system and GLP German Light Products lighting. The venue’s technical director is Wayne Hawthorne.*

Bronwyn Campbell is the general manager; Rob Feller, senior operations manager; and Alex Primeau the booking manager.

*Campbell was previously GM of the historic Commodore Ballroom for 11 years. She spoke to* Pollstar *about the launch of History Ottawa, what it means to the city, the differences with History Toronto, as well as community feedback.*

**Pollstar: Ottawa is the capital but I’m sure many readers outside Canada don’t know much about the city and the proximity to Montreal. Can you tell us about it?**  
**Bronwyn Campbell:** I was new to Ottawa as well. Ottawa has a bit of a bad reputation of being a sleepy city and it needed some life to it, but my experience in the city has been the opposite. Ottawa is really about the people. The people are so welcoming and kind and warm and coming from Vancouver, it’s not always the case \[*laughs*\]. I’m from Vancouver, so I’m allowed to say that. It’s a really beautiful city. It’s a city that is full of excited music lovers.

That was the biggest wave that I got after moving there is how excited people were about this venue and they’re really thirsty for music. It’s about a million people in Ottawa, about a four-hour drive from Toronto and a two-hour drive from Montreal. So it definitely misses the touring stops. They kind of go from Toronto to Montreal, Montreal to Toronto. So, it’s really exciting for the city to have a venue of this size.

**Fans have to come to Toronto or to Montreal to catch certain artists. Ottawa has had some legendary rock venues not of that size. What are you learning about tour itineraries?**  
That’s it. It was the gap in the market that we didn’t have anything of this size, especially in the downtown market. And as far as artists go, that’s the exciting part is that we can now bring bigger touring artists to the city and that would probably skip and go from Toronto to Montreal or vice versa. But I think it’s a new market, so we’re learning that. It’s a new market for the artists as well. It’s about educating them on the fanbase that’s there because the shows that we’ve got on sale right now are doing really well and that speaks to the music lovers in the city. So, it’s about educating them on the fanbase and then educating them on the venue too. We’ve created this beautiful space for them. All the tour managers, all the agents, all the managers, they all talk so I think once it gets out there, it’s going to be a really easy sell.

**How does music fit into the government’s downtown revitalization plan?**  
ByWard Market, what I’ve been told, was a bustling entertainment district and then over the years, with a lot of downtown areas — I come from Granville Street on Vancouver — there’s a lot of sick people on the street that need help and that pushes the tourists away, so there is a big plan now to revitalize the market. They’ve created this entertainment district, which History falls into. It’s called the Arts, Culture and Entertainment District, the ACE District. I’ve sat in on a few meetings in those, and that’s a part of revitalizing the market. And that’s what the city is so excited about because it brings this new activity to the downtown core. As far as surrounding businesses, the hotels, they’re all so excited. They reach out to us all the time because it’s bringing this new life and tourism. People will hopefully come from out of town now to see shows here. And go to restaurants before they come to our shows, or then go have a drink after the show, or take their night further.

**Like History Toronto, were there nearby residents to consult?**  
There is one residence and they’re right next to us on 700 Sussex. I sat down with them, had a good meeting with them. They’ve created a committee at their apartment building, and they have an email set up internally because their concerns, of course, were about sound. The main important thing is letting them know we don’t want to come in here and disrupt anything. We want this to be a positive for the market, and maintaining open lines of communication with them is really important. And so, I’ve done that as well. I invited them to the first show. I walked some of them through the venue. I have one of their phone numbers. It’s a lovely group of people, and they want to make sure that we’re working with them to be good neighbours, and that’s something that is important to us. I care about that. I don’t want to upset people. They came to our first show, and we showed them all the money that we invested in soundproofing the room and then we did our sound test. We played our first show, and we all stood outside together, and they were like, “Oh, OK, this is going to be fine.” They were really happy with it. But again, I’ll maintain those lines of communication with them.

The City helped us work through things we foresaw being issues, because it is on a busy street for load-in. We worked really closely with them for months and got it to a good place where we’ll be able to load-in trucks and parking spots for our buses. We’ll have shore power \[external electricity\].

**Let’s talk about the venue. I saw that History Ottawa has multiple water stations and you don’t have VIP areas on the main floor. What are some things you took from History Toronto and were told were or weren’t necessary?**  
We’re a little bit smaller. We’re 2,000 capacity, 40,000 square feet spread out over two levels. Fully accessible venue, just like History \[Toronto\]. All of our premium seating is in the mezzanine. Like you mentioned, in History \[Toronto\], they have a small seated section downstairs, but for us, it all lives upstairs. They do have one water station, but we have four, so we saw that that was popular in History Toronto and made it bigger. And then our premium seating upstairs, too, something we have that’s different is this beautiful premium lounge, which can hold about 100 people. It’s got 58 seats in it. You can sit and have dinner there. It’s really cool, futuristic and modern and sleek. We can also shut the doors on that space, so it can be a completely separate area from the concert space, and the sound leak is minimal.

**Who led the project initially?**  
We had two Live Nation people internally working on it \[Lea Ghazal, director of architecture; and recently retired Paul Corcoran, then-SVP of venues\] and we have an architectural manager who came in as well, and she essentially was working with PCL, who were our contractors, and managing between all the contractors, and Live Nation side, the design side.

**What is Drake’s involvement?**  
All I can say is that I haven’t met him yet, and it’s his stage anytime he wants to come and jump on it. Obviously, we’d be extremely happy about that, but \[based on\] my later involvement he really did love the design element and was focused on that. I can just say that it’s been a really good partnership, and it’s something that they’re looking forward to hopefully expanding in the future.

**In Toronto, there’s LED lights for the artists as they come from the dressing room onto the stage as well as artwork by Drake’s pals. Do you have any of that?**  
We did it a little bit differently. There is still the neon hallway that goes down to the stage, and I know that is a really cool feature of History in Toronto. The artist dressing rooms are like nothing I’ve ever seen. I’ve been in venues for a long time, and when you go to artist dressing rooms, for some reason it’s always an afterthought, and this has been the first thought. It looks like a five-star hotel back there. But, instead of the artwork, what we did is we took pictures of artists that have played at History in Toronto and framed them and put them throughout all the artist spaces.

**How many shows do you plan to have a year?**  
The target is to get to about 50 shows in the first six months, and then we want to get that up to 120, 140 next year, and then eventually get it to where History Toronto and even the Commodore, around 200 shows a year. We’ve had a lot of shows that have sold out right away, which is cool, too, because they sold out before we were even open.

**Which ones?**  
Marianas Trench, Pretty Reckless, Kaleo, Modest Mouse, which is pretty remarkable that people were buying tickets before they’d even seen the venue or even know what it was at that stage. So, it’s only going to build, once people start to understand the experience that we’re giving them and how much we care about the fans’ experience and how we want to treat people. I think we’ve created a space that both artists and fans are going to look forward to coming back to.

**Do you anticipate having underplays the way that History Toronto had Ed Sheeran, Muse and Nickelback?**  
For sure. There’s been a lot of conversations around that. There’s a few in the pipeline right now that I’m really excited about that definitely are underplays. That’s why a venue of this size is so great because you get the up-and-coming artists that probably the next time you see them, they might be playing the arena, or you get the big artists that want the more intimate experience. So the underplays, there’s a few in the works right now.
