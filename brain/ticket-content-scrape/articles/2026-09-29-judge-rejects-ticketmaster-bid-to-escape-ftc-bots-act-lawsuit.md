# Judge Rejects Ticketmaster Bid to Escape FTC BOTS Act Lawsuit

## Summary

A federal judge denied Live Nation and Ticketmaster's motion to dismiss the FTC's BOTS Act and deceptive-pricing lawsuit, rejecting the argument that the BOTS Act shields platforms (rather than just brokers) from liability. The court found the FTC adequately alleged Ticketmaster knew brokers were using multi-account setups to evade posted ticket limits and profited from the resulting resale activity, and that "+Fees" pricing labels can still support a deceptive-pricing claim even though all-in pricing was shown before checkout. The case, filed September 2025, now proceeds to a scheduling conference rather than being dismissed at the pleading stage; the ruling doesn't decide whether the underlying allegations are true.

### Key Points
- Judge Maame Ewusi-Mensah Frimpong (C.D. Cal.) denied dismissal of all claims, including under the BOTS Act, FTC Act, and state consumer-protection laws (7 states joined).
- Court rejected Ticketmaster's argument that the BOTS Act only protects platforms from brokers, not the reverse — cited internal system "TicketCounter" as evidence platforms could detect circumvention.
- Court found Ticketmaster's role in voiding/reissuing resale tickets makes it a "seller" under the law, not a passive marketplace.
- Deceptive-pricing claims over historical "+Fees" display survive; court called the qualifier "vague and ambiguous" even though total price was shown pre-purchase.
- Ticket-purchase-limit misrepresentation claims and civil-penalty request also survive; three-year BOTS Act limitations period does not bar the case.
- Ticketmaster says it "continue[s] to believe this lawsuit is misguided and will ultimately fail"; case moves to scheduling conference.

### Highlights
- Ruling published Monday (2026-09-29); case originally filed by FTC + 7 states in September 2025.
- Alleged fee inflation from Ticketmaster's pre-all-in pricing reached as much as 44% of ticket cost.
- Court explicitly validated the FTC's "sells or offers to sell" theory against Ticketmaster's passive-marketplace defense.
- Litigation clears the pleading stage and heads toward discovery/scheduling — first substantive test of BOTS Act platform liability theory.

### Industry Problem
The case tests whether a primary ticketing platform can be held directly liable — not just brokers — when its own enforcement systems detect but don't stop bot-driven purchase-limit evasion and fee opacity, a structural compliance exposure for any platform running both primary sales and an affiliated resale marketplace.

### Proposed Solution
No product fix is proposed in the ruling itself, but it raises the bar for provable enforcement of ticket limits (Ticketmaster's own "TicketCounter" system now cited as evidence of capability-but-inaction) and for pricing transparency beyond a bare "+Fees" label — an independent platform should ensure all-in pricing is genuinely upfront and that limit-enforcement tooling is both real and demonstrably used, as a live BOTS Act/deceptive-pricing case will keep surfacing what regulators consider sufficient.

A federal judge has rejected Live Nation and Ticketmaster’s attempt to dismiss a sweeping Federal Trade Commission lawsuit accusing the companies of enabling high-volume brokers to evade ticket limits while profiting from the resulting resale activity.

In a ruling published Monday, U.S. District Judge Maame Ewusi-Mensah Frimpong denied the companies’ motion to dismiss every claim in the case, allowing the FTC and seven states to proceed with allegations under the Better Online Ticket Sales Act, the FTC Act and state consumer-protection laws.

Most significantly, Frimpong rejected Ticketmaster’s contention that the federal BOTS Act was designed to protect ticketing platforms from ticket resellers rather than potentially impose liability on the platforms themselves.

“The BOTS Act does not preclude platforms from facing liability,” Frimpong wrote, “and the Court will not read it as doing so.”

## Court Rejects Platform Shield Under BOTS Act

The [FTC sued Live Nation and Ticketmaster in September 2025](https://www.ticketnews.com/2025/09/ftc-sues-ticketmaster-says-company-turned-a-blind-eye-to-bots-while-deceiving-fans/), alleging the companies publicly represented that they enforced event ticket limits while knowingly allowing some professional brokers to evade them by operating large numbers of Ticketmaster accounts.

The government claims Ticketmaster then allowed tickets obtained through those methods to be resold on its secondary marketplace, generating another round of fees.

The original complaint alleges, among other things, that a senior Ticketmaster executive said the companies would “turn a blind eye as a matter of policy” to brokers exceeding posted ticket limits. Those remain allegations that Live Nation and Ticketmaster dispute.

Ticketmaster argued that exceeding a purchase limit is not itself enough to violate the BOTS Act, which prohibits circumvention of technological measures used to enforce ticket-buying restrictions. The company also argued that Ticketmaster itself does not sell resale tickets; instead, it operates a marketplace on which brokers or consumers sell tickets to other buyers.

Frimpong rejected both arguments at the pleading stage.

The judge found that the FTC had adequately identified technological systems intended to enforce Ticketmaster’s limits, including restrictions built into its purchasing interface and a system known as TicketCounter that the complaint says can analyze activity across accounts and identify attempts to evade purchase limits.

She also rejected Ticketmaster’s argument that “circumvention” under the BOTS Act must involve some form of direct technological manipulation comparable to hacking a security system.

The statute itself does not define the term, Frimpong noted. Applying its ordinary meaning, the court said circumvention can encompass avoiding or evading a restriction. That was enough for the FTC’s allegations that brokers created hundreds or thousands of accounts, sometimes using fictitious information and software allowing users to operate or verify multiple accounts, to survive dismissal.

The court similarly found that the FTC had sufficiently alleged that Ticketmaster “sells or offers to sell” resale tickets within the meaning of the law.

Ticketmaster had argued that the ticket owner, not Ticketmaster, is the seller. But the FTC’s complaint alleges that when a ticket is resold through Ticketmaster, the original ticket is voided and a new ticket with unique identifying information is issued to the resale buyer.

Based on those allegations, Frimpong said Ticketmaster’s role goes beyond merely providing a passive marketplace. She also concluded that the ordinary legal meaning of selling property does not necessarily require the seller to have owned or physically possessed it.

The ruling does not establish that Ticketmaster engaged in those practices unlawfully. It holds that the FTC has alleged enough facts to litigate that question further.

## Alleged Knowledge of Broker Activity Is Enough for Case to Proceed

Live Nation and Ticketmaster also argued that the complaint does not connect any specific resale ticket to an identifiable BOTS Act violation.

The judge found that level of transaction-by-transaction detail is not required at this stage.

According to the complaint, Ticketmaster had access to systems and data capable of identifying users who significantly exceeded purchase limits and was allegedly aware of specific brokers operating large numbers of accounts.

The FTC further alleges the companies “reduced, discontinued, or declined to implement” tools that could have more effectively enforced purchase limits against brokers.

Those allegations were sufficient, Frimpong concluded, to plausibly claim that Live Nation and Ticketmaster knew of alleged circumvention, had the ability to respond to it and nevertheless allowed the activity to continue.

That directly addresses [another defense Ticketmaster advanced earlier this year](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/05/live-nation-says-ticketmaster-is-protected-party-not-violator-in-ftc-bots-act-case/) after a federal judge allowed a separate FTC BOTS Act case against Maryland broker Key Investment Group to move forward.

Live Nation argued in May that the Key Investment Group ruling actually supported its position because the Maryland court treated Ticketmaster as the party whose security measures brokers allegedly circumvented — in the company’s words, the BOTS Act’s “protected party,” rather than a potential violator.

Frimpong expressly considered the Key Investment Group ruling. Although she noted that the Maryland decision is not binding in California, she found its reasoning consistent with her analysis and said imposing liability on brokers is not inconsistent with potentially imposing liability on ticket platforms as well.

## “+Fees” Pricing and Ticket-Limit Claims Also Survive

The court’s ruling extends beyond the BOTS Act.

Frimpong also allowed the FTC’s deceptive-pricing claims to continue, rejecting Ticketmaster’s argument that displaying “+Fees” alongside ticket prices adequately informed customers that additional charges would be added.

The FTC alleges Ticketmaster historically displayed prices excluding mandatory fees for much of the shopping process, with fees in some cases increasing the ultimate ticket cost by as much as 44%.

Although the total price was shown before consumers completed their purchases, the judge cited Ninth Circuit precedent establishing that a deceptive practice can occur at a consumer’s initial contact even if additional information is provided later.

Frimpong called the “+Fees” qualifier “vague and ambiguous,” reasoning that while it indicates additional charges exist, it does not tell buyers how substantial those charges will be.

The challenged practices concern earlier Ticketmaster pricing displays; the company has since moved to all-in pricing. The ruling nevertheless allows the FTC’s claims over the prior practices and its requested relief to continue.

The judge also refused to dismiss claims based on Ticketmaster’s advertised event purchase limits.

Ticketmaster argued that the limits themselves were real and publicly disclosed. But Frimpong said the government’s theory is broader: that Ticketmaster represented to artists and consumers that limits would meaningfully restrict individual buyers while allegedly knowing that some brokers routinely circumvented them through multiple accounts.

The complaint plausibly alleges those representations could be material to both artists setting ticket limits and consumers deciding how and where to attempt to purchase tickets, the court found.

The court additionally ruled that the BOTS Act’s three-year limitations period does not dispose of the case, allowed the FTC’s request for civil penalties to remain in play and rejected an argument that states could not participate in the same BOTS Act action alongside the FTC.

Ticketmaster, [in a statement provided to Billboard following the ruling](https://www.billboard.com/pro/ticketmaster-face-ftc-ticket-resale-lawsuit-bots-act/), maintained that it remains a leader in combating bots and other methods used by scalpers to acquire tickets.

“We continue to believe that this lawsuit is misguided and will ultimately fail,” the company said.

The FTC did not immediately provide Billboard with a response to the ruling.

The court said it will next issue an order setting a scheduling conference, moving the case beyond Ticketmaster’s effort to end the lawsuit at the pleading stage.
