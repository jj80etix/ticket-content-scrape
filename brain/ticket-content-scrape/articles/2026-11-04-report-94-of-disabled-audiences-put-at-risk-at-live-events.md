# Report: 94% of disabled audiences put at risk at live events

## Summary

A new Access is Everything "State of Access" report finds that 94% of UK disabled audiences take on personal risk when attending live events, with inconsistent accessibility provisions costing attendees, artists, and professionals an estimated £99-£184 per engagement, and projects that fixing this could unlock £402 million in additional annual discretionary spending. The report, based on nearly 300 respondents, is the charity's first major study in eight years.

### Key Points
- 94% of disabled UK audience members expose themselves to risk attending live events; only 6% report taking no additional risk.
- 62% of respondents say they repeatedly have to re-explain their access needs; 67% describe provisions as inconsistent.
- Inconsistency carries a "disability tax": ~£99 per audience visit, £138 per artist performance, £184 per professional engagement.
- Report projects £402m (€482m) in unlocked annual discretionary spend from consistently improved access.
- Funded by Purple Guide Grants and Nimbus Disability; first major Access is Everything report since the pandemic.

### Highlights
- 94% of disabled audiences report personal risk at live events; 58% see some improvement, but 67% call provisions inconsistent.
- Estimated £402m (€482m) in annual discretionary spend is left unrealized due to inconsistent accessibility.

### Industry Problem
Disabled patrons face unpredictable, inconsistently enforced accessibility accommodations across venues and events, forcing them to repeatedly justify their needs and absorb added cost and risk just to attend — a direct, quantifiable patron pain point.

### Proposed Solution
The report frames access as economically productive rather than a cost center; a primary ticketing platform could build standardized accessibility-request workflows, venue-level accommodation data, and consistent policy enforcement into the purchase/entry flow to reduce this friction and capture the associated spend.

## Transcript

The UK's Access is Everything finds that improved and consistently applied changes could unlock £402m annually

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/crowdshot.jpg)

Ninety-four percent of disabled audiences in the UK expose themselves to risk when attending live music and live events, according to a new report, underscoring ongoing accessibility hurdles in the industry.

This is according to Access is Everything’s State of Access report, the UK disabled-led accessibility charity’s first major report in eight years and first since the pandemic. It draws from a national base of nearly 300 Deaf, disabled and neurodivergent people of all ages, including artists and professionals in addition to audiences for the first time.

Only 6% of respondents said they take no additional personal risk when attending live events, and 62% said they have repeatedly explained their access needs.

“Our findings show that the majority of disabled people in the live music space describe access as something they must negotiate, not something they can trust,” says Catherine Pymar, CEO of Attitude is Everything.

While 58% agree there have been access improvements, 67% describe access provisions as inconsistent, which carries a personal disability tax for all parties. This translates to roughly £99 (€119) per audience visit, £138 (€165) per artist performance, and £184 (€220) per professional engagement.

If the needs of disabled audiences were met more consistently, the report projects that improved accessibility could unlock £402 million (€482m) in additional discretionary spending annually.

“The State of Access Report 2026 makes the case that access is not an additional provision, but economically productive, profitable and makes events safer,” Pymar says.

Organisers say they hope the study will help usher in new standards for progress across the national industry.

“By sharing the feedback with the sector, and supporting the music industry to embed access, we aim to improve the experiences for the disabled community, because by removing barriers, we don’t just open doors, we open up an entire industry,” Pymar says.

The report was supported with funding by the Purple Guide Grants and Nimbus Disability
