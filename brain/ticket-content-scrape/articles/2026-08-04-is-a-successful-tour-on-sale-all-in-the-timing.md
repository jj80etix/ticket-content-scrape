# Is a successful tour on-sale all in the timing?

## Summary

As touring calendars fill years in advance, IQ examines the industry-wide shift toward earlier onsales — 12-18 months out for top stadium tours — and the tradeoffs promoters and platforms navigate: earlier onsales improve cash flow, demand forecasting and marketing runway, but risk losing urgency and increase the odds fans' plans change before showtime. New Twickets resale data shows events with 300+ day lead times see notably higher ticket-to-resale conversion (67%) than sub-150-day-lead events (48%), with resale activity spread more evenly across a longer campaign rather than concentrated in the final two weeks.

### Key Points
- Top stadium tours now commonly go on sale 12-18 months ahead; arena tours are increasingly following (Olivia Rodrigo, Ariana Grande, Rush and Karol G cited with 12-14 month lead times).
- Twickets data: UK events with 300+ day lead times hit 67% resale conversion vs. 48% for sub-150-day lead times.
- Longer lead-time shows see resale activity spread more evenly (58% in the final two weeks) vs. shorter lead-time shows (69% concentrated in the final two weeks).
- Industry voices (AXS's Sam Ricketts, MC2 Live's Andrea Pieroni, Twickets' Richard Davies) frame onsale timing as now "almost as important as pricing the ticket," citing loyalty programs and payment plans as tools to sustain early-purchase urgency.

### Highlights
- Twickets: 300+ day lead-time events convert to resale at 67% vs. 48% for sub-150-day events — a direct, quantified link between onsale timing and secondary-market volume.
- Longer lead times shift resale activity earlier and more evenly across the campaign rather than concentrating it in the final two weeks pre-show.
- Industry consensus quote: "there is no longer a one-size-fits-all approach" — timing now ranks alongside pricing and routing as a strategic lever.

### Industry Problem
Promoters and primary platforms face a widening structural challenge: longer sales windows improve cash flow and forecasting but erode purchase urgency and increase the volume and duration of secondary-market activity as fans' plans shift between purchase and event.

### Proposed Solution
The item points to loyalty/membership programs, payment plans, and sustained multi-channel marketing as primary-market tools for preserving urgency across long campaigns — a build/improve signal for demand-forecasting and post-purchase engagement features (payment plans, presale/loyalty tooling) a primary platform like Etix could invest in; monitor whether trusted face-value resale integration becomes a competitive necessity as more inventory shifts into longer resale windows.

## Transcript

With touring calendars filling up faster than before, industry execs weigh in on the "double-edged sword" of lengthening sale windows

![Rush in LA](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Rush-in-LA-©-Richard-Sibbald.png)

Rush in LA © Richard Sibbald

With touring calendars at every level filling up faster than ever, the timing of a major tour on-sale has become an increasingly important part of the touring equation. While earlier on-sales improve cash flow, expand marketing opportunities, strengthen demand forecasting, and support premium sales, finding space in an increasingly crowded market raises an important question: how early is too early?

At the top end, stadium tours often go on sale 12-18 months in advance of the first show, with major residencies adding a new layer to the topic. Now, arena tours are increasingly following suit, as promoters and artists seek greater certainty in an increasingly expensive and competitive landscape.

“The market has moved toward earlier onsales because touring has become more complex and more expensive,” Andrea Pieroni, CEO of Italian promoter MC2 Live, tells *IQ*. “Promoters and artists want longer planning cycles, earlier visibility on demand, and more time to build campaigns.”

Sometimes, it is the result of announcing multiple territories simultaneously. [Olivia Rodrigo](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/04/olivia-rodrigo-unravels-massive-arena-run-202627/) and [Ariana Grande](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2025/08/ariana-grande-returns-with-the-eternal-sunshine-tour/) both announced multi-leg tours in one fell swoop, with the UK/EU dates going on sale about a year before the first show. And following the success of Rush’s North American comeback tour, the rock band quickly announced UK/EU dates, with the leg going on sale some 13 months in advance.

At the stadium level, Karol G offers another example: after headlining Coachella, she announced her expansive [*Tropitour*](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/04/karol-g-announces-global-stadium-tour/), giving North American fans three months’ notice while EU/UK dates went on sale roughly 14 months in advance.

With market saturation a key concern, there is a delicate balancing act between getting the ball rolling, boosting financial certainty and marketing visibility, and launching too early.

> “No artist is the same, and no fan is the same”

The shift reflects how dramatically touring has evolved in the past decade. Productions continue to grow in complexity, routing is expanding, financial risk is multiplying, and advanced venue bookings are causing schedules to be mapped out years in advance.

For ticketing providers, promoters, and artists, putting tickets into play earlier provides valuable information and cash flow long before the tour lands in venues.

“No artist is the same, and no fan is the same,” Sam Ricketts, AXS’ director, artist & promoter services, tells *IQ*. “It’s a fun puzzle for an artist and their team to put together when the best time to go on sale is. An artist’s wider business and life plans, with things like album cycles, also affect the timing.”

The stadium circuit is only getting busier. Last year, over 910 artists headlined at this level (up 16.5% from 2024), and the number of shows grew by over 12%, per [*IQ* analysis](https://www.iqmagazine.com/reports/global-stadium-report-2026/) of *Pollstar* data. And the [surging residency business](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/02/global-stadium-boom-accelerates-shift-to-residency-style-tours/) has also pushed lead times further out, in part to accommodate travelling audience segments. Ricketts points to Céline Dion’s forthcoming Paris residency, where she announced 10 shows next May [11 months in advance](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/06/celine-dion-adds-ten-more-dates-to-paris-residency/).

“That extra time gives a wider audience access and time to make those decisions on what day they can make, when they’re going to travel and who they’re going to travel with,” Ricketts says.

For fans, that gap can also make attendance more achievable, especially as ticket prices climb, by offering the ability to split costs over time. Longer, multi-channel marketing campaigns can also convert more fans into buyers, and can increase fans adding on a premium experience.

> “If you go too early, urgency disappears”

But maintaining momentum over an extended campaign presents its own set of challenges.

“Our attention is more scarce than ever,” Ricketts says. “Having good and varied ways to reach and remind fans and retarget them through different platforms is important. You have to make the most of the time that you have.”

Longer campaigns also create opportunities to deepen fan engagement post-purchase. Rather than viewing a ticket sale as the end of a transaction, ticketing companies increasingly see it as the start of a customer relationship.

“You’re in a fan’s pocket for a year,” Ricketts says. “How in that time can we leverage technology to help them have a better time or bring them closer to the artist?”

But while the commercial arguments are growing stronger, the strategy does come with risks.

“If you go too early, urgency disappears,” Pieroni says. “People like the announcement, but delay the purchase. Then six other shows are announced, holidays arrive, priorities change.”

With fans’ spending power regularly shifting, asking audiences to commit a year in advance inevitably introduces uncertainty around finances, personal commitments, and competing entertainment options.

> “Being closer to the event can create stronger momentum and better conversion”

“So yes, there is absolutely a point where going too far in advance becomes counterproductive, especially for mid-level tours,” Pieroni says. “For some global stadium acts, very early onsales still work because demand is immediate. For many others, being closer to the event can create stronger momentum and better conversion.”

With money a concern, fans also expect greater flexibility with buying tickets. While the top end of touring can sustain and is largely empowered by this widening gap, the trend is having a ripple effect on the rest of the touring ladder.

“With theatres, clubs and emerging artists, urgency and proximity are driving demand. It’s probably a more local audience,” Ricketts says.

And if fans splashed out hundreds to see their favourite stadium-level act a year in advance, the available funds may not be the same for tours announced with a shorter lead time. Twickets’ data shared with *IQ* suggests longer lead times also influence resale behaviour, with earlier onsales a part of greater conversion rates.

Looking at popular UK music venues in the past 18 months, the resale platform found that events with lead times exceeding 300 days recorded higher ticket conversion rates (67%) than events with lead times below 150 days (48%).

> “Resale tends to be more gradual rather than concentrated in the final days”

“Putting tickets on sale a year or more in advance is a double-edged sword,” Twickets founder Richard Davies tells *IQ*. “It gives fans more notice and artists greater certainty, but it also means people’s plans are much more likely to change between buying a ticket and the event itself. That’s one reason why we’ve seen more tickets entering the resale market for these longer-lead tours.”

For shows that went on sale earlier, 58% of resale transactions occurred during the final two weeks before the event. For shorter lead-time events, that figure rose to 69%, suggesting earlier onsales spread demand more evenly across the campaign rather than concentrating it immediately before show day.

“The encouraging finding, however, is that those tickets are also more likely to find a new home. With more time before the event, buyers have a longer window to discover listings, so resale tends to be more gradual rather than concentrated in the final days,” Davies says.

“Ultimately, if tickets are going on sale this far ahead, having a trusted face-value resale option becomes even more important for fans,” he adds.

In the primary market, payment plans, loyalty programmes, memberships, ticket upgrades, and premium experiences can all encourage earlier purchasing, all while making the purchase feel less daunting.

> “Timing the onsale has become almost as important as pricing the ticket”

“I think everyone’s job is to make it more attractive to book early,” Ricketts says. “Some venues or organisers will do that through loyalty schemes or memberships because having the cash in is also a benefit to the rights holders.”

But putting tickets on sale today is not a fixed formula. In the case of Fred again.., the opposite strategy of announcing shows mere days in advance has created scarcity and concentrated attention that drives immediate demand.

“There will always be outliers. Obviously not every artist can do that, but it’s a stark contrast with shows 14 months away,” Ricketts says.

As with many aspects of the modern touring business, there is no longer a one-size-fits-all approach. Timing, therefore, has become another key strategic lever alongside pricing, routing and marketing.

“Today there is no fixed formula,” Pieroni says. “Timing the onsale has become almost as important as pricing the ticket.”
