# Democratic Control of Congress and the Rise of Parallel Congressional and State AG Investigations

## Summary

With Democrats taking control of both chambers of Congress after the Nov. 3 midterms, Troutman Pepper Locke's State AG practice expects parallel congressional and state-AG investigations to intensify starting in January, when Democrats gain subpoena power and committee-agenda control. The alert flags "affordability and pricing" — junk fees, subscriptions, and algorithmic pricing — as one of the areas most likely to draw combined congressional and state-AG scrutiny.

### Key Points
- Democrats gain subpoena authority and committee control when the new Congress convenes in January 2026-27 session.
- Affordability/pricing named as a top parallel-scrutiny area: state AGs already target junk fees, subscriptions and algorithmic pricing (cites a bipartisan food-delivery fee effort as precedent).
- Congressional investigations are typically public; AG investigations can produce litigation, restitution and penalties — companies are advised to coordinate a single factual record across both tracks.
- Sen. Banking Chair Elizabeth Warren flagged as likely to prioritize AI, fintech and tech-adjacent oversight, areas where state AGs are already active.

### Highlights
- Junk-fee/algorithmic-pricing practices named explicitly as a likely target of combined House/Senate and state-AG scrutiny starting January 2026.
- Infrastructure for coordinated federal-state investigation already exists via multistate AG coalitions and information-sharing.

### Industry Problem
All-in/junk-fee pricing enforcement is set to intensify and broaden beyond FTC rulemaking into coordinated congressional and multistate-AG investigation, raising the compliance stakes for any platform with disclosed-vs-actual pricing gaps.

### Proposed Solution
The alert's recommendation is defensive posture: maintain a single, consistent factual record across potential congressional and AG inquiries. For Etix, this reinforces the Q9 monitoring priority — all-in pricing compliance should be treated as federal *and* state-AG exposure, not just an FTC-rule question, going into 2027.

On November 3, Democrats scored a resounding victory, taking control of both the House and Senate in the midterm elections. When the new Congress convenes in January, Democrats will gain subpoena authority, control over committee agendas, and substantially greater resources to investigate the Trump administration and private-sector conduct.

That shift has the potential to transform the investigative environment for companies doing business with the federal government or operating in politically sensitive consumer markets. In particular, Democratic control of Congress may create new opportunities for congressional committees and Democratic state attorneys general (AG) to pursue related conduct through parallel investigations.

### What Changes in January

Democratic control of Congress will transform existing minority oversight efforts into potential investigations backed by compulsory process. Letters and voluntary requests that companies or agencies resisted or answered narrowly may become investigative road maps, with recipients facing renewed document requests, testimony demands, or subpoenas.

Those investigations may also generate parallel scrutiny from state AGs. Congressional hearings and reports can surface documents, testimony, and allegations that prompt AGs to examine whether the same conduct affected consumers or violated state law. AG investigations, in turn, can produce public filings and enforcement records that shape congressional inquiries. These parallel tracks do not require a formal joint task force or information-sharing agreement. Congressional committees and AG offices can monitor one another’s work, pursue overlapping records, and seek materials that companies have provided to other government bodies.

The infrastructure for this interaction already exists. Federal and state consumer-protection authorities routinely share information and pursue joint or parallel enforcement, while multistate AG coalitions have established channels for identifying common issues and coordinating investigations. Congressional Democrats and Democratic AGs have also worked together publicly. In June 2025, Democratic members of Congress convened a forum with state AGs challenging actions by the Trump administration. In July 2026, New York AG Letitia James drew on her office’s cryptocurrency investigations in testimony before the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, illustrating how state enforcement can inform congressional oversight.

Recent Republican-led investigations provide another example of this dynamic. Congressional scrutiny of Minnesota Medicaid fraud led to hearings, a White House fraud task force, and increased attention to the performance of state-run Medicaid Fraud Control Units. That pressure has given AG offices additional incentive to demonstrate enforcement results, particularly in areas such as home health care and behavioral health.

With Democrats controlling the committee agenda in January, these interactions are likely to become more frequent and consequential, particularly where alleged misconduct can be framed as both a failure of federal oversight and a violation of state law.

### Where Parallel Scrutiny Is Most Likely

Parallel scrutiny is most likely where congressional priorities overlap with established state enforcement authority.

**Affordability and pricing.** Pricing practices connect congressional concerns about the cost of living with established state consumer-protection authority. State AGs have targeted so-called junk fees, subscriptions, inaccurate prices, and algorithmic pricing, including through a bipartisan effort addressing food-delivery fees, markups, and personalized pricing. Congress can examine whether these practices are widespread and whether federal regulators responded adequately, while AGs pursue specific alleged violations of state law.

**Technology and financial products.** AI, data brokers, fintech, digital assets, and other data-driven products implicate consumer protection, privacy, competition, and national security. As Senate Banking chair, Elizabeth Warren is expected to focus on AI, housing, private equity, credit card rates, and technology exports, several of which have bipartisan appeal. State AGs are already investigating algorithmic pricing, privacy, allegedly misleading fintech claims, and cryptocurrency practices.

**Companies benefiting from federal action.** Congressional Democrats have identified companies that donated to Trump-related initiatives, participated in Trump family business ventures, or received favorable government treatment as potential subjects of oversight. A donation or favorable federal decision does not establish a state-law violation. But congressional investigations may uncover communications or financial arrangements relevant to state consumer-protection, securities, antitrust, contracting, or fraud laws.

### Why the Parallel Track Matters

Congress and state AGs pursue different legal and institutional objectives, but they often seek the same documents and witnesses. Congressional committees may request materials provided to regulators or AGs, while state civil investigative demands may seek congressional submissions, testimony, and related communications.

The forums also create different risks. Congressional investigations often proceed publicly, while AG investigations can result in litigation, restitution, penalties, and injunctive relief. Companies facing scrutiny should therefore maintain a single factual record and coordinate their legal, government affairs, compliance, and communications strategies.

### The Bottom Line

State AGs have long stepped forward when they believe federal regulators are inactive or moving in a different direction. Congress has likewise used investigations to expose private-sector conduct, influence agency action, and shape public debate.

What could change under a Democratic Senate is the alignment of those efforts. Committee control would give Democratic senators greater ability to compel information and sustain public investigations at the same time Democratic AGs are pursuing their own enforcement priorities.

Companies should prepare not merely for a congressional inquiry or a state AG investigation, but for the possibility that one becomes the predicate, source of evidence, or public amplifier for the other.
