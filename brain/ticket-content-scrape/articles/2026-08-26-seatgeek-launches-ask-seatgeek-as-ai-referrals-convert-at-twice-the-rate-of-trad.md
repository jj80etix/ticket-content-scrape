# SeatGeek Launches ‘Ask SeatGeek’ as AI Referrals Convert at Twice the Rate of Traditional Search

## Summary

SeatGeek launched "Ask SeatGeek," a conversational AI search experience letting fans describe the event or ticket they want in natural language instead of using filters. The company says unique visitors arriving from AI tools like ChatGPT have more than doubled since January and convert at roughly twice the rate of traditional search traffic, though it declined to share underlying volume. The launch extends an AI strategy SeatGeek has built over the past year, including its earlier ChatGPT shopping integration.

### Key Points
- "Ask SeatGeek" lets users search in natural language rather than filter-based search.
- AI-tool-referred visitors (e.g., from ChatGPT) have more than doubled since January 2026.
- Those AI-referred visitors convert at ~2x the rate of traditional search traffic.
- SeatGeek did not disclose absolute traffic or transaction totals.
- Builds on SeatGeek's March 2026 launch of blended primary/resale inventory inside ChatGPT.

### Highlights
- AI-referred unique visitors more than doubled since January 2026.
- Conversion rate from AI referrals is ~2x that of traditional search.
- Second major AI-search initiative in 2026, following the March ChatGPT integration.

### Industry Problem
Traditional keyword/filter-based ticket search creates friction for fans who don't know exact event names or dates; competitors are racing to capture increasingly AI-mediated discovery traffic before it becomes the default entry point to ticket buying.

### Proposed Solution
SeatGeek built a natural-language conversational search layer directly into its marketplace and via ChatGPT integration. Etix could evaluate a lightweight conversational/AI-search entry point (or ChatGPT-plugin-style integration) for its own venue clients to capture emerging AI-referral traffic and benchmark conversion lift, keeping pace with primary/resale platforms already shipping this.

SeatGeek is bringing conversational artificial intelligence directly into its ticket marketplace after seeing visitors arriving from AI tools convert at roughly twice the rate of traditional search traffic, according to the company.

The ticketing marketplace launched [Ask SeatGeek](https://seatgeek.com/press/seatgeek-launches-ask-seatgeek-bringing-conversational-ai-search-to-its-marketplace) on Wednesday, a new conversational search experience that allows fans to describe the type of event or ticket they want using natural language rather than searching for a specific event and narrowing results through individual filters.

SeatGeek told TicketNews that unique visitors arriving from tools including ChatGPT have more than doubled since January and that those visitors convert at roughly twice the rate of users arriving from traditional search.

The company did not provide underlying traffic or transaction totals, making the figures more useful as an indication of how consumer behavior is changing than as a measure of the overall scale of AI-driven ticket sales. The higher conversion rate also does not necessarily mean AI itself is causing customers to buy more frequently; users who ask an AI assistant to find tickets may already represent a particularly high-intent group of shoppers.

[TicketNews previously covered SeatGeek’s March launch inside ChatGPT](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/03/seatgeek-claims-first-blended-primary-and-resale-ticketing-presence-in-chatgpt/), where the company claimed to be the first ticketing platform to make both official primary tickets and resale inventory available through a single ChatGPT experience. Rather than operating as a separate chatbot, the feature is incorporated into SeatGeek’s existing search interface. Straightforward searches can still return conventional results, while more complex requests can move users into a conversational experience where they can continue refining what they want.

A fan could, for example, ask for the “cheapest club seats for weekday Mets games” or “popular outdoor concerts this week under $100.” Queries can combine variables including price, location, timing, sightlines and amenities rather than requiring shoppers to adjust each parameter separately.

Results can incorporate SeatGeek’s interactive maps, view-from-seat imagery, Deal Score pricing information, seat-level amenities and fan-submitted tips before directing shoppers into the company’s normal checkout process.

“We’ve spent 17 years organizing the details that matter when someone chooses a ticket—from price and location to sightlines and seat perks,” SeatGeek co-founder and CEO Jack Groetzinger said. “Ask SeatGeek gives fans a simpler way to use that information: ask a question in their own words and get an answer they can act on.”

The launch reflects a broader shift in how ticketing companies may have to approach event discovery as consumers increasingly use AI assistants in place of, or ahead of, conventional web searches.

Traditional ticket search works particularly well when a buyer already knows what they want: a specific artist, team, venue or date. Conversational search potentially opens another part of the funnel, where a user knows the type of experience they want but has not decided on an event.

Someone looking for “outdoor concerts near me this weekend” or affordable premium seats at a baseball game may previously have needed to conduct several searches and compare events individually. SeatGeek is betting that allowing shoppers to express those preferences simultaneously can shorten the path between a vague idea and a purchasable ticket.

That distinction also helps explain why SeatGeek is emphasizing its proprietary ticketing data as part of the product.

General-purpose AI assistants can recommend events, but completing a ticket transaction requires current inventory along with an understanding of pricing, seating locations and the differences between individual listings. SeatGeek says Ask SeatGeek draws from its curated live-event and ticket data, with human oversight of data quality, rather than relying on information generated or scraped from elsewhere.

The system can also surface details that are difficult to capture using conventional filters, such as whether a seat includes food and drinks, has additional padding or sits in a section fans have identified as particularly good for catching foul balls.

For teams and venues using SeatGeek, the company argues that those additional data points can make their inventory more discoverable to consumers who may not begin their search by entering the name of a particular event.

SeatGeek is already pointing toward personalization as the next stage of the technology.

The company says future conversational recommendations could increasingly incorporate a customer’s previous purchasing behavior, such as where the fan has chosen to sit, the types of events they attend or preferences that emerge across multiple transactions.

That could eventually mean two customers asking the same question receive different ticket recommendations based on what SeatGeek knows about their previous behavior.

Such personalization would move AI ticket shopping beyond simply translating a natural-language request into a set of filters. It could allow marketplaces to influence which events and individual listings are surfaced based on a much broader assessment of what a particular customer is likely to purchase.

For now, Ask SeatGeek is beginning to roll out through SeatGeek’s desktop and mobile websites, including through its existing search bar and a dedicated Ask SeatGeek experience. Support for the company’s iOS and Android apps is expected to follow.

The launch gives SeatGeek its own venue for testing whether the unusually strong conversion behavior it says it is seeing from outside AI platforms carries over when conversational discovery becomes part of the ticket marketplace itself.
