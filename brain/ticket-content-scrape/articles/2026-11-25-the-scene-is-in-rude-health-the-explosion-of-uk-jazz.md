# ‘The scene is in rude health’: The explosion of UK jazz

## Summary

UK jazz is experiencing a commercial and demographic boom, with scene leaders telling IQ Magazine of a "distinct shift" toward younger audiences, growing mainstream festival bookings, and new promoter strategies. The 2026 EFG London Jazz Festival, which just wrapped, is highlighted as a festival actively modernizing to capture the genre's expanding, fusion-friendly audience.

### Key Points
- UK jazz scene leaders report a clear demographic shift toward younger listeners and growing mainstream festival presence.
- EFG London Jazz Festival's 2026 edition (wrapped 22 November) is cited as a case study in modernizing programming.
- Festival director Pelin Opcin emphasizes staying "one step ahead" by blending jazz with other genres/fusion acts.
- Promoters are reportedly evolving booking and audience-development strategies in response to the genre's growth.

## Transcript

## ‘The scene is in rude health’: The explosion of UK jazz

The UK scene is seeing distinct demographic shifts in both audiences and artists, new strategies for promoters, and a growing mainstream festival presence

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/smush-webp/2026/11/London-Jazz-Festival-2026-scaled.jpeg.webp)

London Jazz Festival 2026 © EFG London Jazz Festival

An explosion in the popularity of jazz among younger audiences in the UK has seen a “distinct shift” in the cultural and commercial potential of the genre.

Leaders within the national scene tell *IQ* that an uptick in mainstream festival bookings, evolving strategies for promoters, and a rapidly growing variety of audience offerings are the results of a resounding boom for the genre.

One of the live events capitalising on this was the recent EFG London Jazz Festival which wrapped up its 2026 edition on 22 November. The festival tells *IQ* that amid these clear shifts in the jazz landscape, it is prioritising being at the forefront of how the genre is continually modernising.

“It’s always about being one step ahead on bringing a new fresh outlook to the genre. Not only jazz, of course, but anything that could coexist with any creative music, while having jazz at the core,” says Pelin Opcin, the festival’s director.

The festival hosted artists including Yazz Ahmed, Anouar Brahem, and Caravan Palace in a range of sub-genre and fusion-themed events across ten days at venues including the Barbican Centre, Camden Roundhouse, South Bank Centre, King’s Place, and Cadogan Hall, which Opcin says speaks to the the increasingly diverse range of audience experiences that promoters can offer.

“ It’s an amazing time where you get not only as the concert hall listening experience of jazz, but also it being a part of the youth culture, nightlife, its own social essence,” she tells *IQ.*

This has also been observed at Blue Note, the new London branch of the famed brand of jazz clubs, which [opened in September](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/08/a-younger-generation-embracing-jazz-blue-note-on-opening-its-first-uk-venue/).

“There has been a distinct shift toward a younger demographic engaging with jazz in the past decade, and in fact I was told by a trusted source that streaming numbers have nearly doubled for jazz in the past few years in the UK,” the club’s director of music and programming, Nick Lewis, tells *IQ*.

> “\[jazz is\] part of the youth culture, nightlife, its own social essence”

This has given rise to a new set of “non-traditional” strategies on the business side of the genre, as it adjusts to the economic reality of an audience in demographic flux.

“Non-traditional strategies can be to offer more accessible ticket pricing for our later night second houses to ensure younger audiences can access the venue, while maintaining higher table spends during earlier dinner sessions,” Lewis explains.

“As ever, it’s about finding that balance and trying to represent as many different aspects of the music and varying fanbases as possible,” he adds.

Demographic change among the scene’s artists is not limited to age or background either. Lewis believes the European scene is “especially strong”, citing the Berlin-based Moses Yoofee Trio, who played two nights in Blue Note’s opening month, selling out “four performances to a broadly younger, diverse audience with their mix of jazz, fusion, and elements of rave and electronic music”.

Opcin agrees that the UK scene is benefitting from an uptick in artists from Europe and elsewhere outside the urban US epicentres of jazz activity, noting the genre has become a “household thing in other markets”.

This extends to other events in the UK capital such as the recent [Polish Jazz Festival](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/08/how-the-polish-jazz-festival-mobilised-the-diaspora/), which took a similar approach as the EFG London Jazz Festival in using heritage venues spread out across two weeks, and which bosses say “mobilised a diaspora” through jazz in London.

Accordingly, Louise Paley, music curator, programmer, and founder of Women In Jazz, says there has been “a real shift in how people encounter jazz”.

Some of this is driven by the growing mainstream appeal of acts that have risen through London’s grassroots, with a knock-on effect being the increasing genre fusion that jazz has been exposed to within this scene. This mainstream appeal is exemplified by Ezra Collective achieving third-line billing at Glastonbury as well as a prominent slot at Roskilde for 2027, and the likes of Kokoroko heading to Best Kept Secret festival in 2027.

“Ezra Collective, for example, have played a huge role in making it visible and accessible to a much wider audience. What I find interesting is that I think they’re part of a much bigger movement; they’re drawing on jazz, but then also Afrobeats, hip-hop, broken beat, and club culture,” Paley tells *IQ.*

This complex web of influences on the newer generation of jazz artists is also one of the main impetuses behind the programming strategy of the newly-opened Blue Note. But there is an economic as well as a cultural side to this strategy.

“Because younger crowds can have lower average food and beverage spend, it’s about finding a balance – a more mature crowd will bring higher spends on a certain day of the week so you don’t want to lose that – but you need to counterbalance those types of bookings with a predicted lower spend on your acts attracting younger demographics,” Lewis says.

Based on this diverse stylistic scene, Paley believes the jazz artist pipeline in the UK is in good health too. Acts coming through jam sessions organised by artist development programmes, as well as London’s music colleges, and informal collaborations are providing smaller venues with a wealth of young talent.

There is also a comprehensive ecosystem around this pipeline, with the scene’s stock of highly specialised promoters being a vital asset.

> “there has been a real shift in how people encounter jazz”

“ There’s a very important layer of those specialist promoters. The reason why they are special is because they’re very deeply connected to particular music communities,” Paley explains.

Once artists grow beyond this level of venue, however, Paley would still like to see the intermediate stage, where artists graduate from grassroots to larger stages, bolstered.

“There is a whole separate conversation around how ‘part two’ is is lacking at the moment and who is really taking on the risk for those more emerging artists because there are fewer opportunities nowadays,” she says.

The artist pipeline is also supported by organisations including Women in Jazz and the Serious Youth Network, connected to the London Jazz Festival’s promoter, and Tomorrow’s Warriors, which has produced leading artists including Nubya Garcia, Moses Boyd, and members of Ezra Collective.

For their part in this, Lewis says “the importance UK-wide of youth music organisations like Tomorrow’s Warriors cannot be understated in mentoring this next wave of young British jazz artists and remain as vital as ever, with a community focus”.

As a result of their work, “the scene is in rude health,” he tells *IQ.*

Ultimately, Opcin says signs observed by London Jazz Festival organisers show the true marker of the genre’s strength might be that it is outgrowing being siloed as a genre at all.

“ I know from the younger audience we have that they’re coming not because it’s a jazz show; it’s because they just love the artists,” she says. “It’s no longer about being classified.”
