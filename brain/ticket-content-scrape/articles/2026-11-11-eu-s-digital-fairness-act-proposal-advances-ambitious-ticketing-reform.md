# EU’s Digital Fairness Act proposal advances ‘ambitious’ ticketing reform

## Summary

The European Commission formalised its Digital Fairness Act (DFA) proposal, a sweeping crackdown on dynamic-pricing transparency and ticket resale that officials call the most ambitious EU consumer-protection reform in a generation. The rules would force virtual queue systems to show real-time all-in pricing and ban "drip pricing," while a parallel enforcement-powers revision lets the Commission fine companies up to 6% of global turnover. EU ticketing trade body FEAT welcomes the move but warns of cross-border enforcement gaps already seen under the Digital Services Act.

### Key Points
- Virtual queues must prominently show both the highest possible and current real-time price, inclusive of fees/taxes — "effectively banning drip pricing"
- Resale listings must display the original organiser-set total price, plus category/row/seat where applicable
- CPC Regulation revision grants the Commission authority to investigate EU-wide cases and fine up to 6% of worldwide annual turnover; non-EU firms must appoint an EU legal representative
- Also bans "dark pattern" tactics (fake sold-out claims) and personalised pricing/ads targeting minors
- FEAT (Sam Shemtob) welcomes the proposal but cites unresolved two-year-old DSA enforcement complaints as a cautionary precedent
- Proposal now moves to European Parliament and Council; Commission estimates up to €13.7bn in annual consumer savings if adopted

### Highlights
- DFA formally proposed today by the European Commission; not yet law — next stage is Parliament/Council negotiation
- Would mandate real-time, all-in price display on ticketing queues and ban drip pricing outright
- New CPC Regulation powers: fines up to 6% of global turnover, extraterritorial reach via required EU legal representatives for non-EU sellers
- Trade body FEAT flags DSA enforcement gaps (unresolved 2-year-old resale complaints) as a live risk to DFA's effectiveness

### Industry Problem
Fragmented, inconsistently enforced EU consumer-protection rules have let bad actors exploit gaps between national markets on dynamic pricing, drip fees and unauthorized resale, eroding fan trust in ticket pricing transparency across the continent.

### Proposed Solution
The DFA itself is the regulatory fix on offer — mandatory real-time all-in pricing on queues and resale, plus centralized EU enforcement with real financial teeth. For Etix, this is a trigger to audit EU-facing checkout and resale-adjacent flows for all-in price display ahead of likely passage, and to track the Parliament/Council timeline for compliance deadlines.

## Transcript

Ticket queues, resale, and cross-border enforcement are all at the heart of newly finalised EU legislation

![European Commission's Henna Virkkunen & Michael McGrath](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/European-Commissions-Henna-Virkkunen-Michael-McGrath.png)

European Commission's Henna Virkkunen & Michael McGrath

The European Commission today formalised the proposal for the Digital Fairness Act (DFA), progressing a pan-European crackdown on dynamic pricing and resale in what one commissioner says is the “most ambitious reform” of consumer protection rules in a generation.

Industry bodies like Live DMA previously rallied around the DFA, calling it “ [one of the clearest current opportunities](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/06/upcoming-deadline-clearest-opportunity-for-eu-dynamic-pricing-regulation/) ” for regulation of dynamic pricing on the continent.

With the proposal, lawmakers pushed ahead with reform efforts around transparent pricing. The proposal says that when virtual queue systems are utilised, ticket sellers will have to “prominently display” both the highest possible and current price in real time, and whether it is subject to variation in the case of dynamically priced events.

This will need to include additional fees, taxes, and charges. Taken together, the Commission says this will “effectively ban drip pricing”.

Ticket resale is also targeted by the legislation, which requires the original total ticket price charged by the organiser to be prominently displayed, with ticket category, row, and seat number displayed where applicable.

While the DFA’s progression is welcomed by FEAT (the Fully-authorised European Alliance for Ticketing), the non-profit trade organisation raises fears over gaps between markets for complete enforcement.

“It is encouraging that the Commission has recognised event ticketing as an issue, alongside the urgent need for stronger cross-border enforcement of existing consumer laws,” says managing director Sam Shemtob. “But the devil is in the detail. Our members’ experience of the Digital Services Act has been difficult, with some still awaiting responses from Digital Services Coordinators about ticket listings for concerts that took place two years ago.

He continues: “The Commission has recognised that consumer trust in event ticketing depends on stopping bad actors. It must now go further to curb a growing tsunami of fraudulent and abusive resale, by enabling the many small businesses that underpin Europe’s live performance sector to enforce their reasonable ticket terms and conditions easily and efficiently, enabling promoter-authorised resale systems that guarantee consumers entry to shows.”

Els Bruggeman, head of enforcement & policy at watchdog Euroconsumers, says the DFA strikes a “good balance” around consumer control, transparency, and safety – “but even the strongest rules only matter if they are properly enforced.”

The proposed law will also ban “dark pattern” marketing tactics, including fake claims around products being sold out, as a means to protect consumers from being pressured into decisions. Personalised ads and prices that target minors and take advantage of sensitive data are also due to be restricted.

“Shopping has never been more convenient, but convenience must never come at the expense of fairness. Strong consumer protection is the foundation of competitiveness,” says Michael McGrath, Commissioner for Democracy, Justice, the Rule of Law and Consumer Protection.

> “It must now go further to curb a growing tsunami of fraudulent and abusive resale”

“Today we are proposing the most ambitious reform of EU consumer protection rules in a generation, so both consumers and businesses can make the most of digital markets with trust and confidence,” he says.

With the proposal, the Commission has also progressed a centralised plan to revise existing rules “rapidly and effectively” as digital markets change.

A revision of the Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation (CPC Regulation) will also grant the Commission more authority to investigate large-scale cases across the EU and issue fines “of up to 6% of a company’s worldwide annual turnover”.

Beyond the EU, the proposal will also strengthen enforcement outside the Commission’s remit by requiring non-EU companies to appoint a legal representative within the Union to cooperate.

The proposals will now be discussed by the European Parliament and Council. If progressed, it could save EU consumers up to €13.7 billion annually.
