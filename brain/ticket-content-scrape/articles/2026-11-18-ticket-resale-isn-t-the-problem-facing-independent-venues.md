# Ticket Resale Isn’t the Problem Facing Independent Venues

## Summary

In a guest opinion for TicketNews, NATP's Zach Anderson argues that ticket resale isn't the cause of financial distress at independent venues, citing NIVA's own data that 64% of indie stages were unprofitable in 2024 alongside resale-transaction data showing under 1% of secondary volume touches small venues (vs. roughly 75% concentrated at the ~200 largest venues tied to major sports/tours). He contends NIVA's resale-price-cap advocacy would leave Live Nation/Ticketmaster's primary pricing power untouched and could entrench the dominant platforms NIVA is simultaneously challenging in court over the DOJ settlement. This is an opinion piece from a reseller-industry trade group with an obvious stake in opposing resale restrictions, so its framing should be read as advocacy, not neutral analysis.

### Key Points
- NIVA data: 64% of independent venues were unprofitable in 2024.
- Author's reviewed resale data: <1% of secondary transactions involved small independent venues; ~75% concentrated at the 200 largest (20,000+ capacity) venues.
- Venue closures cited (Crowbar/Tampa, Bottom of the Hill/SF, multiple Chicago venues) were attributed to rent, labor, artist fees, and insurance — not resale.
- Live Nation now owns, operates, or holds booking/equity ties to 333 North American venues (460 worldwide) plus ~$800M in 2026 venue-expansion spend.
- Author (NATP, a reseller trade group) argues resale price caps restrict fan/reseller choice without addressing venues' actual cost drivers.
- Piece notes NIVA is separately fighting Live Nation's market power in court over the proposed DOJ settlement.

### Highlights
- NIVA: 64% of independent venues unprofitable in 2024 (self-reported).
- <1% of reviewed resale transactions involved small independent venues; ~75% concentrated at the 200 largest venues.
- Live Nation holds ownership/booking/equity ties to 333 North American venues, 460 worldwide, plus ~$800M in 2026 venue-expansion spend.

### Industry Problem
Independent venues face real cost pressure (rent, labor, artist fees, insurance) and a growing competitive squeeze from vertically integrated major promoters/venue operators — but resale restriction, the policy indie venues are lobbying hardest for, doesn't address either driver and risks strengthening the dominant primary platforms instead.

### Proposed Solution
The piece argues against resale price caps and for targeted anti-fraud/anti-deception rules instead; for Etix, the signal is to keep tracking resale/transferability legislation (Q11) for how it could reshape secondary-market dynamics (Q6) without assuming it fixes venue economics, and to note the continued erosion of primary-platform competition from Live Nation's venue consolidation.

**Guest Opinion | By Zach Anderson**

America has a robust live entertainment ecosystem, offering fans opportunities to enjoy everything from small club shows in New Orleans and up-and-coming country performers at Billy Bob’s in Fort Worth to college football in Boulder and major concert tours in stadiums from coast to coast.

Small, independent music venues are a vital part of that landscape, but many are struggling to survive. They face rising labor and operating costs, intense competition to book artists who will sell tickets, and growing pressure from some of the world’s largest entertainment companies, which compete with them more than ever.

A [June 2025 Eventbrite article](https://www.eventbrite.com/blog/niva-essential-role-of-independent-music-venues/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) described more than 8,000 small music venues in the United States. The National Independent Venue Association (NIVA) says it has [nearly 1,500 members](https://www.nivassoc.org/join?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), including independent venues, promoters and festivals. NIVA has made restricting ticket resale one of its most visible legislative priorities.

Ticket resale price caps restrict what fans and sellers may charge for tickets after the original sale, while leaving primary-market ticket prices and fees untouched. That includes prices charged by Ticketmaster, the world’s largest ticketing company, and its parent, Live Nation, the world’s largest concert promoter.

That’s what makes NIVA’s focus on ticket resale so difficult to understand. Its members face significant financial challenges, but the resale activity they’re seeking to restrict appears to have remarkably little to do with those challenges.

## What Independent Venues Are Actually Experiencing

[NIVA’s own data](https://www.nivassoc.org/stateoflive?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) says that 64% of independent stages were not profitable in 2024. And a string of news reports from coast to coast has documented the difficulties small music venues face.

In Tampa’s Ybor City, Crowbar closed in July 2026 after 20 years. Owner Tom DeGeorge cited rising costs, competition from larger entertainment companies and the limitations of operating in a building he could not purchase. Meanwhile, Live Nation announced plans for a new venue in the neighborhood.

[WUSF documented](https://www.wusf.org/arts-culture/2026-06-10/ybor-city-crowbar-owner-leads-charge-live-nation-tampa-music-venue?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) the broader loss of music venues in Ybor City, alongside rising rent and parking costs and competition from corporate promoters and ticketing operators. DeGeorge has spoken publicly about the influence that control of tours, artists and venues gives large entertainment companies.

In San Francisco, the owners of the long-running Bottom of the Hill announced plans to close after 35 years amid financial and personal pressures and increasing difficulty competing with larger concert companies for artists. In October, however, a [new owner stepped in](https://www.sfgate.com/sf-culture/article/bottom-of-the-hill-owner-22491509.php?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) with plans to keep the club operating in 2027. The change is welcome news, but the circumstances leading to the original closure announcement remain instructive.

In Chicago, the [Sun-Times reported](https://chicago.suntimes.com/music/2026/02/03/chicago-indie-venues-health-metro-ramova-salt-shed?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) that nearly three-quarters of independent live entertainment venues were unprofitable, according to a local industry study. Owners described higher artist fees, labor costs, production expenses, insurance and taxes.

Notably, none of these reports identified ticket resale as the primary cause of the financial struggles they described.

When a professional ticket seller purchases a ticket at the original seller’s asking price, that initial sale generates the same proceeds as a purchase by any other customer. The fan who attends on a valid resold ticket spends money on concessions and merchandise, just like anyone else in the building.

The economic problems described in those articles are real. But ticket resale does not appear to play a significant role in driving the expenses that threaten these businesses. It does not make rent more expensive, drive up labor costs or make it harder for independent venues to compete for artists.

## Frustration With Resale Is Not the Same as Economic Harm

Over the past few years, I have regularly seen independent venue representatives, often members of NIVA, appear at legislative hearings to express frustration with ticket resale. Their complaints frequently involve fans who paid more than the original ticket price, purchased through a website they mistakenly believed was the venue’s official seller, or experienced problems using their tickets.

NIVA’s own research identifies resale among the operational challenges reported by independent venue operators. But identifying a complaint is not the same as demonstrating economic harm or showing that broad resale restrictions will solve it.

A deceptive venue website, unauthorized inventory mirroring or a seller who fails to deliver valid tickets are problems worth addressing. NATP has repeatedly said that we support protecting consumers from deception and fraud.

But frustration with a resale price is different from demonstrating harm. When a ticket is lawfully purchased at the original seller’s asking price, its subsequent resale does not take money away from that original transaction. The fan who attends with that ticket is no less a fan than someone who purchased directly through the venue box office.

A fan who knowingly purchases a ticket from a legitimate reseller, even at a price above face value, is exercising consumer choice.

Just as venues, artists and promoters should be free to establish the prices they believe their tickets are worth, why shouldn’t someone who lawfully purchases a ticket have the freedom to determine what it is worth when they decide to resell it?

Independent venues and ticket resellers operate fundamentally different businesses. Venues book artists, operate facilities and present live entertainment. Professional ticket sellers help consumers find tickets for thousands of events across the country and purchase the tickets they want to attend those events. Both provide services that fans value, and neither is competing to do the same job for fans.

Yet NIVA advocates resale restrictions that could adversely affect millions of consumers, harm legitimate businesses and potentially strengthen the dominant primary ticketing companies that its members compete with for customers. That hardly seems like an outcome independent venues should be seeking.

All of this raises a straightforward question for lawmakers: How would restricting lawful ticket resale help a struggling 2,500-seat music venue become more viable? Would it help that venue pay its rent, keep the lights on amid rising expenses or attract artists? Perhaps NIVA needs to focus more of its legislative efforts on what directly affects its members.

## The Elephant in the Room

Meanwhile, there is a much larger competitive development taking place across the live entertainment industry. According to its [2025 annual report](https://investors.livenationentertainment.com/sec-filings/annual-reports/content/0001335258-26-000009/lyv-20251231.htm?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), Live Nation owned, operated, held exclusive booking rights for or had significant equity interests in 333 venues in North America and 460 worldwide. That includes 125 clubs globally with capacities below 2,000, the same category as many independent music venues.

In June 2025, Live Nation [announced a $1 billion commitment](https://news.livenationentertainment.com/news/live-nation-investing-1-billion-in-u-s-music-venues-boosting-americas-economic-and-cultural-revival?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) to build or revitalize 18 additional venues across the United States. The company also [projected approximately $800 million](https://investors.livenationentertainment.com/sec-filings/all-sec-filings/content/0001335258-26-000052/lyv-2026q3xex991er.htm?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) in venue expansion and improvement spending for 2026.

At the same time, Oak View Group (OVG) provides venue-management, hospitality and other services to hundreds of facilities around the world. OVG was co-founded by Irving Azoff, the former executive chairman of Live Nation, and maintains extensive relationships throughout the live entertainment industry. OVG and Live Nation have also collaborated on venue projects, including booking and promotion arrangements.

Investment in new venues is not inherently bad. More stages, more shows and more seats can benefit artists and fans. But larger operators with extensive venue networks, promotion relationships and access to touring artists can enjoy advantages that smaller venues struggle to match.

These are substantial investments in venue development and operations. They are the kind that can directly affect small, independent venues. As more venues come under the management or commercial influence of large operators, what happens to independent venues and promoters? What happens to competition to book artists or secure desirable dates?

Independent venue owners have described these competitive pressures in reporting surrounding venue closures and financial struggles across the country.

NIVA itself has [challenged Live Nation’s market power](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/09/niva-live-nation-ticketmaster-breakup-tour-cap/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), including urging a federal court to reject the proposed government settlement as inadequate. But advocating resale price caps would restrict the choices available to fans while leaving primary sellers’ pricing power intact. Those policies could ultimately strengthen the dominant company NIVA has spent years challenging.

## A Better Approach for Fans and Lawmakers

NATP represents professional ticket sellers who provide valuable services to consumers. Over the past three decades, millions of fans have chosen to buy from our members. Some come to us because they want access to a sold-out event. Others are looking for tickets below face value, better seats or simply a more convenient buying experience.

The majority of fans I encounter want access to the widest possible selection of tickets at the best possible prices. They want a straightforward buying experience and confidence that the tickets they purchase are legitimate.

Just as a fan can choose which venue to visit on a Saturday night, they should be able to choose where they purchase their tickets. A fan who buys from an independent ticket seller is no less of a fan than someone who purchases directly from the box office.

Independent venues are a vital part of the live entertainment landscape. But professional ticket sellers are, too. We may have different roles in the ticket industry, but we share a focus on fans. The economic challenges facing independent venues are real, and they likely aren’t going away. But repeatedly pointing to ticket resale is off-base and does not change the financial realities small venues face.

Before lawmakers impose price caps on a valuable segment of the ticket industry, those advocating the restrictions should clearly demonstrate the harm they seek to prevent and explain how their proposed policies would solve it.

We should be working to give fans more ways to enjoy live entertainment, not fewer.

*Zach Anderson is director of strategic initiatives at the National Association of Ticketing Professionals (NATP). The views expressed are his own.*
