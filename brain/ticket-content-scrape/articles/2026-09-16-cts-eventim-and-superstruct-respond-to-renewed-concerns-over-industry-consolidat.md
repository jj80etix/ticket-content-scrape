# CTS Eventim and Superstruct respond to renewed concerns over industry consolidation

## Summary

CTS Eventim and Superstruct pushed back on renewed European policy calls to rein in live-music-industry consolidation, arguing that large consolidated operators and independent festivals "strengthen each other." The response follows a joint statement from industry bodies Live DMA, Reset! Network, and SMA warning that four groups — Live Nation, Superstruct, CTS Eventim, and AEG — are now connected to more than 200 of Europe's largest festivals. The dispute comes days after Belgium's competition authority approved Live Nation's acquisition of Pukkelpop, conditioned on a 10-year guarantee of the festival founder's creative independence.

### Key Points
- Industry bodies Live DMA, Reset! Network, and SMA urged the European Parliament to develop stronger competition/antitrust tools for the live-music sector
- Four corporate groups (Live Nation, Superstruct, CTS Eventim, AEG) are tied to 200+ of Europe's largest festivals, per Live DMA/Reset! Network research
- Belgium's competition authority approved Live Nation's Pukkelpop acquisition with a 10-year independence safeguard
- Eventim says a "diverse ecosystem of independent...and strong European players...are not opposites"; Superstruct cites its new "Artistic Code" as protecting creative independence across its 80+ festival portfolio
- Rising artist fees and production costs are cited industry-wide as pressuring independent organizers' margins

### Highlights
- The Live DMA/Reset!/SMA statement ties 4 groups (Live Nation, Superstruct, CTS Eventim, AEG) to 200+ major European festivals
- Belgium's competition authority approved Live Nation's Pukkelpop deal with a 10-year creative-independence safeguard for the founder
- Superstruct's new "Artistic Code" and Eventim's network model (FKP Scorpio, Barracuda Music, Gadget; 50+ festivals) are both cited as answers to consolidation concerns

### Industry Problem
European policymakers and independent-sector bodies argue that festival consolidation among a handful of large groups risks standardizing programming and squeezing out independent voices, particularly as rising artist fees and production costs already pressure smaller organizers' margins.

### Proposed Solution
Both consolidators frame group-level financial/operational backing plus contractual creative-independence guarantees (e.g., Superstruct's Artistic Code, the Pukkelpop 10-year safeguard) as the answer; Etix should monitor how EU competition tools evolve around live-event consolidation, since regulatory tightening here could reshape which platforms and venues remain viable independent customers.

## Transcript

Earlier this month, three industry bodies called on the European Parliament to consider stronger competition and antitrust tools

![Rock am Ring](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/2024-Leoniden-Jule-Mehrhoff-41174.jpg)

Leoniden at Rock Am Ring, a festival connected to CTS Eventim © Jule Mehrhoff

Large, consolidated festival operators and European independent events must co-exist to “strengthen each other”, CTS Eventim has said, in response to renewed policy calls from some of the continent’s industry bodies to address consolidation in the live music business.

The remarks come as CTS Eventim and Superstruct spoke to *IQ* after a collection of sector bodies said in a statement that increased consolidation risks “greater standardisation, fewer independent voices, and a cultural landscape shaped purely by financial interests”.

Earlier this month, the live industry bodies, Live DMA, Reset! Network, and the Syndicat des Musiques Actuelles (SMA), gathered at the European Parliament to outline concerns around the current structure of the continent’s live ecosystem.

They called on officials to develop stronger competition tools and cultural-sector-specific antitrust rules, while also asking for greater financial support for small- to mid-level independent organisations.

In the written [statement](https://live-dma.eu/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Statement_From-Mapping-Who-Owns-What-in-Live-Music-to-European-Action.pdf), the organisations argue that when a small number of companies control cultural production and distribution, “they gain influence over the ideas, narratives, and representations that reach the public”.

Within the festival sector, the cohort points to four corporate groups – Live Nation, Superstruct, CTS Eventim, and AEG – that it says are “connected to more than 200 of Europe’s largest festivals”, citing research published [earlier this year](https://live-dma.eu/live-dma-reset-network-publish-new-maps-of-ownership-in-european-live-music/).

“We must defend the freedom of imagination and Europe’s cultural sovereignty,” said Member of the European Parliament (MEP) Emma Rafowicz, vice chair of the Committee on Culture and Education and co-rapporteur of AgoraEU.

In response, representatives from Eventim and Superstruct both outline that, against a backdrop of rising financial pressures, being part of wider networks can help safeguard events while preserving their individual identities and creative foundations.

> “We believe Europe needs both: a diverse ecosystem of independent small and medium-sized businesses and strong European players with the scale to invest”

“Eventim Live combines strong local entrepreneurship with the scale of an international group,” an Eventim spokesperson tells *IQ*. “Our partners retain their identity and market expertise while benefiting from technology, networks, expertise, and financial strength.”

Eventim has a hand in over 50 festivals, including Rock Am Ring/Rock Im Park, Hurricane/Southside, and Best Kept Secret, through a network that includes FKP Scorpio, Barracuda Music, and Gadget.

In a statement to *IQ*, a Superstruct spokesperson said: “Creative independence is at the heart of our business model. Our festivals and founders have the creative control – the autonomy to run their events – from programming, to line-up, to identity. Our role is to provide the financial stability and infrastructure to help them thrive.”

The company recently rolled out an [Artistic Code](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/09/superstruct-reveals-artistic-code-for-festival-portfolio/), which stands to protect artistic expression and creative freedom across its portfolio of more than 80 festivals worldwide.

The Superstruct spokesperson continued: “Each festival in our portfolio is a successful and distinctive brand in its own right. We believe our model protects against the standardisation of live events that is increasingly being criticised elsewhere in the industry. That combination of independence and support is what makes Superstruct unique.”

The issue has also reached national competition authorities. Recently, Belgium’s competition authority approved [Live Nation’s acquisition of Pukkelpop](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/09/live-nations-pukkelpop-deal-approved-with-ten-year-independence-safeguard/) after the company made “binding” commitments to keep creative direction of the event with the festival’s founder for at least ten years.

The decision highlights one of the central questions around industry consolidation: how can larger networks’ financial resources and infrastructure coexist with creative independence and individuality in today’s market? That balance is one increasingly debated across the industry, recently discussed by a panel of agents and festivals during the International Festival Forum (IFF) in London last week.

During the session, Solo agent Charly Beedell-Tuck said it had changed the “ [balance of power](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/09/consolidation-and-the-balance-of-power-agents-and-festivals-on-independence-in-2026/) “, and added that the “mass booking” of festivals had contributed to increasingly similar lineups across events.

Many [independent organisers](https://www.iqmagazine.com/magazine/the-state-of-independents/) argue that their status allows them to take creative risks and respond to feedback quickly. At the same time, rapidly rising [artist fees and production costs](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/01/european-festival-survey-festivals-lean-domestic-as-costs-and-fees-rise/) – often cited as the biggest challenge for organisers today – can put significant pressure on events’ viability, particularly independent ones operating on thinner margins.

It is against this backdrop that Eventim argued that both sides of the industry should coexist.

“We believe Europe needs both: a diverse ecosystem of independent small and medium-sized businesses and strong European players with the scale to invest, innovate, and compete internationally. These are not opposites – they strengthen each other,” the Eventim spokesperson concludes.
