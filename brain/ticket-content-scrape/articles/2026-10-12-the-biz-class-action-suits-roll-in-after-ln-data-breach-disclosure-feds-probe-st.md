# The Biz: Class Action Suits Roll In After LN Data Breach Disclosure; Feds Probe Streaming Fraud; NYC’s ‘Click To Cancel’ & More

## Summary

Live Nation disclosed a data breach to Vermont's attorney general on Oct. 1, exposing Social Security and government ID numbers for "a small number of individuals," and is now facing at least three class action suits alleging inadequate data security. Separately, the U.S. Copyright Office opened an inquiry (comments open through Nov. 23) into AI-driven streaming fraud and its effect on royalty distribution, and New York City became the first city to mandate "click to cancel" for subscription services, treating hard-to-cancel flows as a deceptive trade practice.

### Key Points
- Live Nation disclosed the breach to Vermont's AG Oct. 1; affected individuals are being notified and offered monitoring support.
- At least three class actions have been filed alleging Live Nation failed to implement "reasonable and adequate" data security (per Digital Music News).
- This is Live Nation's third disclosed breach after 2018 (UK Ticketmaster, £1.25M fine) and 2024 (larger, third-party storage vendor).
- U.S. Copyright Office is soliciting comment on AI-enabled streaming fraud's impact on royalty pools; prompted by a May letter from Rep. Scott Fitzgerald.
- NYC's new "click to cancel" rule requires cancellation to be as easy as sign-up, enforced via escalating fines for deceptive trade practice.

### Highlights
- Three class actions filed post-disclosure; this is Live Nation's third breach since 2018.
- UK's 2018 breach fine for Ticketmaster was £1.25M ($1.65M) — a precedent for potential exposure here.
- NYC's click-to-cancel rule is enforceable now via deceptive-trade-practice fines, a template regulators could extend to ticketing cancellation/refund flows.

### Industry Problem
Repeated breaches at the dominant primary platform keep data-security and litigation risk squarely in the regulatory and legal spotlight for ticketing, while city-level consumer-protection rules (like NYC's cancellation mandate) show regulators are willing to target consumer-facing friction beyond pricing alone.

### Proposed Solution
No product fix is described in the item itself; for Etix, the takeaway is to keep data-security posture and incident-response practices audit-ready, and to watch whether cancellation/refund-ease rules like NYC's extend to ticketing — the trigger to act would be a state or city rule explicitly naming ticket purchase/cancellation flows.

On Oct. 1, Live Nation disclosed a data breach to Vermont’s attorney general, saying “a small number of individuals” had sensitive information including Social Security and government ID numbers exposed.

In the wake of that disclosure, the live giant is facing at least three class action suits, [according to Digital Music News](https://www.digitalmusicnews.com/2026/10/07/live-nation-data-breach-class-action-lawsuits/).

“Recently, we identified, contained, and secured a cybersecurity incident,” Live Nation said in a statement. “Following a thorough internal review and independent investigation by a leading cybersecurity firm, we found the incident was limited in scope and had no impact on our operations. The small number of individuals whose information may have been affected are being notified and offered support to monitor their information.”

DMN says the suits allege breaches of duty by Live Nation to implement “reasonable and adequate” data security.

Live Nation has faced breaches in the past, in 2018 and 2024. The first targeted British Ticketmaster customers and the company was fined £1.25 million ($1..65M). The second was larger and hit a third-party storage service.

**Feds Launch Streaming Fraud Inquiry**

The U.S. Copyright Office is seeking information on streaming fraud, its effects on the economy and how it is connected to music generated with AI, according to an Oct. 7 notice in the Federal Register.

Comments are open through Nov. 23.

The inquiry follows a May letter from Rep. Scott Fitzgerald, a Wisconsin Republican, asking the USCO to look into the matter.

“While there is nothing inherently wrong with using AI as a creative tool in music development, or the organic growth of AI-generated music, specifically, generative AI can serve as a catalyst for criminals to perpetrate streaming fraud,” he wrote. “When AI-generated outputs compete against human-made works, they divert money from a finite royalty pool that would otherwise be distributed to actual rights holders. If done fraudulently by bad actors deploying bots or other forms of manipulation, it represents a clear theft of the royalty pool.”

**NYC Launches ‘Click to Cancel’**

New York became the first city in the world to require subscription services — including streamers — to implement “click to cancel,” a rule requiring clear and conspicuous cancellation in the same manner as what was used to sign up for the service.

Failing to offer it is now considered a deceptive trade practice by the city and is punishable by escalating fines.
