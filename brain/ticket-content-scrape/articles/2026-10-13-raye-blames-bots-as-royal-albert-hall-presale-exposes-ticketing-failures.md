# RAYE Blames Bots as Royal Albert Hall Presale Exposes Ticketing Failures

## Summary

British singer RAYE says her team received 1.8 million registrations for roughly 5,000 seats at her October 23 Royal Albert Hall show, attributing hundreds of thousands of them to AI-powered scalper bots and switching to manual applicant screening in response. Fans still reported presale-code failures and site crashes during the resulting sale, echoing Ticketmaster's 2022 Eras Tour meltdown and raising transparency questions about how the restricted presale allocated access.

### Key Points
- 1.8M registrations for ~5,000 seats; team blames AI bots for "hundreds of thousands" of fake entries.
- Organizers abandoned automated presale selection for manual review of applicants.
- Fans reported site crashes, malfunctioning queues, and rejected access codes during the sale.
- No independent confirmation yet of the bot-detection methodology or allocation criteria used.
- Draws direct comparison to Ticketmaster's 2022 Eras Tour onsale failure.

### Highlights
- 1.8 million registrations vs. ~5,000 available seats — a 360:1 demand ratio.
- Team's unverified claim: "hundreds of thousands" of fake, AI-bot-driven registrations.
- Manual fan-vetting replaced automated presale selection with no published criteria.
- Technical failures (crashes, bad codes) persisted even after switching to manual screening.

### Industry Problem
High-demand onsales remain structurally vulnerable to bot-driven registration floods, and the industry's current response — manual, opaque fan-vetting — trades one fairness problem (bots crowding out real fans) for another (undisclosed allocation criteria), while doing little to fix underlying platform reliability under load.

### Proposed Solution
The item describes no durable fix beyond ad hoc manual review, which doesn't scale and lacks transparency. A primary platform like Etix could differentiate by building verifiable, auditable registration-to-access flows (e.g., published anti-bot detection logic, transparent allocation odds) paired with onsale infrastructure load-tested for extreme demand spikes, turning fairness and reliability into a marketed feature rather than a post-hoc apology.

British singer RAYE is blaming scalpers and artificial intelligence-powered bots for overwhelming registration for her upcoming Royal Albert Hall concert, but the resulting changes to the ticketing process — and subsequent technical problems experienced by fans — are raising broader questions about the failures of primary ticketing systems and the industry’s increasing efforts to restrict independent ticket resale.

The singer revealed Tuesday that her team received 1.8 million registrations for approximately 5,000 seats at her October 23 performance, *This Symphony May Contain Hope*. She attributed hundreds of thousands of those registrations to automated activity by scalpers, prompting organizers to abandon their original presale selection process in favor of manually reviewing applicants.

In a [statement shared on social media](https://x.com/raye/status/2109846823179608547?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), RAYE said the registration system had been overwhelmed by scalpers using AI bots to create “hundreds of thousands of fake registrations.”

She said her team would manually identify applicants believed to be genuine fans and provide those selected with access codes ahead of Tuesday’s noon presale.

## When Everything Becomes a Bot Problem

The term “bot” encompasses a wide range of automated online activity, from software generating registration requests to programs monitoring ticket availability, attempting purchases or circumventing purchase limits.

Those are different activities, presenting different technical challenges and requiring different responses.

RAYE’s team has not publicly provided a detailed breakdown explaining how the allegedly fraudulent registrations were identified, how many distinct legitimate customers registered or whether automated accounts successfully acquired tickets.

Nor has it established that the reported difficulties accessing the actual ticket sale were directly caused by the same automated registrations that prompted the manual screening.

There is little reason to doubt that high-demand concert ticket sales attract automated activity, including operations seeking to circumvent purchase limits. But the presence of such activity does not explain every technical malfunction or eliminate the responsibility of ticketing operators to maintain functioning systems.

The decision to manually select presale participants also introduces questions about how access is distributed.

RAYE’s team has not publicly detailed the criteria used to identify genuine fans, the number of applicants selected or what percentage of available inventory was allocated to the restricted presale.

There is no evidence that applicants were selected based on their willingness to pay or that tickets were deliberately withheld to manipulate prices. But without greater transparency about the selection process and ticket allocation, consumers have limited ability to evaluate how fairly the available inventory was distributed.

That is particularly consequential in a ticketing market where fans frequently have only one authorized primary sales channel for a specific event.

## Echoes of Taylor Swift’s Eras Tour Ticketing Disaster

The situation carries echoes of the disastrous initial ticket sales for Taylor Swift’s Eras Tour in November 2022.

During that sale, Ticketmaster’s systems struggled with extraordinary demand, lengthy queues and technical failures. The company subsequently [canceled the planned general public onsale](https://www.ticketnews.com/2022/11/ticketmaster-cancels-general-public-sale-for-taylor-swift-tickets/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), citing overwhelming demand and insufficient remaining inventory.

Ticketmaster blamed unprecedented traffic, including automated activity, while consumers questioned how a platform that required advance registration through its Verified Fan system could be so thoroughly overwhelmed by a purchasing audience it had attempted to screen.

The cancellation also raised questions about inventory transparency.

As [TicketNews reported at the time](https://www.ticketnews.com/2022/11/skoufis-others-question-ticket-holdbacks-in-taylor-swift-mess/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), critics questioned how many tickets had actually been made available during the initial sales, how much inventory remained held back and when additional tickets might enter the market.

Ticketmaster and Swift’s team subsequently [began offering additional tickets to selected fans](https://www.ticketnews.com/2022/12/ticketmaster-taylor-swift-begin-selling-held-back-tickets/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), illustrating how the cancellation of a general sale did not necessarily mean every available ticket had already been sold.

Those subsequent releases did not establish that inventory had been deliberately withheld to inflate prices. They did, however, demonstrate the importance of understanding how event organizers control the timing and distribution of tickets.

RAYE’s situation is considerably smaller in scale, and there is an important difference: Her team has not canceled the general public sale.

Remaining tickets are still scheduled to become available Thursday, October 15, at 10 a.m. BST, according to the [official Live Nation event listing](https://www.livenation.co.uk/event/raye-this-symphony-may-contain-hope--london-tickets-edp1703289?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content).

Nevertheless, both incidents illustrate how problems attributed to extraordinary demand and automated activity can result in greater reliance on restricted-access sales, discretionary allocation decisions and later inventory releases.

## Industry Calls for Resale Restrictions While Primary Problems Persist

The RAYE incident is also unfolding as the UK government prepares legislation that would substantially restrict the independent ticket resale market.

The questions raised by this presale go beyond automated registrations: The reported failures occurred within the primary ticketing process, before fans could even complete purchases that might subsequently be resold.

As [TicketNews reported last week](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/10/uk-minister-faster-ticket-resale-crackdown/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), UK Creative Industries Minister Ian Murray recently promised music industry representatives that the government would attempt to accelerate its planned crackdown on ticket resale.

The [proposed legislation](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/putting-fans-first-consultation-on-the-resale-of-live-events-tickets/outcome/government-response-to-the-consultation-on-the-resale-of-live-events-tickets?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) would prohibit resale above the original ticket cost, cap fees charged by resale platforms and impose additional compliance obligations on marketplaces.

Supporters argue that eliminating the opportunity to profit from resale would reduce the financial incentives for professional ticket harvesting, including automated purchasing operations.

But those restrictions would not directly address website failures, malfunctioning queues, unreliable verification processes or the allocation of tickets through restricted presales.

Nor would they impose corresponding restrictions on primary-market pricing, including the premium and demand-based pricing practices available to artists, promoters and their ticketing partners.

The proposal would not make all independent resale platforms illegal, but it would substantially restrict their ability to facilitate transactions at prices determined by buyers and sellers.

Meanwhile, the UK already has legislation specifically targeting certain automated ticket-buying practices.

The [Breaching of Limits on Ticket Sales Regulations 2018](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/ticket-bots-ban-comes-into-force?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) established criminal penalties for using automated software to purchase tickets beyond an event’s permitted limits for financial gain.

Those rules do not necessarily cover the creation of fraudulent presale registrations, but they demonstrate that automated ticket acquisition and the separate question of lawful resale pricing are distinct regulatory issues.

For consumers, that distinction matters.

A resale price cap might reduce some incentives for professional ticket harvesting, but it cannot make an overloaded website function, ensure that an authorized customer receives a working presale code or disclose how many tickets have been withheld from public sale.

And when an exclusive primary ticketing system fails, consumers cannot simply take their business to a competing box office offering the same inventory.

RAYE’s team maintains that its intervention was intended to protect fans from scalpers. That intention is not inconsistent with legitimate concerns about the reliability and transparency of the ticketing process itself.

With the general public sale still approaching, the immediate test will be whether remaining customers can access tickets through a functioning system — and whether organizers can provide a clearer accounting of what actually went wrong.
