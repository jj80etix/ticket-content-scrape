# G! Festival: Inside one of the world’s most remote music festivals

## Summary

IQ profiles G! Festival, a 6,600-capacity non-profit music festival held annually since 2002 in the remote Faroese village of Syðrugøta. The piece explores how the festival, run largely on volunteer labor and municipal/sponsor funding, has become a launchpad for Faroese artists while surviving a punishing logistical environment shaped by fog, flooding winds, and a total lack of local hotel infrastructure.

### Key Points
- G! Festival draws roughly 10-12% of the Faroe Islands' entire population (55,000 total) each year, run by around 1,500 volunteers.
- As a non-profit, the festival depends on municipal funding, private sponsors, and vendor fees, and can lose money in bad-weather years.
- Around 1,000 lower-priced tickets typically sell before Christmas, ahead of lineup announcements, with weather driving later, pricier purchases.
- With no hotels on the island, roughly 140 artists and visitors are housed via homestays in private homes and schools each year.
- The festival has helped launch international careers for Faroese acts (e.g. Eivør, Joe & the Shitboys) and coincided with the 2019 founding of the Faroes Music Export office.

## Transcript

With limited resources and temperamental weather on the Faroese village of Syðrugøta, its 6,600-capacity non-profit festival is a feat of logistics and community spirit

![G! Festival](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/G-Festival-©-Lasse-Lagoni.png)

G! Festival © Lasse Lagoni

For an archipelago of just 55,000, the Faroe Islands are producing more than their fair share of talent. Teitur, Eivør, Greta Svabo Bech and Joe & the Shitboys are among the best-known exports of the 18 islands, located between Iceland and Norway in the North Atlantic Ocean.

Bech collaborated with Deadmau5 on the Grammy-nominated Raise Your Weapon, while the Shitboys have been continuously championed as the “best band in the world” on Iggy Pop’s radio show and at his concerts.

A key catalyst in the development and export of Faroese artists is G! Festival, the archipelago’s first outdoor music event, which has been held annually in July since 2002.

The non-profit festival, which aims for an even split of domestic and international artists, was intended to be a launchpad for Faroese artists with international ambitions.

“Now, young musicians here don’t have to ask whether it’s possible to make it internationally,” says Høgni Lisberg, booker of the festival and drummer of former band Clickhaze, who were pioneering figures in the archipelago’s cultural development.

It was Clickhaze members Sólarn Solmunde and Jón Tyril who founded G! Festival in 2002, naming the event after its location in Gøta (officially known as Syðrugøta). Situated on the second-largest of the Faroe Islands, the village of Gøta is home to just 500 people, one music venue and precisely zero hotels or supermarkets.

> “Nonprofit is the only way because this concept doesn’t work economically”

With thousands of people descending on the tiny hamlet for the festival, its production is a feat of logistics and community spirit.

“You would be amazed at all the hands that are working to make this festival possible,” Lisberg tells *IQ*.

With the festival being a non-profit entity, locals are happy to pitch in. Around 1,500 volunteers pitch in to build stages on the beach, in a children’s playground and other unconventional spaces and ultimately 10–12% of the Faroese population attends.

“Nonprofit is the only way because this concept doesn’t work economically,” explains G! Festival managing director Sigvör Laksá. “There’s some money from the municipality and private sponsors who want the festival to happen, and vendors pay to set up at the festival, but we always have to worry if there’s a plus or a minus.”

The bottom line can be massively affected by the weather, which is infamously misty and rainy in the Faroe Islands, adds Laksá.

“If it were three days of rain, then we would lose money,” she continues. “People wait to buy tickets to see if the weather is good, which is not always a bad thing because those are the expensive tickets. We usually sell around 1,000 cheaper tickets before Christmas and before the lineup has been announced.”

> “If bands want an adventure, they won’t be disappointed”

Over the course of 20-odd years, the festival has dealt with a number of weather-related challenges. With the main stage of the festival on the beach, it is subject to flooding and strong winds. Fog, however, has proved to be the most nefarious weather for the festival, occasionally preventing planes from landing and causing major artist cancellations.

“We are probably the best festival at rescheduling events and getting new bands on the programme on short notice,” laughs Laksá. “It can be very stressful, but you get used to it in the Faroe Islands. It’s part of the game, watching the beach and the weather and thinking about Plan B.”

When it’s not too extreme, the dramatic weather and rugged terrain are often a key part of the festival’s draw for artists.

“Our fees are low and it’s a hassle to get here, but if bands want an adventure, they won’t be disappointed,” comments Laksá.

Part of the adventure is homestays. With no hotels on the island, the festival arranges private accommodation in homes and schools for around 140 artists and visitors each year.

“I’ve been booking the festival for three years and at first, I did not like asking artists to come and play and then sleep in a school, for example,” says Hogni. “But it turns out that people always leave with a good feeling – we very rarely have complaints. G! Festival has a good reputation and it’s been around for a while. If you look at the lineups through the years, it’s quite impressive. A lot of great acts have been here.”

> “We want to showcase the quality of the Faroese music industry to the world”

Among the bigger names that have performed at the festival are Faithless, Sister Sledge, and Fatboy Slim, but it’s not what the festival specialises in.

“We have a history of booking names that aren’t famous yet, and we respect the trust people put in us to do so,” says Lisberg. “We’re not so interested in streaming numbers; G! Festival is about the power of live music.”

If the consecutive sell-out editions are anything to go by, that model is working well. The launch of the Faroes Music Export office in 2019 was another positive indicator of the archipelago’s now-buoyant ecosystem of talent.

Speaking about the collaboration between the two entities, Glenn Larsen, head of the Faroes Music Export office, tells *IQ*: “The idea is simple: we want to showcase the quality of the Faroese music industry to the world, but we want to do it here at home. Experiencing Faroese artists in their own environment will always be more powerful than seeing them at an international showcase festival abroad. This approach has already produced significant results, creating new opportunities and opening many doors for Faroese artists and the wider music industry.”
