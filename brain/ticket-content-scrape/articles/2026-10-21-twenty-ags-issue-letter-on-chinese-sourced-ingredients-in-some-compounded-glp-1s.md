# Twenty AGs Issue Letter on Chinese-Sourced Ingredients in Some Compounded GLP-1s and Peptides

## Summary

Twenty state attorneys general, led by Alabama AG Steve Marshall, sent a letter to Secretary of State and National Security Adviser Marco Rubio raising national-security concerns about Chinese-sourced ingredients in compounded GLP-1 drugs and peptides. The letter builds on a 2025 executive order treating active pharmaceutical ingredients as a strategic vulnerability and asks for better federal intelligence-sharing on foreign supply chains. The subject matter (pharmaceutical supply chains) has no connection to ticketing.

### Key Points
- Letter dated Sept. 17, 2026; addressed to Secretary Rubio.
- Flags semaglutide, tirzepatide, and (investigational) retatrutide supply chains as a national-security concern.
- Criticizes FDA's "Green List" under Import Alert 66-80 as a potential "laundering channel" for unverified foreign ingredients.
- Seeks improved federal-state information sharing on supply-chain sourcing and verification.

On September 17, 2026, 20 state attorneys general (AGs) led by Alabama AG Steve Marshall issued a letter to the federal government concerning Chinese-sourced ingredients in compounded GLP-1 drugs and peptides. Addressing their letter to Secretary of State and National Security Adviser Marco Rubio, the AGs characterize these supply chain issues as a national security concern, criticize a 2025 Food and Drug Administration (FDA) policy for exacerbating the problem, and seek federal intelligence on foreign GLP-1 and peptide supply chains and improved information sharing between federal and state partners.

### Background

The letter builds on President Donald Trump’s August 2025 executive order on the Strategic Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients Reserve, which treats active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), the components that give a drug its therapeutic effect, as a strategic national security vulnerability. The AGs identify semaglutide, tirzepatide, and retatrutide. Retatrutide is investigational and not FDA approved.

The vulnerability alleged by the AGs concerns FDA’s “Green List” under Import Alert 66-80, which identifies manufacturers and products FDA has reviewed and exempted from detention without physical examination under that alert. Listed shipments remain subject to FDA examination at the border. The AGs warn that, without real-time verification of source, chain of custody, and downstream use, the list can become a “laundering channel.”

### The Harbin Warning Letter

The AGs cite an FDA warning letter issued to Harbin Jixianglong Biotech Co., Ltd., a Chinese company on the Green List. According to the AGs, an on-site inspection months after Harbin’s placement on the Green List found that it had purchased knock-off semaglutide API from a non-Green List facility not registered with FDA, repackaged and relabeled it under Harbin’s name, changed manufacturing and retest dates, and distributed the API to the United States.

The letter contends that the breach was caught by inspection, noting that detecting the conduct in the Harbin matter required physical presence at the facility. The AGs compare the pattern to the 2007 heparin contamination crisis, in which contaminated bulk material sourced in China was incorporated into finished product in the U.S., resulting in several deaths and hundreds of adverse reactions worldwide. They cite it as evidence that a complex global supply chain is vulnerable to sophisticated adulteration.

### The Fentanyl Nexus

The letter’s second concern rests on reporting rather than on an agency finding. The AGs cite reporting that Chinese manufacturers tied to fentanyl precursor supply chains have moved into the U.S. market for cosmetic and weight-loss peptides.

The AGs ask whether those entities also supply GLP-1 ingredients, related peptides, or key starting materials to the U.S. compounding market. The letter does not establish such a connection.

### Why It Matters

**The letter accompanies active state enforcement**. In January 2026, Marshall’s office announced a settlement with Aurora IV and Wellness, a clinic accused of administering research-grade tirzepatide and semaglutide to patients while advertising the drugs as pharmaceutical-grade. Other AGs have also been active in this space, and their enforcement efforts transcend party lines. State consumer protection statutes may reach misrepresentations about what a product is and where it came from, and they operate independently of federal regulatory authorities.

**The request signals coordinated state interest.** The AGs seek federal information on foreign GLP-1 and peptide supply chains and warning signs states should watch for. Although the letter does not announce an enforcement initiative, it identifies an area businesses should monitor.

**The supply-chain concern extends beyond compounded drugs.** Manufacturers of branded and generic drugs also rely on foreign-sourced APIs. In 2019, FDA told Congress that 72% of API manufacturing facilities supplying the U.S. market were overseas, including 13% in China. The heparin crisis cited by the AGs involved Baxter’s FDA-approved product, not a compounded drug. Although the regulatory frameworks differ, the letter raises a broader question: whether concerns about foreign API integrity will prompt comparable scrutiny of supply chains for FDA-approved drugs.
