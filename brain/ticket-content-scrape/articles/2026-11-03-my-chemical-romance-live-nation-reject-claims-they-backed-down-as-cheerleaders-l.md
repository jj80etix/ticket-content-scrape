# My Chemical Romance, Live Nation Reject Claims They Backed Down as Cheerleaders Lawsuit Ends

## Summary

The Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders' trademark lawsuit against My Chemical Romance and Live Nation over look-alike costumes used at three Hollywood Bowl shows has been dismissed, but the parties dispute whether the band backed down. My Chemical Romance's manager says the costumes were always planned for only three specific October dates, while the cheerleaders' attorney maintains the band "waved the white flag."

### Key Points
- The dispute centered on backup dancers' cheerleader-style costumes during a staged "gunfight" sequence tied to a 20th-anniversary Black Parade storyline.
- Live Nation moved the case to federal court and argued the production was protected artistic/First Amendment expression, not trademark infringement.
- The dismissal leaves the underlying trademark and First Amendment questions unresolved by a court.
- Cheerleaders' corporate owner, Pro Silver Star Ltd., had sought an injunction and damages before withdrawing the suit.

The trademark lawsuit filed by the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders against My Chemical Romance and Live Nation has been dismissed, but the band and concert promoter are disputing claims that they agreed to change their performances in response to the legal action.

Pro Silver Star Ltd., the corporate owner of the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders, filed dismissal papers Monday, November 2, ending a dispute over the use of look-alike cheerleader uniforms during three My Chemical Romance concerts at the Hollywood Bowl in Los Angeles.

The dismissal comes after reports suggested that the band had agreed to stop using the costumes to resolve the lawsuit. However, newly disclosed court filings indicate that My Chemical Romance had already planned to retire the costumes before the legal dispute began.

In a sworn declaration filed October 29, My Chemical Romance manager Brian Schechter said the costumes were exclusively intended for three performances on October 21, 23 and 24, which concluded the theatrical storyline developed during the band’s 2025 and 2026 tours celebrating the 20th anniversary of *The Black Parade*.

“From the outset, the Finale — with its elaborate sets, full cast, and resolution of the story told during the 2025 and 2026 tours — was planned to be performed only on Oct. 21, 23, and 24, 2026, and on no other date,” Schechter wrote, according to [Billboard’s November 2 reporting](https://billboard.com/pro/my-chemical-romance-cowboys-cheerleaders-lawsuit-why-ended?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content).

Schechter said those decisions were made in 2025, nearly a year before he learned that the cheerleaders objected to the costumes. The band’s October 30 and 31 Hollywood Bowl concerts were always intended to feature a different production without the disputed costumes, he said.

The declaration challenges the characterization of the lawsuit’s initial resolution as a victory for the cheerleaders, whose attorneys had sought an emergency restraining order preventing further use of the uniforms.

Alex Spiro, the attorney representing the Cowboys Cheerleaders, rejected Schechter’s explanation in comments to Billboard.

“Sure they were,” Spiro said of the band’s claim that the costumes were already being retired. “They waved the white flag.”

The dispute originated during a sequence of Hollywood Bowl performances in which backup dancers wore blue-and-white uniforms resembling those worn by the Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders. During the theatrical production, the performers participated in a simulated gunfight involving prop rifles.

As [TicketNews previously reported](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/10/dallas-cowboys-cheerleaders-sue-my-chemical-romance-live-nation/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), Pro Silver Star alleged that the costumes infringed its protected trademarks and trade dress, created confusion about the cheerleaders’ involvement and damaged the organization’s reputation.

The company sought an injunction preventing further use of the costumes, along with unspecified monetary damages.

The initial request for a temporary restraining order was withdrawn from consideration after the parties communicated about the remaining performances. An entry in the Texas state court docket indicated that an agreement had been reached, prompting reports that My Chemical Romance had conceded to the cheerleaders’ demands.

However, the band’s attorney, David Byrnes, had informed the cheerleaders’ legal team that the relevant performances were already finished and that the costumes would not appear at future concerts.

Live Nation subsequently moved the litigation to federal court and filed arguments defending the stage production as artistic expression protected by the First Amendment.

“This is a trademark case about a musical group’s artistic expression and the First Amendment,” the promoter’s attorneys argued, contending that the costumes were used as recognizable elements of a theatrical parody rather than to suggest an official association with the cheerleaders.

In a separate filing Monday, Live Nation disputed assertions that the defendants had agreed to modify their performances to resolve the lawsuit.

According to [Rolling Stone’s reporting on the filing](https://au.rollingstone.com/music/music-news/my-chemical-romance-dallas-cowboys-cheerleaders-uniforms-102963/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), Live Nation maintained that neither it nor My Chemical Romance had agreed to alter any planned shows or conceded that the performances infringed the cheerleaders’ intellectual property rights.

The promoter also challenged the suggestion that the decision to stop using the costumes resulted from negotiations with Pro Silver Star, maintaining that the relevant portion of the production had concluded as originally scheduled.

Although the lawsuit has now ended, the dismissal does not establish whether the costumes constituted trademark infringement or whether their use was protected artistic expression.

Those questions had been central to the dispute, with Pro Silver Star arguing that the unauthorized use of its distinctive uniforms created misleading associations with its brand, while Live Nation maintained that the imagery was part of a satirical theatrical production.

The case concluded without a court ruling on those competing arguments, leaving both the underlying trademark claims and the defendants’ First Amendment defense unresolved.
