# Rising ticket prices and digital ticketing risk shutting out football’s traditional fans, says university research

## Summary

New research from Aston University (Dr. Danny Fitzpatrick) finds that rising ticket prices and increasingly restrictive digital ticketing systems are alienating long-standing English football supporters and eroding the sport's intergenerational fan culture. The study traces the shift to the Premier League's 1992 launch, noting ticket prices have risen roughly 800% between 1990 and 2025, with 19 of 20 Premier League clubs raising prices ahead of the 2024-25 season (some by up to 28%) and reduced concessions for younger and older fans. Supporter interviews also flagged concern that clubs are moving away from traditional season-ticket models to court higher-spending "tourist" fans, and that automated fraud/touting detection in digital ticketing systems is sometimes misfiring against legitimate supporters, as in a recent Manchester United case.

### Key Points
- UK football ticket prices are estimated to have risen ~800% from 1990 to 2025; 19 of 20 Premier League clubs raised prices ahead of 2024-25, with some increases as high as 28%.
- Concessionary pricing for under-18s and over-65s has been reduced or eliminated at some clubs, with one child's season ticket reported to have more than doubled in price.
- Supporters suspect clubs are deliberately pricing out traditional/local fans in favor of higher-spending international "tourist" fans, partly by undermining the season-ticket model.
- Digital ticketing has reduced ticket touting but automated detection systems are flagged as sometimes unable to distinguish legitimate account/ticket management from resale violations — cited example: Manchester United supporters facing a potential season-ticket ban under review.
- The Premier League's existing £30 away-ticket price cap is noted as a rare successful fan-campaign outcome.
- Average Premier League matchday crowd age was last measured at 41 (2012-13 season, most recent published demographic data).

### Highlights
- Ticket prices up ~800% (1990-2025); 19/20 Premier League clubs raised prices for 2024-25, up to 28% increases.
- Reduced/eliminated concessionary pricing for youth and senior supporters, including a season ticket for a child more than doubling in price.
- Automated digital-ticketing fraud/touting detection reportedly misidentifying legitimate supporter account activity as violations (Manchester United case under review).
- Existing precedent for successful patron-side pricing pushback: the £30 Premier League away-ticket cap.

### Industry Problem
Affordability and increasingly restrictive digital-only ticketing/account-verification systems are pushing traditional, loyal patrons out of the market in favor of higher-spending "tourist" buyers, while automated anti-touting enforcement risks false positives against legitimate fans — a direct patron-trust and access pain point (Q2), compounded by digital-entry/concession-flow friction that disproportionately affects younger and older ticket buyers (Q16).

### Proposed Solution
The article does not describe a vendor solution, only fan grievances and one prior successful pricing intervention (the away-ticket price cap); for a primary ticketing platform this signals value in building more transparent, appealable digital-entry and anti-fraud flows (e.g., clearer distinction between legitimate account/ticket transfers and touting, accessible concession-tier pricing options) and monitoring how clubs and leagues respond to mounting patron backlash over pricing and digital restrictions.

TheTicketingBusiness News

**Research from Aston University, in the UK city of Birmingham, has shown that affordability pressures and digital ticketing restrictions are “alienating long-standing supporters and threading the intergenerational culture at the heart of English football”.**

According to Dr Danny Fitzpatrick, an expert in sport governance and football fan culture, escalating ticket prices, reduced concessions for younger and older supporters, and increasingly restrictive digital ticketing systems are creating barriers for traditional fans.

Dr Fitzpatrick added that commercial pressures within the modern game are also reshaping access to live football, with concerns centred on affordability, inclusion and the growing power of clubs to control supporter access through digital platforms.

**A ‘significant shift’**

The research noted that while football has always involved an admission charge, “clubs historically adopted a relatively conservative approach to ticket pricing, prioritising loyal local support over maximising revenue”. However, the Premier League’s launch in 1992 marked a “significant shift”.

Since then, ticket prices have soared, rising by an estimated 800% between 1990 and 2025. The economics of attending live football have changed thanks to demand-based pricing models, premium matchday experience and hospitality packages.

Ahead of the 2024-25 season, 19 of the Premier League’s 20 clubs increased ticket prices – with some increases jumping by as much as 28%. The research noted that in one case, the cost of a child’s season ticket more than doubled ahead of the 2024-25 campaign.

Research interviews with supporter groups in the 2024-25 season found widespread concerns that clubs are increasingly prioritising short-term revenue generation over maintaining long-standing, community-based support. The research also highlighted concerns over the reduction of concessionary pricing for younger and older supporters.

Those interviewed for the study also argued that rising prices and reduced concessions risked altering the demographic make-up of football crowds, making it harder for younger generations to develop lifelong connections with clubs.

However, there has been previous success with fan campaigns, with the [£30 (€35/$40) cap](https://www.theticketingbusiness.com/2025/06/premier-league-clubs-agree-to-extend-30-away-cap/) on away tickets in the Premier League.

**A move away from traditional season ticket models?**

According to the supporter interviews, there is a fear that clubs are seeking to move away from traditional season ticket models in favour of attracting higher-spending domestic and international visitors.

Some fans expressed general suspicion that clubs were colluding to adopt similar ticketing policies, in order to minimise reputational damage. This suspicion was reinforced by the decision by some clubs to diminish the established concessionary prices for younger (such as under-18s and students) and senior (over-65) supporters.

The last time the Premier League published data on its fan demographic in 2012-13, the average age of the matchday crowd was 41.

Fans believe that clubs are actively seeking to undermine the season ticket model through prohibitive pricing to free up seats for more lucrative overseas ‘tourist fans’ who are willing to pay higher prices and spend more money on merchandise.

Supporters united in protest, calling on clubs to [‘Stop Exploiting Loyalty’](https://www.theticketingbusiness.com/2024/10/stopexploitingloyalty-protests-planned-for-premier-league-games/) and reverse ticketing policies that would undermine long-standing community ties and make football less accessible.

**The fight against touts**

Research also found that while the introduction of digital ticketing systems have helped to cut down on tickets being sold at heavily inflated prices by touts somewhat, supporters may be inadvertently affected by these measures.

An example of this is the recent reports of Manchester United supporters [being suspected](https://www.theticketingbusiness.com/2026/07/manchester-united-fans-face-potential-season-ticket-ban/) of violating the club’s ticketing terms and conditions during a review into touting and deceptive practices.

Supporters were concerned that automated detection systems may struggle to distinguish between illegal resale activity and legitimate account management.

Dr Fitzpatrick’s research concluded that while football has traditionally balanced commercial realities with supporter interest, the current trajectory risks “creating a game that remains globally successful but increasingly disconnected from its traditional fanbase”.

“Football’s strength has always come from the commitment of generations of supporters who pass their club loyalties from parent to child,” said Dr Fitzpatrick.

“If rising prices and digital barriers continue to push local fans to the margins, the game risks losing the very communities, traditions and atmosphere that made it the world’s most popular sport.

“Growing international investment, changing ownership models and increased pressure to maximise revenues have only intensified concerns about whether clubs continue to prioritise the communities that helped build their success.”
