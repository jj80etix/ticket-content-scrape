# Quebec Resale Law Lets Event Rights-Holders Decide Who Can Sell Above Price Cap

## Summary

Quebec's expanded ticket-resale law took effect Sept. 12, generally capping resale at the price set by the event producer's authorized vendor — but preserving an exception letting producers and presenters grant written consent for resale above that cap, with no requirement to offer the same permission to competing marketplaces. Critics, including the Ticket Policy Forum representing StubHub, SeatGeek, Vivid Seats and others, warn the structure could let Ticketmaster-affiliated rights-holders authorize above-cap resale on Ticketmaster while leaving independent marketplaces bound by the cap — while Ticketmaster's own dynamic/platinum primary pricing remains entirely outside the cap. The law also adds new resale-disclosure requirements (reseller identity, itemized fees, prior owner name) that took effect the same day.

### Key Points
- Effective Sept. 12, 2026: resale generally capped at the authorized vendor's announced price unless the producer/presenter consents in writing to an exception.
- No statutory requirement that a resale exception granted to one marketplace be offered to competitors on equal terms.
- Ticket Policy Forum's Brian Berry argues this could entrench Ticketmaster's primary-market power rather than protect consumers; Ticketmaster had not responded to TicketNews as of publication.
- New disclosure rules require resale platforms to show buyers the authorized price, reseller identity/type, and an itemized fee breakdown before purchase.
- An earlier proposal to ban ticket-transfer fees outright was dropped; replaced with a disclosure/itemization requirement instead.
- Dynamic/platinum primary pricing (set by rights-holders) remains fully uncapped — the law only constrains secondary-market pricing.

### Highlights
- Law effective Sept. 12, 2026 — resale generally capped at the authorized seller's price, with a producer-consent exception carved out.
- No equal-access requirement for the resale exception — a potential structural advantage for rights-holder-preferred marketplaces.
- New buyer-facing disclosure mandates (reseller identity, itemized fees, resale flag) also took effect Sept. 12.

### Industry Problem
Resale price caps paired with a producer-controlled exception can let primary-market power extend into the secondary market if the exception is granted selectively to affiliated or preferred resale partners, disadvantaging independent marketplaces and potentially consumers' ability to recover ticket value.

### Proposed Solution
Item-described solution: consumer disclosure requirements (itemized fees, resale flag, authorized price) are the law's main direct remedy. For an independent primary platform, the watch-item is how rights-holders in Quebec actually use the consent exception — Etix should track whether equivalent-access language becomes a live policy debate, and be positioned to support transparent, non-exclusive resale disclosure practices if operating in that market.

Quebec’s expanded ticket resale law takes effect Saturday, [imposing a broad new set of restrictions on secondary ticket sales](https://www.publicationsduquebec.gouv.qc.ca/fileadmin/gazette/pdf_encrypte/lois_reglements/2026A/108262.pdf) while preserving a [significant exception](https://www.ticketnews.com/2025/12/quebec-unveils-plans-for-sweeping-ticket-resale-restrictions-despite-consumer-risks/) that lets event rights-holders decide when — and potentially through whom — tickets may be resold above the province’s price cap.

Beginning Sept. 12, a ticket generally cannot be resold to a Quebec consumer for more than the price announced by the vendor authorized by the event producer. But the law provides an exception when the producer and, where applicable, the event presenter give prior written consent, the resale complies with an agreement with those parties and the maximum resale price set by the producer is disclosed to the buyer.

In other words, above-cap resale is not actually prohibited across the board. It can continue when the event rights-holder permits it.

The law does not appear to require producers or presenters to offer that permission to competing resale outlets on equal terms. That creates an unusual competitive structure in which a rights-holder can potentially permit one reseller or marketplace to participate in transactions that another marketplace is legally barred from facilitating.

Quebec’s approach adds another layer: instead of applying even an arguably flawed price restriction neutrally, it leaves event producers with substantial power over who can operate outside it.

The producer-controlled exception itself is not entirely new.

Quebec’s previous Consumer Protection Act already prohibited merchants from reselling tickets above the price announced by the authorized seller unless the event producer approved the higher-priced resale and the merchant complied with an agreement with the producer.

Bill 10 substantially expands the system around that provision.

The new version replaces the previous restriction on a “merchant” with one applying to “no person,” adds the consent of the event presenter where applicable and explicitly brings transactional digital marketplaces into the compliance regime. A platform that allows a third party to resell a ticket and receives the payment is deemed to be participating in the resale for several provisions of the law and must ensure the seller possesses or controls the ticket.

That makes the pre-existing rights-holder waiver more consequential.

A producer can simply refuse to permit resale above the authorized seller’s price. But if it does want higher-priced resale to occur, the statute leaves the producer and presenter in control of the agreement needed to make that transaction legal.

Nothing in the provision establishes a right for another compliant marketplace to demand equivalent treatment.

That could become particularly important in a ticketing market where the promoter, event producer, venue operator, primary ticketing provider and resale marketplace can have overlapping commercial relationships.

A rights-holder could, for example, authorize higher-priced resale under an agreement involving a preferred ticketing partner while leaving an unaffiliated resale marketplace subject to the statutory ceiling. The independent marketplace would not merely be competing for buyers and sellers; its ability to facilitate the same range of lawful transactions could depend on obtaining permission from the parties controlling the event itself.

There is no evidence at this point that Ticketmaster or any Quebec event rights-holder plans to use the law that way.

TicketNews specifically asked Ticketmaster whether it intends to enable above-price resale in Quebec when producers or presenters provide the authorization contemplated by the statute, along with questions about how those agreements would operate. Ticketmaster had not responded as of publication.

Ticketmaster’s publicly available seller guidance does say that users may be able to list tickets above or below face value depending on the resale terms established by the Event Organizer and applicable local resale laws. It also says artists, teams and venues decide whether resale is available for their events on Ticketmaster at all.

That makes the practical application of Quebec’s rights-holder exception one of the most important questions left unanswered as the law takes effect.

## Critics See a Route to Greater Ticketmaster Power

StubHub declined to offer a company comment when contacted by TicketNews and instead referred the inquiry to Brian Berry, executive director of Ticket Policy Forum.

The trade group represents major ticket marketplaces including StubHub, SeatGeek, Vivid Seats, TickPick, Gametime and Event Tickets Center and has consistently opposed resale price controls that it argues strengthen dominant primary ticket sellers.

“Regulated resale marketplaces already provide safe, secure places to buy and sell tickets,” Berry told TicketNews.

“The real issues Quebec consumers should be worried about are the massive carveouts in this law that will continue to empower Ticketmaster to increase prices via dynamic and platinum pricing, reduce access to tickets for live events on regulated platforms, and fuel fraud on the dark corners of the web.

“If the Quebec government wants to improve the live event experience, they should repeal this misguided law and pass policies that protect venues, artists, and fans from Ticketmaster’s primary ticketing monopoly.”

Berry’s criticism points to two different asymmetries in the Quebec framework.

The first is common to most resale price caps: the government limits what a ticket owner can charge after purchasing a ticket while leaving the primary rights-holder free to establish — and change — the original price.

Ticketmaster itself says artists, promoters, teams and leagues decide how standard tickets are priced on its marketplace, how many are initially offered and when they are put on sale. It also says those parties can change the face-value price over time.

Dynamic and premium primary pricing therefore remains outside Quebec’s resale ceiling.

The second concern is more particular to Quebec: the same event rights-holder whose primary pricing is unrestricted can also authorize exceptions to the secondary-market restriction.

Ticket Policy Forum argues that structure could disproportionately benefit Ticketmaster if rights-holders working with the company permit higher-priced resale through Ticketmaster’s own marketplace while competing platforms remain constrained by the cap.

That outcome is not required by the statute, and TicketNews has not established that Ticketmaster or any producer intends to adopt such an arrangement. The concern comes from what the law permits: rights-holders control access to the exception, with no apparent requirement that an authorization provided to one reseller also be available to another.

Berry has made a similar argument against resale caps elsewhere this year, including warning Quebec lawmakers that restrictions aimed largely at secondary marketplaces could reinforce the position of vertically integrated companies and reduce consumer choice.

The Sept. 12 changes contain a number of more straightforward consumer-disclosure requirements as well.

Dedicated resale platforms must prominently tell consumers that they are on a resale marketplace and that tickets could be available for less through the vendor authorized by the producer. Platforms carrying both primary and secondary tickets must disclose that some of the inventory shown is being resold.

Before a resale is completed, consumers must be shown the identity of the authorized vendor, its announced price, the fact that the ticket is a resale, the seat or location and the name of the ticket’s last owner. Resellers must also provide a breakdown of the total price showing the value of the ticket when issued and the nature of the fees being charged.

Ticketmaster has already implemented at least one visible change. Its Canadian help pages say that beginning Sept. 8, individual resale listings for Quebec events display the seller’s first and last name, while professional sellers have their company name, contact information and location shown to buyers.

The final law is also somewhat less restrictive than the legislation Quebec initially proposed.

The original Bill 10 included an outright prohibition on charging fees to transfer a ticket. That provision was removed during the legislative process and replaced by the requirement that resale fees be disclosed and itemized.

The legislation passed the National Assembly 102-1 on June 11, received assent the following day and takes effect Sept. 12, with limited exceptions.

For resale marketplaces, the next question is no longer what the statute says but how rights-holders use the authority it gives them.

A universal resale price cap would bring its own substantial consumer and competition problems. Quebec has instead created a system in which a government-imposed limit exists alongside a private escape hatch controlled by the event producer.

If those exemptions eventually become concentrated among marketplaces affiliated with or preferred by the companies controlling primary ticket distribution, the province’s effort to restrict the secondary market could have the perverse effect of extending primary-market power further into resale.

That is the part of the law worth watching after Saturday.
