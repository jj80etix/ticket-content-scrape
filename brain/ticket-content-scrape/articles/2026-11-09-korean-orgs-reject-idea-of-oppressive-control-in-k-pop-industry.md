# Korean orgs reject idea of ‘oppressive control’ in K-pop industry

## Summary

HYBE subsidiary ADOR and the Korea Popmusic Industry Association pushed back on a New York Times investigation into NewJeans' 2022 contracts (signed when members were 14-17), which the Times said omitted minors' rights to education, sleep and health and granted the label indefinite rights to members' name, image, voice and AI-generated likeness. ADOR says a separate youth-protection addendum covers those rights and that AI use requires individual consent.

### Key Points
- KPIA (whose members include HYBE, JYP, SM and YG) says K-pop's training system "should not be judged solely through a Western lens."
- NYT: contracts banned "insincere attitude," romantic relationships, and public criticism of the label.
- ADOR says a National Standard Youth Addendum covers sleep/education/rest rights and that AI-likeness use needs individual consent.
- Labor/contract dispute is specific to K-pop artist development, not ticketing distribution or sales.

## Korean orgs reject idea of ‘oppressive control’ in K-pop industry

A trade body criticised The New York Times' reporting, saying the development system "should not be judged solely through a Western lens"

![NewJeans at Lollapalooza](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/NewJeans-at-Lollapalooza.png)

NewJeans at Lollapalooza

HYBE subsidiary label ADOR and the Korea Popmusic Industry Association (KPIA) have pushed back on *The* *New York Times* reporting around the contracts of K-pop group NewJeans, with the latter saying that the “distinctive” training and development system “should not be belittled as ‘oppressive control”‘.

Trade body KPIA – whose members include K-pop’s Big Four agencies HYBE, JYP Entertainment, SM Entertainment, and YG Entertainment – criticised the report, saying the K-pop development ecosystem has “its own characteristics and competitive strengths, and should not be judged solely through a Western lens.”

The statement was issued in response to *The Times* ‘  [comprehensive review](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2026/11/05/world/asia/kpop-newjeans-contract.html) of the girl group’s contracts signed in 2022, when the members were aged 14 to 17, published on Friday (5 November).

*The Times* reported that the contracts omitted the minors’ rights to education, sleep, rest, and physical and mental health, in a move away from the Korean government’s nonbinding contractual template. The adjustments were ultimately accepted by the performers’ parents after months of negotiations.

In addition, the contracts reportedly banned behaviour like an “insincere attitude”, forbade the members from entering “inappropriate romantic relationships”, and publicly criticising the record label. The agreements also give the label the rights to the performer’s name, image, and voice indefinitely, including through AI-generated representations.

ADOR has pushed back on the reporting, saying: “We regret that confidential documents and litigation materials have been leaked and misused to attack K-pop.”

In a statement issued to *[The Chosun Daily](https://www.chosun.com/english/kpop-culture-en/2026/11/06/G54MOPQKFJHQ5OBMGU4NE2AYIQ/),* the company said all trainees sign a National Standard Youth Addendum Agreement, which “includes protection clauses for personal rights, sleep rights, education rights, rest rights, and remains valid until adulthood”.

“We also designate youth protection officers as required by law. The NewJeans exclusive contract includes fundamental human rights protection clauses, encompassing the contents of the National Standard Youth Addendum,” the company says.

Regarding the AI recreation of the members, ADOR said the contract “includes personal rights protection clauses, and AI content usage requires individual consent. Parents were informed and agreed without objections. Post-contract use of IP by former agencies could violate laws like the Unfair Competition Prevention Act, which the company is aware of and has no intention to breach.”

Additional behaviour clauses in the contracts stipulate that members shall “not engage in acts that damage their dignity as a pop culture artist”, which includes any acts prohibited under law (such as drug use, fraud, gambling) or causing “public controversy” through acts like underage drinking or behaviour “involving racial, regional, or gender discrimination”.

KPIA says the reporting offers a “one-sided and biased assessment” of the industry and conflates one group’s situation with a wider-reaching, systemic issue.

“We regret that *NYT* has taken the controversy surrounding an individual contract and interpreted it as a structural problem affecting the K-pop industry as a whole, without sufficiently acknowledging regulatory progress and the industry’s efforts to improve its own practices,” the association says.

It says the government’s pair of contract templates – the Standard Exclusive Contract for Popular Culture Artists and the Popular Culture and Arts Industry Development Act – establish protections for entertainment companies’ “substantial financial risks” while “safeguarding the rights and interests of artists”.

The association continues: “The rights of minor artists must be protected. At the same time, entertainment companies’ legitimate investments and contractual rights must also be safeguarded.”

“This dedicated approach to artist management and upfront investment represents a distinctive Korean industry ecosystem that should not be belittled as ‘oppressive control.’ It is within this ecosystem that globally celebrated boy groups and girl groups have emerged, and where countless trainees continue to work tirelessly toward their dreams of becoming the next generation of K-pop stars,” KPIA concludes.
