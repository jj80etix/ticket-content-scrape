# What Corteva’s Arkansas AG Settlement Means for Loyalty and Rebate Programs

## Summary

Law firm Troutman Pepper Locke recaps Corteva's $3.1M settlement with the Arkansas AG over pesticide-distributor loyalty/rebate programs alleged to function as de facto exclusive dealing. The piece is a general antitrust-compliance advisory for companies using loyalty/rebate incentive structures — it concerns agricultural distributor programs, not ticketing or consumer pricing, and doesn't substantively inform any tracked research question.

### Key Points
- Corteva paid $3.1M to settle Arkansas AG claims that distributor loyalty thresholds suppressed generic-pesticide competition.
- Case followed a 2022 FTC/multistate-AG action against Corteva and Syngenta on the same theory.
- Advisory's throughline: state AGs are increasingly active, independent antitrust enforcers of loyalty/rebate program structures across industries.

On August 14, 2026, Corteva, Inc. agreed to pay $3.1 million to settle an antitrust lawsuit brought by the Arkansas attorney general (AG) alleging that the pesticide maker’s rebate and loyalty programs functioned as de facto exclusive dealing arrangements that suppressed competition from generic pesticide manufacturers.

### Background and the Arkansas AG’s Claims

The Arkansas lawsuit did not arise in isolation. It was one of several enforcement actions challenging distributor loyalty programs used by Corteva and Syngenta. In September 2022, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and a bipartisan coalition of state AGs filed an enforcement action alleging that the companies paid distributors to limit purchases of competing generic pesticide products. Three months later, Arkansas filed its own separate action asserting similar theories of anticompetitive harm under federal and state law.

The central allegation was that crop input distributors received payments only if they kept their purchases of competing generic pesticides below a certain threshold. According to the Arkansas AG, these loyalty programs functioned as de facto exclusive dealing arrangements by discouraging distributors from purchasing competing generic products. The complaint alleged that these arrangements allowed Corteva to maintain monopoly or market power in several crop-protection markets and resulted in farmers paying inflated prices. In March 2024, the Arkansas AG amended its complaint to expand the scope of the case by adding additional products.

In February 2025, an Arkansas federal judge rejected Corteva and Syngenta’s attempts to have the case dismissed. The court concluded that the complaint adequately alleged that Arkansas consumers paid higher prices for crop-protection products as a result of the challenged loyalty programs and exclusive dealing arrangements.

### Key Takeaways

The Corteva Arkansas settlement carries several implications for companies operating in agricultural input markets and other industries where rebate and loyalty programs are common commercial tools.

- **State AGs are increasingly shaping antitrust enforcement.** This case demonstrates that state AGs are not merely participants in federal antitrust investigations. States can bring their own actions, pursue their own remedies, and continue litigating even when parallel federal actions are pending. State AGs also have procedural tools that can allow them to keep cases in their chosen forums, increasing the likelihood that companies will face overlapping investigations and litigation in multiple jurisdictions.
- **Loyalty, rebate, and market-share incentive programs remain a priority enforcement area.** Although loyalty rebates are often lawful and procompetitive, enforcers continue to examine whether incentive structures effectively discourage distributors from purchasing competing products. Companies with significant market share should carefully evaluate whether rebate structures, loyalty thresholds, market-share incentives, or preferred provider programs could be characterized as de facto exclusive dealing arrangements.
- **Antitrust exposure increasingly extends beyond a single agency or lawsuit.** Corteva faced parallel scrutiny from the FTC, private plaintiffs, and multiple state AGs. The case illustrates how a single business practice can generate simultaneous investigations and litigation across multiple forums. Resolving claims in one proceeding may not eliminate exposure in others, making early antitrust review and risk assessment particularly important.

### Why It Matters

The Corteva settlement reflects continued antitrust scrutiny of loyalty, rebate, and other distributor incentive programs, particularly where those programs can be characterized as discouraging purchases from competing suppliers. While these arrangements often serve legitimate and procompetitive business purposes, enforcers continue to examine whether they function as de facto exclusive dealing arrangements when implemented by firms with significant market power.

Companies that rely on distributor incentives, loyalty rebates, market-share thresholds, or similar programs should periodically review those arrangements through an antitrust lens, particularly in concentrated markets. Early risk assessment and compliance review can help identify potential concerns before they become the subject of government investigations or litigation.
