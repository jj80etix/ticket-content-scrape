# ‘We don’t take this for granted’: How Labyrinth on the Thames is navigating success

## Summary

London's Labyrinth on the Thames festival returns for a second, expanded eight-show edition after selling out nearly 80,000 tickets in its debut year, with founder Nick Castleman describing the operational challenges of staging shows at the UNESCO-listed Old Royal Naval College.

### Key Points
- Grows from six to eight shows across three weekends, July 31-Aug. 16.
- Lineup diversifies beyond dance music with The Kooks and Moby joining Dom Dolla, Peggy Gou and Overmono.
- Heritage-site constraints require sign-off from Historic England and the venue on every production plan.

## ‘We don’t take this for granted’: How Labyrinth on the Thames is navigating success

Ahead of its second edition, co-founder Nick Castleman reveals how the series' historic setting helped power back-to-back sell-outs

![Black Coffee at Labyrinth on the Thames](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Black-Coffee-at-Labyrinth-on-the-Thames.png)

Black Coffee at Labyrinth on the Thames

Ahead of the second year of London’s Labyrinth on the Thames, the festival says it is invested in cultivating “new and different audiences”, with the strategy led by a diversified cross-genre programme for its upcoming sophomore edition.

As it prepares to step beyond the dance music remit of its debut edition last year, founder Nick Castleman dives into what was behind the open-air series’ immediate success, and how it is balancing the rewards and restrictions of a UNESCO World Heritage Site, in an in-depth conversation with *IQ.*

The open-air series, staged at the scenic 17th-century Old Royal Naval College on the banks of the Thames, returns this week, with eight shows lined up across the next three weekends from 31 July-16 August. This year will feature a mix of stars, with The Kooks and Moby lined up next to Dom Dolla, Peggy Gou, and Overmono.

Created by Labyrinth Events, the series debuted last year with six shows, all of which quickly sold out. Growing to eight this year, organisers are celebrating another sell-out success with nearly 80,000 tickets sold, no easy feat in the increasingly crowded outdoor events market in the UK capital city.

The response has exceeded even the team’s expectations, with the inaugural edition establishing the series almost immediately as one of London’s hottest summer tickets.

“The reception to the launch of the concept and the shows themselves was so amazing, and the speed at which tickets sell for the series speaks for itself,” Castleman tells *IQ*. “It is arguably the fastest-selling summer music series in London.”

> “Every single production plan and all of the protection measures have to be signed off by the venue”

“We’ve always had an innate curiosity for taking our events to unique spaces that are not your standard – they could be off the beaten track, beautiful, historic, rarely used, or a space nobody else would ever think to operate at because it seems impossible,” he explains. “It started with empty loft studios we’d found and quickly progressed to Tofte Manor, a 17th-century manor and wellness sanctuary, to disused Edwardian tube stations, then East London churches, street parties, and much more.”

With nearly a decade of experience as a promoter, curator, and producer, Labyrinth is known for its shows in spectacular locations, with the riverside architectural feat no different.

“The setting is dreamlike,” he says. “Behind the stage you have the River Thames and the London skyline, so if you’re a punter, you’re dancing, watching your favourite artist, and looking out across the river and the City of London. It’s magic, particularly as the sun goes down and you can see the skyline lit up behind the stage.”

The debut season’s success extended beyond ticket sales, with industry recognition following shortly afterwards.

“We were nominated for Best Festival of the Year at the *Music Week* Awards, which for a first-year event was a huge accomplishment that we were very proud of. Most of the other nominees had been running for decades,” he says.

> “Protecting the space we’re using is the most important part of the whole production”

But creating and executing concerts at unique and regulated settings comes with its own set of challenges.

“As a Historic England site and a venue of OUV \[Outstanding Universal Value\], protecting the site must be at the very forefront of everything we do when building the show,” he explains. “We must be very careful about weight loadings, when and where we drive plant, protecting the historical features, ensuring all of our suppliers are fully briefed on how they should work on site, no staking into the ground (everything is held down with ballast), and extensive ground protections. Plus, we must provide tenant access for those at Trinity Laban \[Conservatoire\] and the University of Greenwich, which needs to be maintained throughout the build.”

He continues: “Every single production plan and all of the protection measures have to be signed off by the venue and Historic England well ahead of time. We work all year round on this project. Ultimately, we’re very lucky to be able to produce these shows in one of only four UNESCO World Heritage Sites in London and take our role as custodians of the venue extremely seriously. We do not take this for granted – protecting the space we’re using is the most important part of the whole production.”

Securing the venue set the foundation for the vision of the outdoor series, and organisers aspired to have a production that would complement the site while “making a real impact,” Castleman says.

That ambition extended beyond simply staging shows at distinct locations, instead shaping every aspect of the event’s presentation.

“We had a clear vision for the show, and we wanted it to stand out from everything else on the London summer circuit,” he says. “From the way we marketed the concept, to the artists booked, to the production and experience on-site, to all of the post-event hype and coverage – we wanted it to cut through, and we were happy with how that played out.”

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/smush-webp/2026/04/Labyrinth-on-the-Thames-430x269.png.webp)

“We invested huge sums into the open, see-through stage that protects the views of the river, along with the audio and visual production on the site,” he says. “We wanted people to see the production and just say, ‘Wowza, I’ve never seen something like that in London before!’ and it worked.”

With the concept firmly established, the focus has shifted from proving its viability to ensuring its continued evolution. Despite launching as a [dance music-focused event](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2025/01/new-six-day-live-music-series-to-launch-in-london/), this year has seen the integration of other kinds of music with the programming of The Kooks and Moby on the bill, alongside some of dance music’s heavy hitters.

“We book artists who we would love to see play in that setting. If we get excited about the idea of seeing an artist play there, then we’re generally onto a winner,” he says. “We will continue to diversify the musical programme every year, to get new and different audiences to experience the venue and festival itself.”

While not much has changed between the two years, organisers have enhanced the premium offerings of the series, with VIPs gaining access to the site’s Painted Hall, which comprises more than 3,700sqm of Baroque art adorning the room’s walls and ceilings, now complete with a private bar.

> “A festival is a living organism, and there’s always room for tweaks and refinements”

“We want to get better every year. Whether that be through new production ideas, faster speed of service at the bars, improved signage, more engagement with the local community – we just want to do our very best to improve the experience for everyone who touches the festival every year. A festival is a living organism, and there’s always room for tweaks and refinements that make the thing run smoother and smarter,” Castleman says.

That philosophy stretches beyond the three-weekend programme. This year, organisers have expanded their community initiatives, partnering with local charity Greenwich and Bexley Community Hospice, rewards platform Earnt, and running a food drive where donations can be exchanged for tickets and festival perks.

For Castleman, Labyrinth on the Thames’ long-term success is not measured only by what happens on stage or how many fans descend on the iconic site, but by the impact it has on its local community.
