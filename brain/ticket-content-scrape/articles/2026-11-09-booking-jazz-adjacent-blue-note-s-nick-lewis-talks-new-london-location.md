# Booking ‘Jazz Adjacent:’ Blue Note’s Nick Lewis Talks New London Location

## Summary

Pollstar profiles Blue Note London, the jazz club group's 10th global location, which opened in late September in Covent Garden. Music director Nick Lewis discusses the venue's intimate 280-capacity dinner-club format and booking philosophy.

### Key Points
- Blue Note London is the brand's 10th location worldwide, opened late September in Covent Garden/West End.
- 280-capacity room: 250 seated, 30 standing, built in a former Gymbox fitness club space.
- High-end dinner menu and "star table" plaques are signature brand touches shared across Blue Note venues.
- Piece is a venue-profile feature, not industry/ticketing analysis.

Robert Glasper was the grand opening concert at the Blue Note London, on Sept. 23.

*Blue Note Entertainment Group opened its 10th location in London in late September, a plush, blue-bathed dinner club with tables so close to the stage, you can reach out and touch the artist — except that’s not the kind of place Blue Note London is. With its high-end dinner menu, dim bespoke lighting, blue drapery and carpet, wood and leather chairs downstairs, velvet chairs and sofas upstairs and a no-flash preference when it comes to phone cameras, it’s a sophisticated night out, where one can see world-class artists, many of whom play venues much larger than its 280-capacity. The two levels accommodate 250 seated and 30 standing at the back bar.*

*Located in the Covent Garden and West End theatre district of central London, which has heavy foot traffic, at the entryway, outside, in front of a blue velvet rope and branded welcome mat, one is greeted by the head of security, a dapper-dressed lady, Amy, in a black shirt, tie, coat and gloves. The dark blue painted corridor with sconces and gold accents and matched blue carpet is lined with framed photographs of artists who have played the venue around the world, from Duke Ellington to Mos Def.*

*Downstairs, the room is equally dark and ambient, each tabletop centered with a custom Blue Note “star table” plaque signed by the artist, a signature of the brand. There is also a well-stocked gift shop selling everything from vinyl to branded clothing and glasses.*

*Earlier in the evening, Nick Lewis, Blue Note London’s director of music & programming, talked with* Pollstar *at the St Martins Lane Hotel’s Library room. Blue Note London is in the basement, where the space was a Gymbox Fitness Club for the last 10 years and before that a Lumiere Cinema. The jazz club was designed by Julien Legeard and his team at Legeard Studio. They also designed the B-Side upstairs, the 120-capacity space due to open later this year or at the start of 2027. Sound and lighting was built and installed by Amit Peleg, owner of Peltrix. He has overseen and designed the sound systems for Blue Note clubs since 1988.*

*Lewis previously booked Camden’s 1,400-capacity Koko for five years and before that was at Ronnie Scott’s Jazz Club for 15 years, his first job. “I started as a receptionist and then learned every job,” he tells Pollstar. Blue Note London’s opening season kicked off Sept. 23 with Robert Glasper, and has so far included shows by Erykah Badu, Baaba Maal, Moses Yoofee Trio, Nubya Garcia, Hak Baker, Lianne La Havas, Common, Jamie Cullum, Yussef Dayes and Masego. Coming up, Glasper returns for seven nights, including two with special guest Common, and other big gets in November and December such as Slum Village, Digable Planets and Brand New Heavies.*

**Pollstar: Do you know when did they start thinking of opening a Blue Note in London and if they were just waiting for the ideal space to become available?**  
**Nick Lewis:** Steve and Tsion \[Bensusan\], the two brothers, the co-CEOs of Blue Note worldwide, they own and operate the business. They inherited it from their father \[Danny\], who started the New York Blue Note in 1981. There were nine clubs, until now. London is the 10th. They’d been looking to come to London for a number of years, but they never quite found the right site. They’ve been aware of how vibrant our jazz scene is here, and, obviously, it’s a major city, a major market, and this site came up into their view, and they made it happen.

**Do other Blue Notes have two clubs in one?**  
They vary. The newest one before London is LA. That’s been open about a year now and is a basement with a room, too.

**How was opening night? Was everything ready?**  
We opened on the 23rd of September. I could tell you some stories that will give you anxiety \[laughs\]. Let me put it this way, the chairs arrived at 3 p.m. on the day. The painters were still finishing off the painting. There were some railings going up. The sound system was still being tweaked.

**What? On the same day?**  
Same day. This is my third venue opening so I’ve learned to just suspend your anxiety and know that it will be OK. I feel like time expands or contracts to what you’ve been given. I feel like if the opening date would have been next March, we would have been finishing off next March.

**It’s cutting it a little close to tweak and test the sound system.**  
It’s very close. Robert \[Glasper\] was supposed to come in and sound check the day before. He didn’t have time. He came in and did the first two nights for us. He’s our cultural ambassador. On that strand of music programming, he’s our perfect artist because he is a virtuoso jazz musician but mixing modern styles. He’s best friends with Erykah Badu and he MD’ed Lauryn Hill’s album and Kendrick Lamar’s album. Very modern and cutting edge but also has that knowledge of all the jazz chords.

**What was the night like for you, barring all the panicky last-minute stuff? People are outside lined up. You watch them file in. You overhear their remarks on the space. Did you feel like a proud dad?**  
Yeah. We’ve been overwhelmed by the response. There’s so much that’s gone right. People are loving the room. They’re loving the ambience. They’re loving the program. One or two teething problems, but you’re going to get that in any place, so these things are easily fixable. The important thing is the fundamentals are there. So many of the shows are selling out. The feedback we’re getting is fantastic. So yeah, super grateful. It’s been a lot, but it’s been worth it.

**What are the design threads of a Blue Note venue besides the logo?**  
I’ve only been to the New York one, so it’s probably a better question for Steve or Tsion or Alex \[Kurland\], who books LA and has been to Tokyo, Milan, Rio and all the rest of them. There is a common aesthetic. When you see the mock-ups of the room and the aesthetic, they all share a similar decor, similar tables, similar ambience, attention to detail when it comes to the sound and sightlines to the stage. Decent stage, decent back line equipment for the musicians. There’s always a grand piano. Obviously, that’s important for a jazz venue. But in terms of the layouts of the rooms, they can vary wildly, I’m told. The New York original is very long and thin. The LA one has very high ceilings and is a little bit wider.

Sizes can vary. We’re one of the bigger ones. We’re approaching 300. Some of them are more like 200.

**So that’s one of the key things about the Blue Note venue around the world: intimacy and the fact you can do these seated underplay shows with world class artists that usually play venues much bigger than two or three hundred.**  
Absolutely. I think that’s the USP \[unique selling proposition\] and why the brand has done well and is able to expand because, without going too deep on it, I think even more these days, people are looking for more than just standing in a gig and having a warm pint of beer, right? They want to have good service. They want to have good drinks. They want to have good food. They want to have a great ambience. Great experience. And hear some world-class music.

**So they’re buying a ticket and that’s for a table.****Is there also a mandatory minimum order off the menu?**  
No. Funny you ask that, in fact, Steve asked me, “Should we do our $20 minimum like we do in America?” I said, “Doesn’t really work here.” A UK crowd can be a bit cynical sometimes; I don’t mind saying that as a Brit \[*laughs*\]. I think we don’t like to be forced into anything we don’t want to consume. If it’s a soft sell, come in, welcome us, wow us with the ambience and the occasion. And if the menu looks good, we’ll order something. But don’t force us.

**How do ticket prices compare since many of these acts play bigger places?**  
I think we’re pretty reasonable. I’m a lapsed musician; I am very keen to welcome emerging scenes and artists that may not be on the biggest paycheck in the world. And I want it to be accessible for everyone. So, I’m big on trying to keep ticket prices as low as I can. Obviously, that’s difficult sometimes when you get pushed on an artist fee, quite rightly. Some of our acts are arena level. And if they’re willing to come and play here, they might play for a knock-down fee. But we’ve still only got 250 seats to sell. So, you do the math and you try and break even. But I’m making sure there’s enough space in the programme for the 20-to-30-pound tickets USD $27-40\], which are pretty reasonable. That’s similar to what you pay to go to a standing venue these days to watch a mid-level band.

**Since it was built from scratch, is there anything especially modern or cool for the artists in of the dressing rooms?**  
Again, we’re lucky in that Steve and Tsion, this is their 10th club. So, they’ve absolutely got some great experience putting together the perfect room and knowing what artists want.

So when they were designing, they’ve made sure there is probably the biggest dressing room I’ve ever seen. It’s not far off the size of this room \[The Library on main floor of St Martins Lane\]. There’s two of them; there’s one like this and then there’s one above.

Just for your notes, what would we say this room is? 20 x 10 feet, something like that, maybe a bit bigger, 20 by 12. So, for example, tonight \[Oct. 10\] we have Erykah Badu playing, probably our big marquee name in the whole opening season. She will have one of these dressing rooms to herself and the band will have the other one.

**Is there a private entrance?**  
There’s a private entrance. You can enter it without even being seen. So that’s very important. And then she can literally go straight onto stage, open the door straight on.

So it’s all been designed with the artist in mind.

**Have you found that the Blue Note name is so special that when you’re talking to agents or managers that they don’t need much convincing, even if it’s an underplay situation?**  
Yeah. I’m lucky enough to have the history of the Blue Note brand on my side when it came to starting to program the room. There was a lot of, “Oh yeah, we’ve played the other Blue Notes. We know what to expect.” I’ve also tried to couple that with 15, 20 years in the industry where I’ve lived and breathed this jazz industry for that amount of time. I know all the artists. I’m lucky enough to call a lot of them my friends and the agents and the industry behind them, a lot of them are friends as well. So, I had a very small amount of time to get a program together. I called all my best friends and asked them if they were able to do a show and a lot of them came to the party, which I’m super grateful for. So, it’s a little bit of trading on the heritage and the great reputation, but it’s also leaning on my relationships.

**When you are booking, would you be OK for a one-off show or does it have to be more to make sense financially?**  
We have done that very occasionally in the past. The only problem with the single show thing is obviously it’s the business model is set up. Certainly, every act in this opening season is doing two shows in a night, which they’re happy to do. And obviously it makes the figures add up for them.

**Blue Note is considered a jazz club. Your sign, your marquee, actually reads “Jazz Capital of the World.” But Erykah Badu is a fusion of so many styles. What is your booking policy?**  
Yeah, that’s an interesting one. There’s this term now called jazz adjacent, which seems to be in common vernacular now. I take a leaf out of the book of the New York and the LA Blue Notes, which go pretty broad with their program. It’s not just straight-ahead jazz, although they do do a lot of that, and they celebrate the great legends, but they also find space for R&B music and a lot of hip hop music and a lot of pop music with jazzy chords. I’ve taken that approach to my first opening season here, where we’re celebrating a lot of the cool new UK jazz scene, which absolutely takes elements of hip hop and Caribbean rhythms and Afrobeat and all this great music. So, it’s quite a broad brush, but it still has to have the jazz DNA.

**Essentially, they’re all sophisticated.**  
Yeah, we’re not going to book a TikTok pop act.

**Long-running jazz festivals have been doing the same thing for many years now, booking acts on the fringes or not jazz at all. That can irk the purists.**  
Oh, absolutely, you have the jazz police. I don’t mind upsetting the jazz police. The fact is we do plenty of really good jazz music. If we want to put in a pop crossover act that happens to be world class, I don’t see a problem with it.

**The smaller upstairs venue, the B-side, when it opens, that will provide a space for showcases, emerging acts and DJs?**  
The B Side, which is our second venue, it’s a little smaller cap. It’s about 100, 120 with a low ceiling, sort of a club room. We very much want to celebrate the emerging generation in there. So emerging artists, artists coming up. I want to have really cool jam sessions going on in there with jam collectives. I want to have a great Latin night. I want to have some great DJ-led programming. Make it maybe a little bit younger, 10 years younger in general, in terms of the audience and a lower ticket price.

**Are you curating or can unrepresented artists cold reach you?**  
Absolutely. Your job as a curator is you’re reactive and you’re going out as well. So a lot of stuff’s coming your way. A lot of the A-list stuff you have to go out and get. It’s a combination of both. I am super grateful for all the stuff that I get sent all the time. Sometimes, there’s incredible stuff that you’re sent and you have your known tastemakers and the people that you trust in the industry that send you stuff. And there’s the various methods of music discovery these days, whether it’s Spotify or radio or podcasts.

**You came to Blue Note from Koko**, **1,400 capacity. How is programming different?**  
Very different because Koko is mostly a venue for hire. So you’re working with promoters like Live Nation and AEG and all the big ones to allow them to come and hire your room. We did do some of our own in-house promotions, but at the big level of 1,500 capacity, you do need promoters because they have exclusive deals with artists at that level to bring talent in. With Blue Note, I’m going out and organizing all the deals and bringing the talent here.

**So never a promoter at Blue Note?**  
No, no, absolutely not. We do all our deals direct with the artist or the artist’s agent or the manager. We have very close relationships with all the artists, which is great. It’s what I love.

**And before Koko, you spent 15 years at the iconic Ronnie Scott’s. Jazz Club. Is Blue Note its competition?**  
That’s a good question and a few people have mischievously asked that. I don’t think it is. I happen to think it’s a rising tide lifts all boats situation where London is a city on par with New York. New York’s got dozens of jazz clubs. We’ve got one major jazz club. Well, now we’ve got two. We’ve got Ronnie Scott’s and we’ve got Blue Note. I think we do pretty well to keep out of each other’s way.

Their programming is different. I would say 80% of the Blue Note program that I’ve done here, Ronnie Scott’s wouldn’t book. They’re much more traditional, I would say. Not to say they don’t do modern cutting-edge stuff, but in general it’s more straight-ahead jazz. What we’ve done with this opening program is much more broad.

**What do you have on for the rest of the year into next year?**  
The rest of the year, some amazing UK stuff. We’ve got Robert Glasper coming back for seven nights \[Nov. 13–15; 19–22\], with Marsha Ambrosius from Floetry \[14–15\] and Common \[20-21\] joining him. All sorts of stuff.

**And now that you’re at this iconic venue, are there a couple of artists that would be a real get?**  
Dream artists? Yeah. The greatest of all time for me is Stevie Wonder, which sounds insane. However, he has dropped in on the LA Blue Note twice in the last six months. That would be a dream. Lauryn Hill would be a dream. Some of these surviving jazz legends from the heyday. Herbie Hancock, absolute dream. Gary Bartz would be a dream, who is hopefully coming next year.
