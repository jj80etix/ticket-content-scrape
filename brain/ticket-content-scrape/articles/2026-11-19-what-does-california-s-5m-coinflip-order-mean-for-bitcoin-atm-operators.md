# What Does California’s $5M CoinFlip Order Mean for Bitcoin ATM Operators?

## Summary

California's DFPI ordered crypto-ATM operator CoinFlip to pay over $4.8 million in restitution and disgorge $350,000+ in fees after finding it failed to verify kiosk users, catch suspicious activity, or follow the state's $1,000 daily limit, fee cap, and disclosure/receipt rules under the Digital Financial Assets Law. CoinFlip neither admitted nor denied the findings. The piece frames this as California using consumer-protection law to backstop anti-money-laundering gaps that federal AML rules don't cover with victim restitution.

### Key Points
- CoinFlip ordered to pay $4.8M+ restitution and disgorge $350K+ in allegedly excessive fees; consent order, no admission of wrongdoing.
- Violations alleged: failure to verify kiosk users/catch fraud, plus breaches of the $1,000 daily transaction limit, fee cap, and pre-transaction disclosure/receipt requirements.
- Action brought under California's Digital Financial Assets Law (licensing required since July 1, 2026) and its Consumer Financial Protection Law.
- DFPI can weigh this enforcement history, though not as sole basis, when deciding CoinFlip's pending license application.

## Transcript

California ordered CoinFlip, one of the state’s largest crypto ATM companies, to pay more than $4.8 million in restitution to fraud victims and disgorge more than $350,000 in allegedly excessive fees. CoinFlip neither admitted nor denied the findings in the [consent order](https://www.law360.com/articles/2547085/attachments/0).

The order comes after California began requiring crypto businesses to [be licensed](https://dfpi.ca.gov/regulated-industries/digital-financial-assets/digital-financial-assets-law-information-for-kiosk-operators/) under its [Digital Financial Assets Law](https://legiscan.com/CA/text/AB39/id/2834136) on July 1, 2026. The law also sets the kiosk rules described in more detail below, and the [California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation (DFPI)](https://dfpi.ca.gov/press_release/california-recovers-over-4-8-million-for-scam-victims-of-major-crypto-atm-company-coinflip/) can weigh enforcement history like this when it decides on license applications. Here, the findings cannot be the only basis for approving or denying CoinFlip’s license application, but DFPI can still consider them.

### What Triggered the Inquiry?

DFPI alleged that CoinFlip did not verify who was using its kiosks or catch suspicious activity, which facilitated fraudulent activity by bad actors. It also alleged that CoinFlip violated four rules that specifically apply to transactions that occur at a crypto ATM kiosk: the [$1,000 daily limit](https://california.public.law/codes/financial_code_section_3902), the [fee cap](https://california.public.law/codes/financial_code_section_3904), the [pre-transaction disclosure, and the receipt requirements](https://california.public.law/codes/financial_code_section_3905). The DFPI sought restitution on the basis that CoinFlip failed to protect customers from fraud and engaged in unlawful, unfair, deceptive or abusive practices.

### California Is Using Its Consumer Protection Law to Enforce Anti-money Laundering Rules

Federal anti-money laundering rules typically aim to stop illicit finance, not compensate people who are scammed. California is trying to fill that perceived gap. California’s Consumer Financial Protection Law provides that DFPI may treat a violation of any consumer financial law, including the federal anti-money laundering rule, as an unlawful practice and obtain restitution damages for a violation. Here, DFPI sought restitution on the basis that CoinFlip failed to protect customers from fraud and engaged in unlawful, unfair, deceptive, or abusive practices.

### The Online Desk Is Part of the Exposure

Sixteen of the 20 recipients of restitution used CoinFlip’s online trade desk, which the kiosk rules do not cover. DFPI could include these consumers in the restitution because its anti-money laundering findings and the consumer protection law apply to both channels. A compliance program built only around the kiosk rules leaves the online desk exposed.

### Most of These Violations Come Down to How the Kiosk Is Programmed

The kiosk rules must be programmed into the kiosk itself. The software must stop a customer at $1,000 a day, cap fees at the legal limit, and display the required disclosure and print the required receipt on every transaction. The same is true of the anti-money laundering regulations: the software must be programmed to verify who is using the kiosk and to flag suspicious activity, and it has to be tested and updated regularly. Compliance by design — building regulatory requirements and system controls directly into the system architecture — is an important practice for crypto asset businesses, particularly those operating in California.

### Crypto ATM Operators Under Scrutiny

CoinFlip is not the DFPI’s first crypto ATM target. The agency previously shut down the kiosk operator Hermes Bitcoin and fined the operators RockItCoin, Coinme, Inc., and Coinhub. In May, the Missouri attorney general (AG) [sued CoinFlip](https://ago.mo.gov/attorney-general-hanaway-files-suit-against-crypto-atm-network-for-enabling-scams/), alleging that it did not adequately protect consumers from fraud or clearly disclose its fees. [CoinFlip has denied the allegations](https://www.regulatoryoversight.com/2026/05/missouri-ag-files-suit-against-cryptocurrency-atm-operator-over-consumer-protection-issues/).

### Next Steps: Test Your Own Machines the Way a Regulator Would

Operators should test their machines against the four kiosk rules, and test their anti-money laundering controls, before a regulator does:

- **Try to break the daily limit.** Make several purchases at different kiosks on the same day with the same customer, and confirm the system stops at $1,000.
- **Check the fees line by line.** Compare what customers pay against the greater of $5 or 15% of the transaction’s market value, counting direct and indirect charges.
- **Read the disclosure and receipt against the statute.** The disclosure must be clear, conspicuous, and separate. The receipt must show the operator’s name, the dollar amount of the spread, and the exchange used to calculate it.
- **Test the anti-money laundering program for each channel.** Confirm it verifies customer identity, flags suspicious activity, and fits the risks of kiosks and the online desk.
- **Name an owner for** **each control.** A failure should go to a person, not a committee.
