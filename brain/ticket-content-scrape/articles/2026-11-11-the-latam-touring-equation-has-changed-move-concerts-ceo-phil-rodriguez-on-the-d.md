# The LATAM Touring ‘Equation Has Changed’: Move Concerts CEO Phil Rodriguez On The Development Of Central America

## Summary

Pollstar's Q&A with Move Concerts CEO Phil Rodriguez covers Central America's shift from an afterthought to a viable touring-route addition, driven by routing economics and a new wave of venue infrastructure. Costa Rica leads today on market maturity; Guatemala has the biggest upside pending its new arena.

### Key Points
- Costa Rica is the most mature Central American market: strong middle class, proven stadium-level demand (two Bad Bunny shows sold ~115,000 tickets, ~$12M gross)
- Guatemala has the largest population/youngest demographic but has lacked arena-scale infrastructure until now
- Routing economics have shifted: artists already moving between Mexico and Colombia can now add Central American stops cost-effectively
- Biggest remaining constraints are infrastructure, permitting bureaucracy and inconsistent government support across the seven countries

## Transcript

Move Concerts CEO Phil Rodriguez

*Few people understand the complexities and potential of Latin American touring better than Move Concerts CEO Phil Rodriguez.*

*With nearly five decades of experience as a promoter and offices in Miami, Argentina, Brazil, Colombia, Costa Rica, Peru and Puerto Rico, Rodriguez has helped develop the LATAM region by bringing the biggest names in music. The seasoned executive just wrapped up Iron Maiden’s South American and Central American run with a two-night stand at Estadio Nacional Julio Martínez Prádanos in Santiago, Chile, which grossed $5,751,643 off 106,987 tickets across both dates, according to Pollstar Boxoffice. Rodriguez has also worked with a diverse group of artists including Bad Bunny, Karol G, The Rolling Stones, U2, Ed Sheeran, Shakira, Bruno Mars, Katy Perry, Slayer and K4OS.*

*With growing interest from major live entertainment companies like Live Nation and AEG Presents in Latin America, Rodriguez took time to answer* Pollstar *‘s questions about Central America’s potential and what is needed to help develop the region.*

***Pollstar*:****I know you’ve worked just about everywhere in Latin America, Phil, but tell me about your history with Central America. How long have you worked in those markets, and what shows have you promoted there?  
Phil Rodriguez:** My history with Central America goes back more than three decades. We started working in the region in the 1990s through Water Brothers and later Evenpro, which eventually became part of what is today Move Concerts. Andrés Guanipa has been a key part of that history as my partner in the region since 1997, and together we have been developing the Central American markets for close to 30 years.

During that time, we have promoted hundreds of shows across Costa Rica, Guatemala, El Salvador, Panama and other markets in the region, working with artists across virtually every genre. The list includes artists such as Elton John, Bon Jovi, Iron Maiden, Green Day, Foo Fighters, Guns N’ Roses, Aerosmith, Metallica, Red Hot Chili Peppers, Sting, Shakira, Ricky Martin, Luis Miguel, Juan Luis Guerra, Alejandro Sanz, Bad Bunny, Karol G, Ed Sheeran and many others.

What is important is that we didn’t just come into Central America when the international touring business became attractive. We have been there for decades, through very different economic cycles and at a time when bringing major international productions into these countries was considerably more complicated than it is today.

**The region is relatively small with seven countries. In your view, which markets are leading the charge in live business, and what advantage do they have that their neighbors don’t?  
**Costa Rica is probably the most mature live entertainment market in Central America today. It has a relatively strong economy, a sizable middle and upper-middle class, good consumer purchasing power, strong brand participation and, most importantly, an audience that has developed the habit of buying tickets for major international shows.

Costa Rica has also demonstrated that it can support stadium-level business. Our two Bad Bunny shows at Estadio Nacional in December 2025 sold approximately 115,000 tickets in less than four hours, generating close to $12 million in gross ticket sales. Those are numbers that would be significant in almost any market in Latin America.

Guatemala is probably the market with the greatest upside. It has a much larger population than Costa Rica and a very young demographic, but historically it has lacked the infrastructure required to fully exploit that potential. If the new arena in Guatemala City delivers what is being promised, it could materially change the touring landscape there.

Panama is also important because of its economy, connectivity and international profile, while El Salvador has become increasingly interesting because the government has been much more proactive in supporting entertainment and major events.

The difference between these markets isn’t necessarily the appetite for music. The appetite exists throughout Central America. The differences are infrastructure, disposable income, sponsorship, government support, ticketing maturity and how easy or difficult it is to actually produce a show.

**We always talk about Mexico and South America when talking about their influence in the global live business, especially post-pandemic. How have markets like Costa Rica and Guatemala fared during the recent live boom? How, if at all, do they benefit from the growing influx of international acts coming to Mexico and South America?  
**They have benefited enormously. Historically, one of Central America’s biggest challenges was geography. An artist could play Mexico, Brazil, Argentina and Chile and still have difficulty justifying the additional production and transportation costs required to add one or two Central American dates. That equation has changed. There are now so many international tours moving through Latin America that Central America can increasingly become part of the routing rather than a standalone logistical exercise. If an artist is already moving production between Mexico and Colombia, for example, adding Costa Rica or Guatemala can make considerably more sense.

Costa Rica has probably benefited the most because it has proven that the demand is there. Promoters and agents now have data showing that major international artists can generate very significant business there. Guatemala is following the same path, and improved infrastructure should accelerate that process.

I think the biggest change is psychological. For many years, Central America was viewed by the international touring industry as a secondary or optional region. Increasingly, agents are beginning to see certain Central American markets as legitimate stops on a Latin American tour.

**We know many Latin music fans love rock, but taste varies from country to country. What distinguishes Central American markets from others in the LATAM region? What genres do audiences respond to?**  
Central America is much more musically diverse than people sometimes assume. Rock has historically been extremely strong, particularly in Costa Rica. Artists such as Iron Maiden, Metallica, Guns N’ Roses, Red Hot Chili Peppers, Green Day, Foo Fighters and many of the major legacy rock acts have built very loyal audiences there. Costa Rica in particular has one of the strongest rock audiences relative to the size of the country that I’ve encountered in Latin America.

But today’s market is much broader. Urban music is obviously enormous. Bad Bunny demonstrated the scale that the genre can achieve in Costa Rica, and artists like Karol G have extraordinary demand throughout the region. Latin pop remains very strong, as do artists such as Luis Miguel, Alejandro Sanz, Juan Luis Guerra and Ricardo Arjona. Guatemala has traditionally been particularly strong for Latin pop and regional artists, while Panama naturally has a very strong connection to urban and Caribbean music.

What distinguishes Central America is that there isn’t one musical identity. These are small countries geographically, but culturally they are very different markets. You cannot assume that because something works in Guatemala, it will automatically work in Costa Rica, Panama, or El Salvador.

After almost 30 years working there, that local knowledge remains extremely important.

**Infrastructure is always an issue when it comes to LATAM nations, but we’ve seen some progress lately in some South American countries. Have you seen similar traction in Central American markets? What do you think is needed to strengthen the region’s live ecosystem?**  
There has been progress, but infrastructure remains probably the single biggest limitation to the growth of the Central American live business. Costa Rica has Estadio Nacional, Parque Viva and several other venues, but there is still a major gap when it comes to a modern indoor arena capable of accommodating major international touring productions. Guatemala has had the same problem, which is why the new arena there is so important.

But infrastructure is only one part of the equation. Governments need to understand that concerts are an economic activity. They generate tourism, hotel nights, airline traffic, employment, taxes, sponsorship and significant spending around an event. Unfortunately, in some countries, the regulatory environment has become more complicated rather than easier. There are more permits, more agencies involved and more bureaucracy. That ultimately increases costs and makes some tours less viable.

El Salvador has been an interesting contrast because the government has actively embraced entertainment and major events as part of the country’s positioning. Whether one agrees with every aspect of that model or not, from an entertainment-industry perspective it demonstrates what can happen when government sees live events as an economic opportunity rather than simply something to regulate.

For Central America to take the next step, we need more modern venues, clearer regulations, faster permitting processes, better regional logistics and greater cooperation between the private sector and governments. The demand already exists. The challenge is creating the ecosystem that allows us to service that demand.

**Guatemala City is about to have a new arena. What are your thoughts about that market, and how significant is it to the region’s overall live ecosystem?  
**I think Guatemala is potentially one of the most important growth stories in Central American live entertainment. The fundamentals have always been there. Guatemala has the largest population in Central America, a very young audience, a strong music culture and a substantial consumer base in Guatemala City. What it hasn’t had is enough world-class infrastructure.

A modern arena changes the conversation. There are many tours that simply cannot be adapted easily to stadiums, convention centers or improvised venues. A proper arena gives promoters and agents another tool. Suddenly you can accommodate productions that might require 8,000, 10,000 or 15,000 seats without assuming the risk or production complexity of a stadium.

More importantly, I don’t think Guatemala’s growth comes at the expense of Costa Rica, Panama or El Salvador. It strengthens the entire region.

If an agent can look at a routing and see Mexico City, Guatemala City, San Salvador, San José, Panama City and Bogotá as a logical sequence of viable markets, Central America becomes much more attractive as a touring corridor.

That’s ultimately what we’ve been working toward for almost three decades.

When Andrés and I started developing these markets, the challenge was convincing the international industry that Central America mattered. Today the conversation is very different. The audiences have proved that they are there.

Now the infrastructure and the institutions need to catch up.
