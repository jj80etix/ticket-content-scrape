# NATP Calls for Open Ticket Authentication Beyond Ticketmaster’s Selected Partners

## Summary

The National Association of Ticketing Professionals (NATP) published a Nov. 3 commentary urging policymakers to require open ticket authentication and transfer access for any qualified resale marketplace, rather than leaving verification to issuer-negotiated deals like Ticketmaster's CashorTrade integration. NATP argues ticket authenticity should be separable from a marketplace's resale-pricing policy, and that buyers shouldn't face unnecessary account creation or delayed transfers for tickets they already own. The proposal is industry advocacy, not pending legislation, and arrives amid scrutiny of Ticketmaster's transfer infrastructure following an October outage and the DOJ's pending Live Nation-Ticketmaster antitrust settlement.

### Key Points
- NATP's Nov. 3 commentary (by Zach Anderson) calls for "open transfer and open authentication" extended to all qualified resale marketplaces, not just issuer-selected partners.
- Cites Ticketmaster's CashorTrade integration (expanded to Front Gate Tickets Oct. 30) as proof cross-platform authentication works when issuers choose to enable it.
- Argues ticket authenticity should be decoupled from a marketplace's resale-pricing policy — CashorTrade is face-value-or-below only.
- Wants prompt ticket delivery and no unnecessary account creation for buyers receiving transferred tickets.
- References Ticketmaster's Oct. 2 transfer disruption, which prompted the Ticket Policy Forum to ask state AGs to investigate impact on buyers and independent marketplaces.
- Notes overlap with — but a distinct ask from — the DOJ's proposed Live Nation-Ticketmaster settlement's open-distribution provisions.
- Commentary does not specify a certifying body, cost allocation, or data-access model for any open-authentication standard.

### Highlights
- Nov. 3 NATP commentary proposes industry-wide open ticket authentication and transfer access, not tied to any single bill.
- Ticketmaster's CashorTrade deal and new Oct. 21 NFL secondary-ticketing agreement show issuer-backed verification is technically feasible today.
- Oct. 2 Ticketmaster transfer disruption triggered a Ticket Policy Forum request for a state AG investigation.
- This sits alongside, but is distinct from, DOJ's pending Live Nation-Ticketmaster antitrust settlement terms.

### Industry Problem

Resale/transfer authentication today runs through issuer-negotiated, marketplace-by-marketplace deals, so a ticket's verifiability — and a buyer's ability to transfer or resell it without friction — depends on whether the original issuer chose to grant that specific platform access, leaving independent marketplaces and buyers subject to a dominant issuer's discretion.

### Proposed Solution

NATP's own ask — standardized, nondiscriminatory authentication/transfer access for any qualified marketplace — is the solution on the table. For Etix, the actionable move is tracking how any open-authentication standard gets defined (certifying body, data scope, cost allocation) and being positioned to adopt it early: issuer-backed verified transfer without exclusive-partner gating would be a direct trust/UX differentiator for independent clients competing against Ticketmaster-tied venues.

The National Association of Ticketing Professionals (NATP) is urging lawmakers to consider policies that would give qualified ticket resale marketplaces broader access to authentication technology, arguing that consumers should be able to verify and receive legitimate tickets regardless of where they buy them.

In a [Nov. 3 commentary](https://blog.natp.org/a-better-ticket-industry-starts-with-two-simple-ideas-open-transfer-and-open-authentication/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), Zach Anderson, writing for the professional ticket resale trade association, points to Ticketmaster’s partnerships with CashorTrade as evidence that independent platforms can securely authenticate tickets through their original issuers. NATP wants that capability extended beyond individually approved commercial arrangements.

The proposal combines three objectives: prompt delivery of tickets that are available for secure transfer, fewer unnecessary account and personal-data requirements, and interoperable systems that allow qualified marketplaces to verify a ticket’s authenticity and status. It is an industry advocacy proposal, not an announced bill or a new legal requirement.

In April, Ticketmaster [announced an integration with CashorTrade](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/04/ticketmaster-expands-face-value-resale-cashortrade/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) that allows eligible concert tickets to be listed on the independent fan-to-fan marketplace at face value or below. Ticketmaster’s systems authenticate the tickets, and completed sales result in new ticket credentials being issued to buyers within the CashorTrade app.

The model expanded Oct. 30 when [CashorTrade announced a similar integration with Front Gate Tickets](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/cashortrade-and-front-gate-tickets-partner-to-expand-face-value-ticket-resale-for-festival-goers-302925044.html?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), the Ticketmaster-owned festival ticketing provider. That arrangement verifies the original purchase price, including fees, and supports authenticated resale and ticket reissuance for eligible festival passes.

Ticketmaster has presented the arrangements as a way to add secure resale options while respecting artist pricing preferences. In its [April announcement](https://business.ticketmaster.com/press-release/ticketmaster-expands-face-value-ticket-resale-with-cashortrade/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), David Marcus, the company’s executive vice president of music, said, “Fans deserve more ways to buy and sell tickets at the original price, with confidence that what they’re getting is legitimate.”

NATP welcomes the technology but questions why the same authentication capability should depend on a marketplace’s pricing policies or a separate agreement with the issuer. CashorTrade is restricted to face-value-or-below transactions, while other legitimate resale marketplaces permit higher prices where lawful.

“The price of a ticket has nothing to do with whether it is authentic,” Anderson wrote. NATP’s position is that determining whether a ticket is genuine should be separate from decisions about resale prices.

Ticketmaster also has experience supporting verification across multiple resale platforms in professional sports. Its [new NFL agreement](https://business.ticketmaster.com/the-nfl-chooses-ticketmaster-to-power-the-next-era-of-secondary-ticketing/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), announced Oct. 21, is designed to expand distribution of verified season-ticket resale inventory to participating marketplaces. That is an authorized, league-specific network rather than the broader open-access standard NATP is proposing, but it offers another example of issuer-backed resale verification beyond a single storefront.

## A Policy Debate Over Transfer Rights and Marketplace Access

NATP’s proposed reforms also address the period between purchase and entry. The group argues that buyers should receive tickets promptly once those tickets are available for secure transfer, rather than face unexplained delays, and should not have to create unnecessary accounts with a different ticketing company simply to receive tickets they already purchased.

Delayed delivery and controlled transfers can serve legitimate security or event-management purposes. NATP’s proposal does not eliminate the need for safeguards. Instead, it asks policymakers to distinguish necessary controls from restrictions that burden consumers or give the original issuer leverage over transactions occurring on competing platforms.

Those concerns have become more prominent after Ticketmaster’s Oct. 2 transfer disruption. As [TicketNews reported](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/10/ticket-policy-forum-ticketmaster-transfer-investigation/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), the Ticket Policy Forum subsequently asked state attorneys general to investigate the impact on buyers and independent marketplaces. No evidence has established that Ticketmaster intentionally favored its own transactions during the incident, but it illustrated how resale fulfillment can depend on infrastructure controlled by a competing ticket seller.

The broader market-access question also appears in the Justice Department’s proposed Live Nation-Ticketmaster antitrust settlement. The proposal includes open-distribution provisions for qualifying primary ticketing providers, while [TicketNetwork has challenged resale-related eligibility conditions](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/09/ticketnetwork-live-nation-ticketmaster-resale-settlement/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content). NATP’s request is different: it seeks nondiscriminatory authentication and transfer access for qualified resale platforms, not merely the ability to distribute newly issued primary tickets.

Making that vision operational would require more detail. NATP calls for fair, objective technical standards, but its commentary does not specify who would certify a qualified marketplace, how verification costs would be allocated or what access providers would have to sensitive ticket data. The association acknowledges that any system would need privacy protections, cybersecurity safeguards and controls against unauthorized transfers.

No specific legislation implementing NATP’s proposed framework was identified in the commentary. The immediate question for policymakers and ticket issuers is whether authentication can become a broadly available consumer-protection function without compromising security or giving the original issuer final discretion over which competing marketplaces may participate.
