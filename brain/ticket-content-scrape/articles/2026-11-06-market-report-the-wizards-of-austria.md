# Market report: The wizards of Austria

## Summary

IQ Magazine's market report on Austria's live entertainment industry finds the top end of the business — large stadium and arena shows — at record strength, highlighted by Seiler & Speer's sellout of Vienna's Ernst Happel Stadium. Promoters describe a more mixed picture beneath the headline numbers, with rising costs and more selective audiences squeezing mid-tier shows even as major venues and international markets stay healthy.

### Key Points
- Seiler & Speer became the first homegrown act to sell out Vienna's 50,000-capacity Ernst Happel Stadium.
- CTS Eventim-owned promoter Barracuda Music also staged Foo Fighters and Linkin Park there this summer.
- Live Nation GSA cites continued growth in its Austrian arena business (Justin Timberlake, Helene Fischer).
- Austria is Europe's 5th-highest GDP-per-capita country but a compact market (~9m people).
- Promoters flag rising costs and a tougher "geo-political environment" squeezing the mid-tier segment specifically.

## Transcript

A deep dive into the live entertainment industry in Austria finds a market in good health across many aspects – but not all

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/IQ-and-Annual-Banners-22-Market-Report-1.png)

Kraftklub

From a distance, Austria’s live music scene appears to be firing on all cylinders. Seiler & Speer became the first homegrown band to sell out Vienna’s Ernst Happel Stadium, drawing around 50,000 fans to the country’s biggest venue in 2025.

It was a landmark moment for a market where stadium andarena business remains at record levels. “Business is constant and stable, which is a good sign in these times,” says Richard Hörmann, co-MD of leading promoter Barracuda Music.

The CTS Eventim-owned company staged Seiler & Speer’s Ernst Happel Stadium concert and was also behind this summer’s shows by Foo Fighters and Linkin Park at the same venue.

Live Nation GSA CEO Marek Lieberberg provides a similarly upbeat assessment, citing stadium dates with Justin Timberlake and Helene Fischer over the past 12 months. “We continue to grow our arena business substantially,” he adds.

Austria remains one of Europe’s wealthiest and most compact markets: a country of just 9m people with the fifth-highest GDP per capita in the EU, according to Eurostat.

Yet, dig beneath the impressive headline numbers and a more nuanced picture emerges. While the biggest shows are thriving, rising costs, tighter margins, and more selective audiences are squeezing the middle tier. The result is a sector where confidence and caution now coexist.

Filip Potocki of FKP-backed German-Austrian JV Arcadia Live points to a stadium highlight of his own in German rockers Die Toten Hosen’s tour finale in Vienna this September, but he strikes a more cautious note, pointing to a “geo-political environment” that is pushing up costs and intensifying competition across the board.

> “Austria is \[not\] simply an extension of the German market. While we share the language, Austria has its own live market”

Also on the stadium circuit, Böhse Onkelz’s Ernst Happel Stadium show, staged by Jolly Roger Live in partnership with Wizard Live and promoted through Werner Stockinger’s Live-blood – Passion Entertainment, drew 57,000 fans; the biggest show in the German rock band’s 47-year history.

“We’re very proud of that,” says Stockinger, who agrees that Austria’s situation is “significantly influenced” by the global political and economic climate.

“The trend over the past one and a half years has not been particularly strong – to put it mildly,” he says. “We’ve noticed that consumers have become more cautious.”

Anyone doing business here needs to make sure they don’t fall for the biggest misconception identified by Günther Beer of Cobra Agency, that “Austria is simply an extension of the German market.”

He adds: “While we share the language, Austria has its own live market, with its own dynamics, promoters, agents, world-class festivals, and incredibly passionate rock and metal fans.”

Stockinger lists higher taxes as one Austrian-specific drag on competitiveness, alongside operational strain closer to home, including the cost of finding staff and getting them through the mandatory security clearances required by the national security agency.

> “ski resorts that promote shows themselves are something that affects the market”

Notably, he observes that presale patterns have shifted noticeably since war broke out in Iran, underlining just how closely tour promoters are watching the geopolitical weather. The bottom-line effect is best summed up by Beer: rising ticket prices haven’t translated into increased profits.

“In many cases, profits are actually lower than they were a few years ago because higher revenues are being offset by significantly higher costs,” he says.

Cobra’s response has been to lean into ticket scaling – offering premium and VIP tiers along-side a deliberately protected pool of affordable seats, “ensuring live shows remain accessible to the rock and metal community that has always been the foundation of our scene.”

Georg Leitner, founder of Vienna-based independent artist agency Georg Leitner Productions (GLP), sees a market moving in two directions.

“The Austrian live entertainment market has continued to grow,” he says. “Major players such as Live Nation, CTS Eventim, and Barracuda Music have increased the number of stadium and arena shows, while mid-sized and smaller concerts have suffered from lower ticket sales.

“Overall, demand remains strong, although increased competition, higher costs, and more selective audience spending require careful pricing, targeted marketing, and efficient planning.”

Veteran indie promoter Alex Nussbaumer of al-x, who has promoted The Cure in Austria for more than three decades, observes the tension from a different angle. Healthy ticket sales remain achievable, he says, but developing new and emerging artists to the point where audiences are willing to invest in them has become harder since the pandemic.

“This is not new, but it is getting tighter,” he says, linking the shift to what he describes as a widening gap between rich and poor in Austrian society.

“For newer projects, people will turn down a ticket if it’s two euros too high,” he laments. “Then you’ll have a flagship \[artist\] where the price doesn’t matter. So it’s getting tougher. The audience is running out of money, because they’re spending it on festivals. Then they can probably only \[afford\] a couple of headline shows, and that’s it.”

There’s an occasional upside to this shift, too. “It means last-minute things can \[happen\],” says Nussbaumer. “Sometimes, you’re nearly at the point of cancelling a show because of poor ticket sales, and then you get this old-school surprise of people turning up on walk-up, which is unbelievable.”

**Top-Level Promoters**  
Arcadia Live has had an eventful year, marked by both expansion and a significant retreat. The promoter is now in the middle of its fourth season at METAstadt, the roughly 8,000-capacity open-air venue that managing partner Filip Potocki says has firmly established itself on Vienna’s summer calendar.

Shows from Deftones, BIBIZA, and Marilyn Manson all sold well, and a fifth season is already confirmed for next year. From 2027, Arcadia is adding a second exclusive open-air venue to its Vienna operation: the Generali Arena (currently known as Franz Horr Stadium), with a capacity of up to 20,000.

> “Spending for live entertainment is considered essential”

“The two venues complement each other perfectly,” says Potocki. “METAstadt offers an intimate open-air setting for up to 8,000 visitors, while the Generali enables us to bring larger-scale productions to Vienna in a central, easily accessible location.”

Happily, one long-running headache has eased, at least for now. Vienna’s noise curfew, which put METAstadt’s future in doubt in last year’s report, is no longer the live threat it was. “Those discussions have largely settled down for the time being, and the situation around METAstadt is currently stable,” reports Potocki.

This October sees the Vienna launch of Minecraft Experience: Villager Rescue, an immersive, non-music format. “Touring remains at the heart of what we do, but we’re also investing in new formats, such as immersive exhibitions, that allow us to reach different audiences,” says Potocki.

Elsewhere, Live Nation GSA has presented sold-out performances at Vienna’s 16,000-cap Wiener Stadthalle from Billie Eilish, David Garrett, and Nine Inch Nails, among others. Pitbull is lined up for November, alongside Cirque du Soleil dates in Salzburg and Graz.

On pricing, Lieberberg says Austria is broadly in line with the rest of Europe. “Spending for live entertainment is considered essential,” he asserts.

Barracuda Music remains the dominant force in the Austrian market, and Hörmann describes the past year in measured terms. Among the highlights: a record Clam Live season at Clam Castle, with more than 115,000 tickets sold across 12 shows – a company record – plus sold-out runs for Linkin Park and Foo Fighters at Ernst Happel Stadium and Volbeat at Salzburg’s Residenzplatz.

> “Our sales are still constant and strong, due to our policy of not going too high with the prices”

As per Hörmann, competition is not just intensifying from “our main competitor” Live Nation, but from a wider field. “New promoters coming up and ski resorts that promote shows themselves are something that affects the market,” he notes.

He suggests Barracuda has taken a conservative approach to pricing on its own shows. “Our sales are still constant and strong, due to our policy of not going too high with the prices,” he says.

Sitting somewhat apart from the two giants is cute concerts – majority-owned by Barracuda but run, in effect, as its own small operation. Founded two years ago by Richard Petz and Ines Fernau, the firm has just landed a significant hire: Klaus Plewa, formerly a senior booker at Live Nation GSA, whose roster includes Fontaines D.C., Ethel Cain, and Mac DeMarco.

Asked what Plewa brings, Petz says: “First of all, a lot of banging shows. We’ve traditionally promoted more mainstream artists, so it’s refreshing to have someone bringing more indie and alternative acts into the mix.”

He adds that Barracuda’s backing gives cute concerts “the best of both worlds”: a small independent team with room to experiment, but the stability of a bigger group behind it.

The company is also beginning to reap the rewards from Austria’s blossoming domestic scene. “Local artists have finally reached a point where they can really compete with their German counterparts,” says Petz. “It took years of groundwork from bands like Bilderbuch and Wanda, but now we’re selling out 8,000-capacity venues with Austrian alternative artists.”

> “it is an important secondary market and a useful routing point between Western and Eastern Europe”

Over at GLP, former BMG Austria managing director Harald Büchel succeeded Georg Leitner as CEO last year. Leitner credits Büchel with strengthening GLP’s booking structure and expanding its corporate and private events business.

“Harald has brought valuable experience from his former positions in the industry, helping to professionalise internal structures, expand the company’s network, and support its continued growth,” he says.

Recent GLP highlights include booking Jennifer Lopez’s Eastern European and Central Asian dates as junior partner to MAMA Concerts/Marcel Avram. A JLo summer tour is planned for 2027, alongside upcoming work with José Carreras, Plácido Domingo, Gipsy Kings, Kool & The Gang, George Clinton, The Jacksons, Foreigner, and the Earth, Wind & Fire Experience.

Asked where Austria sits in the global touring picture, Leitner is candid, characterising it as “a competitive but relatively small touring market” that often competes “with nearby cities such as Bratislava, Prague, and Budapest for international tour dates.

“Globally, it is an important secondary market and a useful routing point between Western and Eastern Europe, although it does not carry the same weight as larger markets such as Germany, the UK, or France,” he says.

**Stadiums/ Arenas**  
Beyond the capital’s two principal live venues, Austria’s venue market also includes the 10,000-capacity Olympiahalle Innsbruck, plus the 6,700-capacity Salzburgarena and the 5,000-capacity Arena Nova in Wiener Neustadt.

Beyond Vienna, Liveblood’s Stockinger flags Graz – helped by its proximity to Slovenia and Croatia – as an increasingly i mportant secondary market, alongside opportunities in each of Austria’s nine provincial capitals. “It’s important not to oversaturate these markets,” he cautions.

“Austria is as big as Bavaria.” Outside the capital, one of the country’s biggest venue operators is Messe Congress Graz (MCG), which stages more than 400 events a year across the 14,520-capacity Stadthalle, Messe Graz Open Air, and six other venues.

Nevertheless, Christof Strimitzer, MCG’s head of marketing and communications, positionsGraz as complementary rather than competitive to Vienna, highlighting its accessibility and infrastructure.

“We don’t really see it as a highly competitive market,” he says. “Austria’s event industry is built on strong partnerships, and we believe collaboration ultimately benefits organisers, artists, and audiences alike.”

Recent concerts at MCG have included 5 Seconds of Summer, Kraftklub, and Zucchero, with Cirque du Soleil and the opening night of the Aufsteirern festival among the year’s upcoming draws. Family entertainment continues to perform strongly at the venue, alongside international touring productions.

Lieberberg contends that international artists remain keen to play Austria, provided appropriate venues are available. But the lack of suitable infrastructure remains one of the market’s biggest constraints in the eyes of many promoters.

> “We are confident that we will be able to present a much-needed venue option in the 5,000/6,000 range”

“Venues are a key issue, and Austria definitely needs a new state-of-the-art arena, which has been discussed for a long time but is far from getting started,” he says. “A new arena will attract bands and events that can currently not be accommodated.”

Progress on Vienna’s long-awaited new arena – and on fixing the city’s mid-size venue shortage – remains frustratingly slow. CTS Eventim was awarded the contract to build and operate the planned 20,000-cap venue in Vienna’s Neu Marx district in 2024, but the arena is now not expected to open until 2030 at the earliest.

Stockinger recalls having to skip shows due to scheduling conflicts, pointing to the Stadthalle’s annual October tennis tournament, which takes the venue out of circulation during an otherwise high-demand period – underscoring the city’s need for two large indoor venues.

Potocki stresses the urgency of the situation. “Suitable venues remain in short supply and, if anything, the situation has become even more challenging,” he says.

“Smaller clubs in particular are under enormous pressure, and every closure weakens the live ecosystem as a whole. Austria – and Vienna in particular – would bene- fit from greater investment in venue infrastructure across all capacities,” he opines.

On that subject, Lieberberg hints at positive movement. “We are confident that we will be able to present a much-needed venue option in the 5,000/6,000 range soon,” he reveals. Not everyone agrees the perceived shortage is the real constraint, however.

Indeed, Al-x’s Nussbaumer rejects the narrative outright. “I don’t think there is a lack of good infrastructure,” he argues. As he sees it, what the country actually lacks is the kind of state cultural and broadcasting help that underpins the scene in neighbouring Switzerland.

> “The national broadcaster is not supportive in that way,” he says.

**Festivals**  
Barracuda’s festival business had another strong year, topped by the 55,000-cap Nova Rock, held in Burgenland in June, with headliners Volbeat, The Cure, Iron Maiden, and Bring Me The Horizon. Its FM4 Frequency Festival in St.Pölten in August featured artists including Kraftklub, Lorde, Fontaines D.C., and Zara Larsson, while Lovely Days in Eisenstadt starred Alice Cooper and Foreigner in July.

Last year, the company looked to the past and rebooted Forestglade, Austria’s veteran rock festival brand. Initially revived for a one-off, 30th-anniversary edition, it is now confirmed as an annual fixture. Hörmann says that audience demand compelled Barracuda to bring it back permanently and brushed off any concerns of overheating in Austria’s festival calendar.

“It’s getting better; we are not worried,” he says. His outlook for the year ahead is similarly steady, adding: “2027 is going to be a good year; we expect the same stable development as in the last one.”

Lieberberg, by contrast, is more circumspect about Live Nation’s festival ambitions. The company brought hip-hop franchise Rolling Loud and the one-day Racino Rocks, headlined by Metallica, to the 60,000-cap Racino open-air venue in Ebreichsdorf, Vienna in 2024, but there has been no sign of a return.

> “We’re seeing more major rock and metal arena tours than ever before”

“Festivals have had a tough time all over Europe, and it is difficult to establish new formats,” he says. “We continue to look for interesting opportunities, while focusing on our growing arena and stadium business.”

Austria is also home to one of the world’s largest free-entry festivals. Donauinselfest in Viennna holds the Guinness World Record for the largest attendance at a music festival in one location, with 3.3m visits recorded across its three-day 2015 edition. The multi-stage, publicly supported event is organised by SPÖ Wien, Verein Wiener Kulturservice, and Pro Event Team.

Then there’s the alpine market: Austria’s ski resorts operate in a world of their own. In Ischgl, Andrew Zweck’s Sensible Events has run the Top of the Mountain Concert since 1995 – self-styled as the world’s longest-running free festival on a mountaintop – with headliners including Elton John, Rihanna, Bob Dylan, Pink, and Muse.

> “Smaller clubs are under increasing financial pressure, while larger venues are often booked well in advance”

Meanwhile, Snowbombing, touted as Europe’s biggest snow and music festival, has run in Mayrhofen for more than 20 years and has been majority-owned by Superstruct Entertainment since 2023.

**Mid-Tier Venues and Promoter Insights**  
Salzburg-based Cobra Agency booked Seiler & Speer’s Ernst Happel Stadium sellout, a highlight in a busy year for the agency that also included The Legendary Tour by Sabaton, which sold well over 270,000 tickets globally, with a second leg scheduled for Q2 2027.

Cobra’s Beer describes a rock and metal touring market that has kept growing even as it becomes tougher to navigate.

“We’re seeing more major rock and metal arena tours than ever before, with many of them selling out,” he says. “At the same time, certain niches within the genre are experiencing remarkable growth, with artists below arena level building very loyal fan bases.”

The agency’s upcoming slate includes Amon Amarth’s biggest arena tour to date, and Edguy’s farewell run, which sold 10,000 tickets in under 24 hours for its first date.

Cobra, which formed an alliance with US-headquartered Independent Artist Group (IAG) in 2025, has also been expanding its own footprint, opening its first UK office in Manchester earlier this year.

If there’s a consistent theme running through this year’s conversations, it’s the growing strain on the middle of the market – venues and shows too big for a club, too small for a stadium.

“The biggest challenge lies in the mid-sized venue market,” says Isabelle Pfeifer of Bella Concerts. “Smaller clubs are under increasing financial pressure, while larger venues are often booked well in advance.”

It’s been a strong year for Bella Concerts regardless: this spring, concert hall Wiener Konzerthaus chose the agency’s artist Ledisi to front its season campaign, and Pfeifer is now curating a new festival for Seebühne Bremen, set to premiere in July 2027.

She points to a broader shift in audience behaviour. “Rather than going out every weekend, many people choose a few highlights throughout the year,” she says. “That makes it even more important to create meaningful live experiences and to build long-term relationships between artists, promoters, and audiences.”

Bella Concerts has also expanded into publishing. “Success today requires a broader, long-term strategy,” Pfeifer says. “That means combining live performances with strong publishing management, building engaged audiences through social media, choosing the right touring opportunities, and creating multiple, sustainable income streams. All of these elements need to work together to help independent artists build lasting careers.”

**Grassroots**  
For its part, Live Nation has been quietly building depth beneath its marquee shows. “We have substantially increased the number of concerts in Austria, especially with emerging artists and at the club level,” says Lieberberg. “This adds a strong grassroots base to our continuing presentation of the biggest international attractions in the entertainment industry.”

> “As soon as we have built up an artist, we have to fear that it will be taken over \[by bigger players\]”

Nobody paints a more vivid picture of the pressures facing developing artists in Austria than al-x’s Nussbaumer, who says that developing an emerging act from the ground up carries its own risk.

“As soon as we have built up an artist, we have to fear that it will be taken over \[by bigger players\], which I know is free trade, but at the end of the day, I’m not interested in this anymore.”

Even so, Nussbaumer’s own approach has stayed rooted in exclusivity and long-term relationships rather than scale. He has promoted The Cure in Austria exclusively since the early 1990s.

On domestic acts, he is enthused, if slightly baffled by the economics. “It is surprisingly extremely good, but I have no clue how they can survive,” he says, naming Wanda and Bilderbuch as Austria’s flagship exports – both, tellingly, bigger in Germany than at home.

Still, the story of its live business in 2026 is less one of crisis than of a market dividing along clear lines – resilient and growing at the very top, more cautious further down the ladder.
