# Inside the decision to promote Kanye West

## Summary

Following Ye's controversial July 30 show in Madrid, promoter Gonzalo Peña of Vibra Music told IQ about the "enormous" pressure of staging a concert for an artist with a history of antisemitic and pro-Nazi remarks, after multiple other countries blocked or cancelled his shows this year. Peña said the promoter's focus was legal compliance rather than making a moral judgment on the artist's behalf, and that ticket sales tapered as fans questioned whether the show would go ahead amid cancellations elsewhere.

### Key Points
- Ye's shows have been blocked or cancelled in the UK, France, India, Poland and Italy in 2026 over safety/public-order concerns.
- Spain's Culture Minister confirmed the government would not intervene to stop the Madrid show.
- The promoter says ticket sales were initially strong but slowed as fans doubted the show would proceed, given cancellations in neighboring markets.
- Ye has upcoming dates in the US, Indonesia and Russia (a rare Western-artist booking there since the Ukraine invasion).

## Transcript

Following Ye's concert in Madrid, Gonzalo Peña of Vibra Music speaks to IQ about platforming a widely condemned artist

![Ye in Madrid](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Ye-in-Madrid-©-Vibra-Music.png)

Ye in Madrid © Vibra Music

Ye, the artist formerly known as Kanye West, has made a divisive return to the international stage this year. Following his 30 July show at Madrid’s Riyadh Air Metropolitano Stadium, promoter Gonzalo Peña of Vibra Music tells *IQ* he felt “enormous” pressure around staging the show and navigating the moral questions of platforming a widely condemned artist.

In recent years, West has issued a string of antisemitic, racist, and pro-Nazi comments, including releasing a song titled *Heil Hitler* in May 2025. Though he has attributed this behaviour to his bipolar disorder and apologised, his actions have led to ethical and practical queries from promoters, audiences, and authorities, with local governments in the [UK](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/04/report-kanye-west-blocked-from-uk-wireless/), [France](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/04/kanye-west-postpones-france-concert-adds-poland-date/), [India](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/05/kanye-wests-india-show-scrapped-over-safety-concerns/), [Poland](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/04/kanye-wests-poland-and-switzerland-dates-cancelled/), and [Italy](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/06/italys-hellwatt-festival-saga-ends-in-cancellation/) moving to strike down the stadium shows, largely over concerns of public safety and public order.

The controversial rapper’s return to widespread touring launched with two nights in Mexico City in late January. Since then, his fragmented schedule has spanned shows in the US, Türkiye, the Netherlands, Georgia, [Albania](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/07/kanye-west-draws-60k-to-purpose-built-albanian-venue/), Portugal, and Kazakhstan, alongside a string of cancelled dates.

Before the year’s end, he has performances lined up in the US, Indonesia, and most recently, [Russia](https://www.theguardian.com/music/2026/aug/18/kanye-west-announces-tour-dates-st-petersburg-russia-ye), making him one of the most prominent Western artists to schedule a show in the country since its invasion of Ukraine.

Against that backdrop, moving ahead with a show in the Spanish market was a decision that came with immense scrutiny, Peña tells *IQ*. But when asked about the decision to platform an artist with this history, he says he “would not frame this solely in relation to Ye, but rather as a broader question concerning any artist”.

He adds he had “no hesitation” regarding the risks of putting on the show, which he cites as concerns around “audience response” as well as financial viability.

“The level of pressure surrounding the concert reached a scale we had never experienced before in the music industry,” he says. He stresses the team chose to focus on compliance issues, saying: “Faced with that situation, our entire team focused on providing the strongest possible assurances that the concert could take place in strict compliance with the law and all applicable requirements.”

> “As promoters, our responsibility is not to make that decision on behalf of the public”

The one-night performance began taking shape after the Mexico shows, with part of the Vibra team travelling to see the performances put on by their partner promoter, 515 Entertainment, which also co-promoted the Madrid show along Roller Group. The team then “worked relentlessly to secure and announce the Spanish date,” a process that Peña says was “far from straightforward” as promoters from all over had thrown their hats in the ring to host the artist.

“Opportunities like this come along very rarely in life and, when they do, you have to decide whether you are prepared to take the risk. We had no hesitation,” he says.

After the show’s announcement in March, Peña says the initial stream of ticket sales was strong but tapered off as “people began to question whether the concert would ultimately take place” amid shows in neighbouring markets being axed. Ahead of the Madrid date, Spain’s Minister of Culture, Ernest Urtasun, confirmed that the government would not intervene, despite the artist’s “ [unacceptable](https://www.eldiario.es/cultura/musica/deberia-espana-cancelar-concierto-kanye-west-han-hecho-paises_1_13398337.html) ” comments.

Asked about the response to platforming the artist, Peña says it is not his role as a promoter to act as an arbiter.

“I firmly believe in culture and in the freedom of audiences to decide what they want to see and what they want to listen to, always within the boundaries of the law,” he says. “As promoters, our responsibility is not to make that decision on behalf of the public, but to ensure that the shows we produce are delivered professionally, safely, and lawfully. After that, every individual is free to decide whether or not they wish to attend.”

Asked how he attempted to deal with public and political backlash to the decision, he says his approach is that, “ultimately, we are accountable to the people who buy a ticket and place their trust in us.”

In response to a question regarding Ye’s previous antisemitic comments and actions, Peña says: “We are talking about an artist with an extensive international career, who has performed hundreds of concerts around the world in front of millions of people.

> “If somebody came to us tomorrow and asked us to organise a concert on the Moon, we would probably say yes”

“From a strictly professional and production perspective, our responsibility was to ensure that the concert took place within the agreed terms and in full compliance with all applicable regulations. We were confident that the performance would be conducted professionally and that nothing would jeopardise the concert. That is exactly what happened,” he says.

So how does an organiser ensure that a large-scale gathering does not become a vehicle for incitement to hate? Veña points to local frameworks governing public events, which are “not unique to this particular concert”.

“Madrid has a very clear legal framework governing public events, including the circumstances under which an event may be suspended and, naturally, compliance with legislation relating to hate crime and incitement,” he says. “We held numerous meetings on this issue and took all the necessary measures to ensure that the event was conducted lawfully and with all appropriate safety measures in place.”

The safety of the 65,000 attendees was paramount, he says, with the team taking extra preventative measures to “respond to any eventuality”. More than 1,700 people worked on the event, with Veña attributing its success to their participation. In the background, the Vibra team also helped to bring the 7 August show at Portugal’s Estádio Algarve (cap. 30,000) to go ahead after “issues arose with the local promoter,” he explains.

“It is not easy to step into the international big leagues, particularly for relatively small promoters to take the lead on productions of this scale,” he says. “After this experience, the entire team feels stronger and, above all, far better prepared to take on any kind of event.”

“I always joke that if somebody came to us tomorrow and asked us to organise a concert on the Moon, we would probably say yes – and then work out how to do it,” Veña says.

But ultimately, the production came at a price.

“The pressure and responsibility we carried throughout those months were enormous. In 28 years of my professional career, I had never found it so difficult to bring an event to fruition,” he explains.
