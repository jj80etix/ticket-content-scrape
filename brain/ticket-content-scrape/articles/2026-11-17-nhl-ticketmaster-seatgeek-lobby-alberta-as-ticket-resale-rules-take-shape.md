# NHL, Ticketmaster, SeatGeek Lobby Alberta as Ticket Resale Rules Take Shape

## Summary

The NHL, Ticketmaster, SeatGeek and Live Nation have all registered lobbyists on Alberta's Bill 34, which would require resale platforms to disclose full ticket prices and give the province authority to regulate resale prices and fees — without yet proposing a specific cap. The lobbying (SeatGeek registered as early as June, the NHL two days before the bill's Oct. 28 introduction) comes as neighboring Ontario's April resale-price cap has produced an escalating enforcement fight: Ontario has fined SeatGeek Canada a cumulative C$47,500 and StubHub C$20,000 (under appeal) over original-price verification failures that independent marketplaces say they structurally cannot access, while Ticketmaster — which can access its own primary-sale records — disputes that claim.

### Key Points
- Alberta's Bill 34 (introduced Oct. 28, passed second reading Nov. 3) would mandate all-in resale price disclosure and give government rulemaking authority over resale prices/fees; no cap proposed yet, pending stakeholder consultation.
- The NHL's lobbying filing explicitly states its goal is ensuring league interests (World Cup of Hockey 2028, Flames, Oilers) "are not negatively impacted"; the NHL has a commercial stake via its Ticketmaster-run resale exchange.
- SeatGeek's lobbying since June names "resale, consumer protection, competition enforcement and fraud prevention" as its focus; Ticketmaster and Live Nation Canada registered the same day (Aug. 15) via the same firm.
- Ontario's precedent: SeatGeek Canada fined C$47,500 and StubHub C$20,000 (appealing) for resale-cap/price-disclosure violations; independent resellers say they lack access to original purchase records that vertically integrated primary/resale platforms (like Ticketmaster) already hold.
- A rule that caps independent resale markups while leaving primary pricing/fees unrestricted could shift pricing power toward original ticket issuers, giving primary-resale hybrids a structural compliance advantage over standalone resale marketplaces.

### Highlights
- NHL, Ticketmaster, SeatGeek and Live Nation all actively lobbying Alberta ahead of final resale-regulation details; SeatGeek began in June, four months before the bill's introduction.
- Ontario's resale cap has already produced C$67,500+ in fines against SeatGeek Canada and StubHub over original-price verification disputes.
- Alberta has not yet committed to a price cap, leaving an open window to address the verification-access asymmetry Ontario's rollout exposed.

### Industry Problem
Resale-price-cap regulation modeled on Ontario risks entrenching an asymmetry where vertically integrated primary/resale platforms can verify compliance easily while independent resale marketplaces cannot, turning a consumer-protection measure into a competitive moat for incumbents.

### Proposed Solution
Monitor Alberta's rulemaking process (consultation phase, no cap yet) as a bellwether for other Canadian provinces and U.S. states; if a cap emerges, the compliance mechanism that matters most is original-purchase-price verification — a primary platform that exposes verifiable purchase-price data/receipts to any downstream resale channel (not just its own) would defuse the "unfair advantage" critique and could be a differentiator worth building ahead of regulation.

Major players in the ticketing industry are lining up to influence Alberta’s proposed ticket resale regulations, with the NHL, Ticketmaster, SeatGeek and Live Nation Entertainment all retaining lobbying firms as the province considers new restrictions on secondary ticket sales.

The lobbying activity comes as Alberta advances legislation that would require resale marketplaces to disclose the full price of tickets and give the provincial government authority to regulate resale prices, service fees and other charges. The proposal does not currently establish resale price controls, though it does leave the door open to the creation of such resale-specific regulations without comparable primary market price controls if passed as written.

An [investigation published Monday by the Investigative Journalism Foundation](https://theijf.org/brief/nhl-ticketmaster-alberta-reselling-bill?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) (IJF) identified lobbying by the NHL and Ticketmaster. Additional Alberta lobbying registrations reviewed by TicketNews show that SeatGeek began pursuing discussions over ticket resale regulation in June, while Live Nation separately registered to lobby provincial officials on broader live entertainment issues.

The disclosures highlight how competing interests are positioning themselves ahead of rules that could reshape Alberta’s ticketing marketplace, even as neighboring Ontario continues to grapple with the fallout from its own resale price cap.

## Alberta Proposes New Ticket Resale Regulations

Alberta’s Bill 34, the Service Alberta and Red Tape Reduction Statutes Amendment Act, 2026, was introduced October 28 by Minister Dale Nally and passed second reading November 3.

The [proposed amendments to the Consumer Protection Act](https://docs.assembly.ab.ca/LADDAR_files/docs/bills/bill/legislature_31/session_2/20251023_bill-034.pdf?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) would require secondary ticket sellers and resale platforms to disclose the total price of tickets, including taxes, service fees and other charges. The legislation would also authorize the government to establish additional rules governing resale prices and markups.

Rather than impose a specific ceiling on resale prices, the government has said it intends to consult stakeholders before developing those regulations.

That leaves important questions unanswered, including whether Alberta will follow Ontario in imposing a resale cap, how fees would be treated, and whether affiliated resale exchanges and independent marketplaces would face equivalent requirements.

Alberta already has ticketing regulations dating to 2018 that prohibit ticket-buying bots and establish certain refund protections for secondary-market purchases. Bill 34 would expand that framework into pricing and disclosure.

## Industry Lobbying Began Months Before the Bill

The NHL’s November 2 lobbying registration is particularly explicit about the league’s interest in the legislation.

The league retained Prospectus Associates in Corporate Development, with partner Jodie Parmar listed as its consultant lobbyist. The undertaking began October 26, two days before Bill 34 was introduced.

The registration identifies proposed consumer protections for resale event tickets as its subject and states that the lobbying is intended:

“To ensure the interests of the NHL (e.g., World Cup of Hockey 2028, Calgary Flames, Edmonton Oilers) are not negatively impacted.”

The premier’s office, Executive Council, Service Alberta and Red Tape Reduction, and Alberta Tourism and Sport are among the government offices identified as lobbying targets.

The NHL has a direct commercial interest in ticket resale through its partnership with Ticketmaster, which operates the league’s authorized resale exchange.

The IJF investigation illustrated the financial stakes with premium resale listings for the Calgary Flames’ April 10, 2027, home game at the Saddledome. One pair of club seats was advertised for C$23,600, including approximately C$3,600 in service fees. The report noted that Ticketmaster says such fees can be shared among the ticketing company and event stakeholders, including venues, teams and promoters.

Ticketmaster Canada LP registered its own lobbying undertaking August 15, identifying Enterprise Canada’s Jennifer De Paz and Steven McLelan as consultants.

Its stated objective includes discussing the Alberta government’s consideration of measures to protect consumers in live event ticketing. The registration identifies the premier’s office, Executive Council, Service Alberta and several other provincial departments.

Live Nation Entertainment Canada filed a separate registration the same day, using the same lobbying firm and consultants.

The Live Nation registration describes a broader effort to raise awareness of the company’s role in Canada’s live entertainment business and discuss opportunities to expand cultural experiences. Unlike Ticketmaster’s filing, it does not specifically identify ticket resale restrictions as an objective.

SeatGeek, meanwhile, registered its lobbying undertaking June 8, more than four months before Bill 34 was introduced.

The company’s registration identifies StrategyCorp as its lobbying firm and names Tom Olsen, Juliette Castillo, Karin Finley and Fraser Macdonald as consultants.

Its stated interests include a potential Alberta equivalent to ticket sales legislation elsewhere in Canada, with lobbying focused on “live event ticket resale, consumer protection, competition enforcement, and fraud prevention.”

The registration identifies numerous government targets, including the premier’s office, Service Alberta, Alberta Justice and the legislature.

While the filings establish that these companies retained consultants to engage with provincial officials, they do not disclose specific amendments being advocated, establish that meetings occurred with every listed office, or indicate whether the organizations support a resale cap.

Ticketmaster told the IJF that it operates in jurisdictions with different resale regulations, including price caps, and welcomed continued discussions with Alberta officials.

## Ontario’s Troubled Rollout Offers a Warning

The lobbying activity comes against the backdrop of Ontario’s ongoing struggle to implement and enforce a ticket resale cap introduced in April.

Ontario generally prohibits tickets from being resold for more than their original total purchase price, while allowing applicable taxes and permitted resale fees.

Ticketmaster initially [removed resale listings in Ontario](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/04/ticketmaster-delists-ontario-resale-tickets-as-new-price-cap-hands-live-nation-a-competitive-win/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) to bring its marketplace into compliance. Independent resale platforms, however, have argued that the legislation created substantial practical difficulties, particularly around verifying the original purchase price of tickets.

Those concerns have developed into an increasingly contentious enforcement dispute.

In October, Ontario imposed an additional C$22,500 in administrative penalties against SeatGeek Canada, bringing its total assessments under the new rules to C$47,500.

The latest penalties involved violations related to the resale price cap and original-price disclosure requirements. Ontario also assessed C$20,000 in penalties against StubHub, which said it would appeal.

As [TicketNews reported in its coverage of the escalating SeatGeek penalties](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/10/ontario-seatgeek-fines-ticket-resale-compliance/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), independent marketplaces contend they frequently lack direct access to the original transaction records necessary to verify a ticket’s permitted resale price.

Ticketmaster has disputed the suggestion that original purchase information is inaccessible, arguing that purchasers receive receipts documenting what they paid and can provide those records to resale platforms.

The disagreement exposes a potentially consequential distinction between ticketing platforms.

A company that originally issued a ticket may already possess the purchase records needed to establish its resale ceiling. Competing marketplaces may instead have to rely on documentation supplied by individual sellers, sometimes for tickets purchased through another company’s system.

That problem becomes more complicated for season-ticket packages, hospitality allocations and bundled inventory where the original cost of a particular seat may not be readily identifiable.

Ontario’s restrictions have also [created difficulties for season-ticket holders](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/07/ontario-ticket-resale-cap-season-ticket-holders/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), who may depend on higher-demand games to offset losses when selling less desirable tickets below their original cost.

Meanwhile, primary ticket sellers remain free to employ demand-based pricing and charge substantial fees, while restrictions apply to the amounts consumers can recover when reselling their tickets.

Ontario officials maintain that the legislation protects consumers against excessive markups and have defended enforcement efforts. But the continuing disputes demonstrate how a resale cap can introduce additional regulatory and competitive complications without necessarily addressing pricing practices in the primary market.

Alberta’s proposed approach differs from Ontario’s in one important respect: the province is seeking legislative authority before settling on the specific resale restrictions it intends to impose.

That creates an opportunity to address questions that have complicated Ontario’s experience before new rules take effect.

For consumers, the outcome could influence not only resale prices but also which marketplaces remain viable, whether tickets can be sold above or below their original cost, and how buyers and sellers navigate competing ticketing platforms.

For the companies lobbying Alberta, the eventual regulations could affect fee revenue, resale inventory, compliance costs and control over ticket distribution.

A policy that limits independent resale markups while leaving premium primary prices unrestricted could shift enormous pricing power toward original ticket issuers. Requirements involving original-price verification could also provide an operational advantage to platforms that operate simultaneously as primary and resale vendors over independent competition.

The next steps will therefore determine whether Alberta develops a framework that applies consistent rules across competing resale channels or follows Ontario into a system where consumer protections remain entangled with disputes over platform access, pricing information and enforcement.

Bill 34 remains under consideration, with the government planning further stakeholder consultation before establishing the detailed resale regulations.
