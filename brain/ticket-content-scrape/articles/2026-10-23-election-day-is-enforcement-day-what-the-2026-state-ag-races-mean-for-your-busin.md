# Election Day Is Enforcement Day: What the 2026 State AG Races Mean for Your Business in 2027 and Beyond

## Summary

A Troutman Pepper Locke client alert previews the Nov. 3, 2026 state attorney general elections across 30 states plus D.C., noting guaranteed turnover (at least 11 new AGs), several genuinely competitive races, and enforcement positions increasingly becoming campaign issues. The firm advises companies with regulatory exposure to track candidates' stated enforcement priorities now, since incoming AGs' leadership picks and multistate-coalition alignments in 2027 will follow directly from this election.

### Key Points
- 31 state AG offices on the ballot Nov. 3, 2026; Democrats defend 17, Republicans 14; at least 11 seats are open due to term limits/other campaigns.
- Nationally Republicans hold 27 AG offices to Democrats' 24; Democrats have a path to a national majority this cycle.
- Key competitive races: Arizona (Mayes vs. Petersen, 2022 margin 0.01%), Wisconsin (Kaul vs. Toney), Kansas (Kobach vs. Mann, rated "leans Republican").
- Nevada and Michigan are open-seat races to watch given outgoing AGs' active multistate enforcement roles (e.g., Nevada's Aaron Ford on children's-privacy actions).
- Firm's advice: assess whether pending investigations/litigation in open-seat states should be resolved before transition; track candidates' consumer-protection and tech-enforcement positions as a preview of 2027 priorities.

### Highlights
- 31 AG races Nov. 3, 2026, with guaranteed turnover of at least 11 seats — directly reshaping which offices lead multistate consumer-protection/junk-fee enforcement in 2027.
- Tight races (Arizona, Wisconsin, Kansas) could flip enforcement priorities in swing states.
- New AGs' senior-staff appointments and multistate-coalition choices (the bodies that drive junk-fee and resale-cap actions) follow in early 2027.

### Industry Problem
The state-AG enforcement landscape that drives junk-fee disclosure rules and resale/price-cap actions (Q9/Q11) is about to see a large, hard-to-predict reshuffle, making near-term compliance posture harder to plan around.

### Proposed Solution
No ticketing-specific solution is proposed in the alert; the actionable takeaway is to monitor the Nov. 3 results and incoming AGs' early 2027 staffing/priority announcements as a leading indicator of which states will escalate junk-fee and resale enforcement next.

The 2026 election season is poised to bring substantial changes to the roster of state attorneys general (AGs). On November 3, voters in 30 states and the District of Columbia will decide AG races, and the results are set to influence legal and policy outcomes well beyond state lines. For companies operating in these states, the stakes are concrete: AGs now sit at the center of enforcement affecting technology, consumer protection, gambling and prediction markets, health care, consumer financial services (including Tribal lending), privacy, and other regulated areas.

Three features define this cycle. First, turnover is guaranteed: at least 11 new AGs will take office in January. Second, the competitive map has widened beyond the traditional battlegrounds. Third, enforcement itself has become a campaign issue, with candidates running on — and against — their offices’ litigation records. Together, these dynamics mean the results will shape not only who occupies these offices but also how enforcement priorities and multistate coalitions develop in 2027.

### The Numbers Behind the Turnover

Democrats are defending 17 of the 31 offices on the ballot, and Republicans are defending 14. Nationwide, Republicans hold 27 AG offices and Democrats hold 24. Given the current political landscape, Democrats have a path to a national majority, while Republicans could absorb limited losses and retain control.

The scale of change is notable. When this group of 31 offices was last on the ballot in 2022, three seats changed party hands — Arizona, Iowa, and Vermont. This year, at least 11 seats are open because the incumbent is term-limited or seeking another office, and additional turnover is possible in races involving incumbents. A sizable incoming class could also reshape how AGs work together, including those who emerge as leaders within the national associations and multistate coalitions.

### Open Seats: Transition Risk Regardless of Party

Open seats pose a distinct challenge for companies even where the office is expected to stay in the same party’s hands. When an AG leaves to pursue higher office, devoted senior staff often follow, creating turnover across the senior ranks of the office, not just at the top. Companies with active investigations or litigation in these states face a practical question: whether to seek a resolution before the current AG departs or to let the matter proceed under the new administration.

Nevada illustrates the stakes. Democratic AG Aaron Ford is term-limited and running for governor, and he has been a leader in children’s privacy cases against technology companies and among his colleagues at the Democratic Attorneys General Association. In the open race, Democrat Nicole Cannizzaro has outraised Republican Adriana Guzmán Fralick and every other statewide candidate. Michigan, where term-limited AG Dana Nessel’s successor will be chosen in a race forecasters view as close, has another open seat to watch.

### Competitive Incumbents: Familiar Matchups, Tighter Margins

Several incumbents face serious challenges, and several of the most closely watched contests are rematches of narrow 2022 races. In Arizona, Democratic AG Kris Mayes, who won by roughly 0.01% in 2022, faces Republican Warren Petersen in what is expected to remain a tight race. In Wisconsin, Democratic AG Josh Kaul again faces Republican Eric Toney, whom he defeated by 1.33% four years ago.

Kansas shows how far the competitive map has expanded. Republican AG Kris Kobach, who won the 2022 race by 1.6 percentage points, faces Democrat Chris Mann in a rematch. Sabato’s Crystal Ball has shifted the race from “likely Republican” to “leans Republican,” before the Kansas Fraternal Order of Police endorsed Mann — its first Democratic endorsement for attorney general since 2002. Democrats have won an AG election in Kansas just once in the last 50 years. Because the Kansas office is large for the state’s size, the outcome will determine whether those resources are directed toward Republican or Democratic priorities.

### Enforcement Has Become a Campaign Issue

In several states, candidates are debating not only who should lead the office, but how its authority should be used. Iowa offers a clear example. Republican AG Brenna Bird points to her office’s technology litigation and consumer protection work as part of her reelection campaign against Democrat Nate Willems, and the national associations have each committed $1.5 million to the race. In Georgia’s open race, Democrat Tanya Miller and Republican Brian Strickland are debating government transparency, open-records enforcement, and license-plate surveillance.

For businesses, the implication is straightforward: AG enforcement is no longer something to evaluate only after Election Day. Positions candidates take on the campaign trail represent a reliable preview of where new administrations may direct their resources.

### What Businesses Should Watch

Companies with significant state regulatory exposure should look beyond which party wins each race. The more consequential questions are which candidates are emphasizing issues relevant to those companies’ industries, which offices are likely to experience leadership and senior staff turnover, and how a sizable incoming class could alter the makeup and priorities of multistate coalitions.

Between now and November, businesses should monitor competitive races alongside candidates’ stated enforcement priorities, particularly where AG activity is already increasing, and should assess whether pending matters in open-seat states are better resolved before the transition. After Election Day, attention will shift to incoming AGs’ senior staff appointments and early policy announcements, and to which existing investigations, litigation, and multistate coalitions they choose to join or lead. Understanding these changes early will be critical for companies seeking to anticipate and respond to new AG leadership.
