# UK Minister Promises Faster Resale Crackdown at Industry-Backed Labour Conference Panel

## Summary

UK Creative Industries Minister Ian Murray told a Labour-conference, industry-organized panel that the government hopes to move its ticket-resale price-cap legislation faster than planned, though he gave no firm date. The proposed framework would cap resale at face value plus up to a 10% platform fee, eliminate legal above-cost resale, and carry penalties up to 10% of global turnover — a structure UK Music is actively lobbying to accelerate and avoid "dilution" of, notably with no independent resale marketplace represented on the panel.

### Key Points
- Draft UK ticketing bill would cap resale prices at original cost (incl. fees) plus ≤10% platform service charge.
- Penalties for violations could reach 10% of global turnover; enforcement treated as critical given risk of transactions moving offshore.
- Minister Murray signaled a faster timeline but did not commit to a publication date for the draft bill.
- Industry-advocacy panel (UK Music, BPI, Musicians' Union) pushing to accelerate; no independent resale platform included in the discussion.
- Proposal targets secondary market only — no corresponding caps on primary-market demand-based/premium pricing.

### Highlights
- Proposed cap: face value + fees, +≤10% resale-platform service charge; no profit margin allowed on resale.
- Penalties up to 10% of global turnover for violations.
- No firm legislative timeline yet, despite the "faster" signal.

### Industry Problem
Regulators are moving toward structurally eliminating profitable independent ticket resale in the UK, while leaving primary-market demand-based pricing untouched — reshaping resale economics industry-wide and raising enforcement/offshore-evasion risk that any platform operating in or near the UK market needs to track.

### Proposed Solution
No product build applies directly; posture is monitor/improve — track the draft bill's final compliance obligations (fee caps, inventory-limit verification) since any orchestrator or marketplace touching UK resale will need to support them once enacted.

Creative Industries Minister Ian Murray says the UK government hopes to move its planned crackdown on ticket resale faster than previously expected, making the pledge at a Labour Party conference event organized by one of the music industry groups that has spent years pressing lawmakers for tighter restrictions.

Speaking at a UK Music fringe event in Liverpool on Monday, [Murray said](https://www.artsprofessional.co.uk/news/murray-says-government-will-legislate-on-ticket-touts-as-quickly-as-possible) the government’s draft ticketing bill was being prepared as quickly as possible and that he was hopeful it could progress “quicker than we initially intended.”

The setting was notable. UK Music, which organized the event, openly describes itself as a “direct conduit” between the music industry and government and says influencing policy on behalf of the sector is a core part of its work. The group specifically identifies the government’s commitment to cap ticket resale prices as an area in which [its policy efforts](https://www.ukmusic.org/policy-campaigns/secondary-ticketing/) have had an impact.

The Labour conference panel was chaired by UK Music chief executive Tom Kiehl and included Murray, BPI chief executive Jo Twist, Musicians’ Union general secretary Naomi Pohl, All-Party Parliamentary Group on Music co-chair Alex Sobel and UK Music head of diversity Eunice Obianagha. No representative of an independent ticket resale marketplace appears on the published panel lineup.

Murray told the audience that £400,000 per day was leaving fans’ pockets and going to ticket touts, saying the issue “has to be resolved.” He did not announce a publication date for the draft legislation, but said he hoped the measure could advance more quickly than initially planned.

Sobel separately described passage of ticket-touting legislation as the APPG on Music’s top short-term priority, while Kiehl welcomed Murray’s commitment to accelerate the process.

The comments come as UK Music is explicitly lobbying the government not only to enact a resale price cap but to avoid further delay or “dilution” of the proposal. Its preferred framework would allow a ticket holder to recover the original purchase price, including fees, but make a profit on the resale unlawful. UK Music has proposed allowing resale platforms an additional charge of no more than 10% to cover operating costs.

That position broadly tracks the government’s existing plan, though ministers have not yet settled every detail.

Under the framework announced last year, tickets generally could not be resold for more than their original cost, including unavoidable fees paid during the initial purchase. The government also plans a separate cap on resale-platform service fees, restrictions preventing sellers from reselling more tickets than they were originally entitled to purchase and strict compliance obligations for marketplaces facilitating transactions.

Violations could ultimately fall under consumer-enforcement powers carrying penalties of up to 10% of global turnover.

The proposal would not technically prohibit independent resale marketplaces from operating. In its consultation response, the government said its eventual platform-fee cap should leave enough room for compliant marketplaces to operate sustainably.

It would, however, eliminate legal above-cost ticket resale and substantially change the economics of independent resale platforms that currently facilitate market-priced transactions between buyers and sellers.

The government’s own consultation also recorded objections that a price ceiling could push transactions “underground” or toward less regulated channels where buyers have fewer protections. Officials acknowledged that primary and secondary ticket markets are interdependent and that enforcement will be critical if sellers or platforms attempt to evade the restrictions.

Those [implementation questions](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/09/uk-ticket-resale-cap-bill-offshore-enforcement/) have become increasingly important as the bill moves toward drafting.

TicketNews reported this week that the Department for Culture, Media and Sport has assembled a dedicated team to work on the measure while officials consider risks including transactions moving offshore. The government has committed to publishing a draft bill during the current parliamentary session for pre-legislative scrutiny.

That remains different from introducing a final bill for passage.

Murray’s latest remarks signal that ministers would like the process to move faster, but he did not provide a new legislative deadline or guarantee when the proposed restrictions would become law.

The broader debate also remains more complicated than the political shorthand of cracking down on “touts.” The proposed restrictions focus primarily on the secondary market and would not impose corresponding limits on how artists, promoters or primary sellers initially price tickets, including through premium or demand-based pricing.

Plans for price cap legislation [stalled over increasing concern for the potential consumer harm](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/05/uk-ticket-resale-price-cap-reportedly-pushed-back-as-debate-over-consumer-harm-intensifies/) brought forth in similarly price-capped markets, but their supporters have continued to press for them on both sides of the Atlantic.

Supporters argue that eliminating the ability to profit from resale would remove the incentive for professional operators to acquire scarce tickets simply to resell them at higher prices. The Competition and Markets Authority has supported a resale cap on that basis. Critics have argued that restricting prices on regulated marketplaces does not create additional inventory for high-demand events and could instead divert some buyers and sellers toward social media, private transactions or offshore services.

What Murray’s Labour conference appearance makes particularly clear is which side of that policy debate currently has direct access to the government’s public discussion of the legislation. His promise to accelerate the crackdown came before a panel populated by music-industry organizations that have advocated for tighter resale restrictions, while the independent marketplaces that would have to operate under the new price, fee and compliance rules were absent from the published lineup.
