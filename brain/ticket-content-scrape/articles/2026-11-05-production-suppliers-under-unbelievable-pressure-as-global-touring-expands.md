# Production suppliers under ‘unbelievable’ pressure as global touring expands

## Summary

Universal Pixels' commercial director tells IQ that "unbelievable" supply-chain pressure is reshaping touring economics, with equipment lead times stretching from roughly 30 days pre-pandemic to around 200 days now. Rising demand for AI infrastructure is squeezing component availability further, and specialist cinematic camera gear faces long waits due to small-batch manufacturing. Flexibility and local collaboration are described as essential as tours expand into new, less-infrastructure-rich territories.

### Key Points
- Equipment order-to-pickup lead times have grown from ~30 days pre-pandemic to ~200 days currently, per Universal Pixels.
- AI-infrastructure demand is competing for the same components touring-production suppliers need.
- Shift toward simpler video-based visuals is increasing demand for specialist cinematic cameras, which have long lead times.
- Universal Pixels celebrates its 10th anniversary, having supplied tours for Oasis, Radiohead, Robbie Williams, and others.

## Transcript

## Production suppliers under ‘unbelievable’ pressure as global touring expands

Flexibility, local collaborations, and 'not being too dogmatic' are essential to future expansion, Universal Pixels' Phil Mercer says

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/JBAJSEL_OASIS-CDMX-N1_2025_0912_223229_5464.jpg)

Universal Pixels supplied screens for Oasis Live '25 © Julian Bajsel

“Unbelievable” supply chain pressures are changing the economics of global tours and accelerating the strain on production suppliers, Universal Pixels’ commercial director tells *IQ*, making flexibility and local collaboration essential to future expansion.

Celebrating its 10th anniversary this year, the UK-based video supplier has helped support tours from clients including Oasis, Radiohead, Robbie Williams, Roger Waters, Robyn, and Katseye. A more recent project includes Greek singer Anna Vissi’s massive Olympic Stadium show in Athens, which featured the company’s largest-built screen at over 1,200 square metres.

New challenges for production suppliers have expanded in recent years, as touring teams expand where, when, and how they take their shows around the road. At the same time, more artists from more places are contributing to rapid growth, also increasing the level of flexibility required in delivering shows across territories with varied levels of infrastructure.

Equipment availability is a leading challenge in this case, and has only intensified post-pandemic. While the industry experienced significant supply chain disruption in the aftermath, the current demand for AI infrastructure is putting immense pressure on component availability.

“We’re 200 days at the moment from placing an order to being able to pick it up and ship it, which is just unbelievable,” Phil Mercer tells *IQ*, adding that the timeline was around 30 days before COVID.

As more tours shift to [simpler video-based visuals](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/06/has-simplicity-become-tourings-next-great-spectacle/), demand for specialist cinematic cameras has become another concern, with long lead times due to small-batch manufacturing. Both of which make production planning difficult, particularly in a time when tours are being mapped out much further in advance.

“There’s this kind of insatiable demand at the moment from our industry and others that are all fighting for capacity, whether it’s on a production line or for components. It’s a real challenge,” he says.

> “We all use the same equipment, but we’re always looking for an edge”

For suppliers, remaining competitive is about more than just offering the latest and greatest equipment. Mercer says that, with much of the technology available to everyone, Universal Pixels’ differentiator is the way they package, deploy, and transport their offering.

This means developing less power-intensive solutions that are lighter and occupy less space in trucks and shipping containers, both a commercial consideration as much as an environmentally friendly one.

“We are always constantly trying to package what is out there better than our rivals. We all use the same equipment, but we’re always looking for an edge. We’re always looking to be efficient,” Mercer explains.

More than anything, being flexible in their production provisions is of the utmost importance, especially as tours reach markets with varied supply capabilities.

He cites Dave’s recent two-night stand in Lagos’ Wole Soyinka Centre for Culture and Creative Arts (FKA [The National Theatre](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/02/adekunle-gold-puts-lagos-rebuilt-national-theatre-to-the-test/)) as an example, with Universal Pixels supplying portable media servers while the other elements were sourced locally.

> “The camera phone is our friend, we have to work with it rather than fight it”

For Mercer, this flexibility is essential as the next chapter of global touring unfolds.

“It’s about not being too dogmatic about things. As tours reach these far-flung places, it’s having real flexibility to source locally. It’s absolutely the right thing to do. And then if there’s an element and a component that comes from us, we’re working with people. Don’t be the blocker. You have to work with what’s there,” he says.

Yet with audiences around the world often capturing shows through personal devices and sharing them online, fans have developed an expectation of consistency around the world.

“If someone sees a show in one territory, there’s an expectation that in another they want to see the same show,” Mercer says. “There’s a real demand for some degree of consistency because these things are not secret anymore. The camera phone is our friend, we have to work with it rather than fight it.”

One major development that Mercer says could significantly change the look of live productions is the movement of loudspeakers behind video screens. He credits French band Indochine with the pioneering approach, which has since been rolled out at Sphere shows in Las Vegas and at Harry Styles’ Madison Square Garden residency.

“It’s going to change the way shows look for sure. It’s going to be a game changer,” he says, advising that the speakers need consistent distance from the screen to help lessen the impact on sound quality.

> “What we will see in the next 10 years is what I would call an all-season stadium circuit”

While the configuration presents technical challenges and potential quality impacts, the draw for a more visually cohesive stage design is considerable.

Looking ahead to Universal Pixels’ next decade, Mercer says the stadium touring model is on the verge of a transformation.

“What we will see in the next 10 years is what I would call an all-season stadium circuit,” he says. “We will see stadiums maybe in the capital cities with closing roofs, take the pitch out, when that football team’s playing away that weekend, they’ll have a gig in.”

Turning stadiums into year-round entertainment destinations could open up more opportunities for touring productions and innovations – a growth path reliant heavily on what happens at the other end of the business.

“Touring needs to be healthy at every level, and at the moment, it’s not quite,” Mercer concludes, but adds that the opening of mid-tier venues is a strong signal for the development pipeline.
