# vivenu Summit Puts Fragmented Fan Data at Center of Ticketing’s AI Push

## Summary

At vivenu's invitation-only AI Summit in New York (Sept. 17), speakers from StubHub, OVG360, Playfly Sports, and others said fragmented customer data — not AI itself — is the biggest obstacle to putting AI to practical use in ticketing, marketing, and fan engagement. Panelists noted that even large-market teams sometimes still manage data manually in spreadsheets, and cautioned organizations against starting AI initiatives before solving the underlying data-unification problem.

### Highlights
- Event: vivenu AI Summit, New York, Sept. 17, 100+ senior leaders from ticketing, sponsorship, and fan-engagement organizations.
- Speakers included representatives from StubHub, OVG360, Playfly Sports, WMT Digital, Breadcrumb, Minerva, and Next League.
- Panel theme: data spread across ticketing, CRM, premium seating, sponsorship, F&B, and retail systems blocks practical AI use.
- Some large-market sports teams reported still managing portions of customer data via Excel/Google Sheets.
- Breadcrumb CEO Marcus Ellison cautioned against leading with AI itself rather than a clear business problem.

### Industry Problem
Ticketing and sponsorship organizations — including sophisticated, large-market operators — lack a unified view of customer data across systems, which is the practical bottleneck preventing them from deploying AI for personalization, forecasting, or fan engagement, directly benchmarking against Etix Assistant's own data-grounding challenge.

### Proposed Solution
The summit itself surfaced the problem rather than a single vendor fix; the implied response (aligned with vivenu's own AI-Ready Revenue Stack framing) is investing in data unification/integration layers before layering AI on top. For Etix, this validates prioritizing a unified customer/fan data model as a prerequisite for Etix Assistant's usefulness, and suggests benchmarking Etix Assistant's data-grounding approach against what vivenu and peers are building.

Live entertainment’s push to bring artificial intelligence into ticketing, marketing and fan engagement is running into a decidedly less futuristic obstacle: many organizations still cannot get a unified view of the customer data they already have.

That was a recurring theme at vivenu’s AI Summit, an invitation-only event hosted by the ticketing technology company at its New York offices Sept. 17. The event brought together more than 100 senior leaders from ticketing, sponsorship and fan engagement, with speakers from StubHub, OVG360, Playfly Sports, WMT Digital, Breadcrumb, Minerva, Next League and other companies.

Across three panels focused on The AI-Ready Revenue Stack for Sports & Entertainment, speakers repeatedly pointed to data spread among ticketing, CRM, premium seating, sponsorship, food and beverage, retail and digital platforms as a barrier to putting AI to practical use, according to event materials supplied to TicketNews after the summit.

The problem can exist even at relatively sophisticated organizations. During the opening panel, speakers said some large-market teams are still managing portions of their data through Excel and Google Sheets, while some smaller organizations have moved further toward extracting and acting on customer insights.

## Ticketing Platforms Compete to Become the Data Layer

The discussion comes as ticketing technology companies increasingly position their platforms not simply as systems for processing transactions, but as the underlying data infrastructure from which organizers can run marketing, analytics and AI applications.

That is a direction vivenu has been moving toward directly. The company [launched vivenu Engage in June](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/06/vivenu-launches-engage-ticketing-data-fan-marketing-fuel/), adding segmentation, campaign creation, consent management and other customer relationship tools directly to its ticketing platform rather than requiring organizers to move that information into separate systems.

The company followed that in July with an AI suite built to query live ticketing data, create reports and customer segments, and assist with marketing campaigns. vivenu says its [AI Assistant](https://vivenu.com/vivenu-ai-assistant) draws from an organizer’s first-party data as well as a ticketing-specific knowledge base.

At the summit, vivenu CTO and co-founder Jens Teichert said 48% of vivenu users who have tried the AI Assistant are now using it daily, according to figures supplied by the company. He argued that simply attaching an AI interface to existing software is not enough.

“It’s not enough to bolt agentic capability on top of an API — it actually has to work,” Teichert said. “vivenu’s platform is built on a context layer — a knowledge base that understands the product, the industry, and the context of your business.”

Teichert also emphasized data ownership, saying vivenu does not believe customer information should be used to train its own models or turned into a competitive advantage for another organization.

The broader idea is not unique to vivenu. TicketNews has covered an increasingly competitive push among technology providers to unify the data sitting underneath AI applications.

Jump, for example, [introduced an agentic AI suite earlier this year](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/02/jump-debuts-agentic-ai-suite-ticketing-sports-teams/) around a platform combining ticketing, marketing, messaging, fan identity and other customer information into what the company calls a single source of truth. Accesso similarly launched an intelligence product [following its acquisition of Dexibit](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/03/accesso-expands-ai-strategy-dexibit-acquisition/) that connects data from ticketing, retail, operations and other systems.

The emerging competition suggests AI may make the architecture underneath a ticketing platform increasingly important. An organizer can have access to the same general-purpose AI models as its competitors while getting very different results depending on what customer information those models can see, how current it is and whether systems can connect a fan’s activity across different parts of the business.

## Personalization Without Automating the Experience Away

The summit also highlighted a second question that becomes more important once organizations solve the data problem: Just because something can be automated or personalized does not necessarily mean it should be.

A panel featuring StubHub Director of Product and Strategy Rob Tomlinson, Wolf Cycle co-founder and partner Scott Kornfield and 3DDV Chief Revenue Officer Francis Casado focused on growing consumer expectations for personalization.

Live events increasingly compete for attention against digital products that already tailor recommendations around individual users, speakers said. Better customer data can allow teams and venues to identify particular interests and connect buyers with theme nights, premium products or other event experiences rather than treating an entire audience as a single customer segment.

The final panel — featuring OVG Alliance and OVG360 Co-Chairman Peter Luukko, Playfly Sports President Chris Marinak and Next League Chief AI & Innovation Officer Shripal Shah — emphasized limits as much as opportunities.

Speakers pointed to repetitive work as an obvious starting point for AI, particularly at smaller organizations where automation can expand what a limited staff can accomplish. One example involved AI-powered live translation allowing a single production to be adapted into dozens of broadcasts.

Other parts of the live-event experience may benefit from remaining inefficient.

The panel used baseball umpiring as an example: AI could theoretically make calls faster and more consistently, but removing the human umpire could also change an element of the experience that fans associate with attending a game.

“Sporting events are the ultimate analog experience, but you have to personalize to compete,” Marinak said. “Fans want an escape — not to go back in time 50 years. Something that adds value for your customer is better than something ‘cool’ that might actually worsen the experience for the fan.”

That distinction — whether technology adds something meaningful for the customer rather than merely demonstrating what can be automated — ran throughout the summit.

The result was an AI strategy considerably less sweeping than automating every available interaction. Across the panels, the recurring model was to use technology for speed, analysis and repetitive execution while leaving people responsible for deciding what action actually makes sense.

“Technology is changing fast, but the relationships remain,” vivenu VP of Global Partnerships & Strategy Keal Blache said. “At the end of the day, this will always be a business grounded in relationships.”
