# Music Industry Groups, Including Azoff’s Coalition, Back D.C. Resale Cap in Court

## Summary

Ten music-industry organizations — including Irving Azoff's Music Artists Coalition, AFM, SAG-AFTRA and the Recording Academy — filed amicus briefs backing Washington D.C.'s RESALE Act against legal challenges from resale marketplaces (SeatGeek, Vivid Seats, Gametime, TickPick) and ticket brokers. The law caps covered resales at 110% of the original price plus a 10% service fee starting Jan. 1, 2027, but leaves primary-market pricing, including dynamic pricing, untouched. The amici argue resale caps blunt bot/speculative-purchase incentives, while plaintiffs and some economists counter that capping only the secondary market does nothing for affordability and may entrench Ticketmaster's primary-side dominance by weakening independent resale competition.

### Key Points
- RESALE Act caps resale at 110% of original price (incl. fees/taxes) + up to 10% marketplace service fee; takes effect Jan. 1, 2027 if upheld.
- Plaintiffs: Ticket Policy Forum (SeatGeek, Vivid Seats, Gametime, TickPick) and National Association of Ticketing Professionals (with brokers ASC Ticket Company, Stage Front Tickets) are seeking a preliminary injunction; hearing was Nov. 19 before Judge Carl J. Nichols.
- Amici include AFM, Artist Rights Alliance, Fan Alliance, Future of Music Coalition, Music Artists Coalition, Music Managers Forum-US, NITO, Recording Academy, SAG-AFTRA, Songwriters of North America.
- MAC's board has documented ties to Live Nation/Ticketmaster/Oak View Group (Azoff, Coran Capshaw, Ali Harnell, Susan Genco); National Consumers League has flagged this in Senate testimony as undisclosed overlap.
- Amici argue resale brokers don't compete for primary venue contracts, so capping resale doesn't address Ticketmaster's primary dominance — and may let Ticketmaster collect more fees when its own-sold tickets are resold on its own marketplace.
- Prior GAO (2018) and academic research (Marketing Science, 2023) found resale price caps have uncertain or even counterproductive effects on consumer prices and marketplace competition.

### Highlights
- RESALE Act: 110% price cap + 10% max service fee on covered resales, effective Jan. 1, 2027.
- Preliminary-injunction hearing held Nov. 19, 2026 before U.S. District Judge Carl J. Nichols; ruling pending on whether the law takes effect on schedule.
- Plaintiffs include SeatGeek, Vivid Seats, Gametime, TickPick (Ticket Policy Forum) and independent ticket brokers.
- Academic/GAO research cited by both sides disputes whether resale caps actually lower what consumers pay.

### Industry Problem
The case crystallizes a structural tension in ticketing regulation: resale-only price caps target intermediaries without touching primary pricing power, so a primary platform with venue-contract dominance (Ticketmaster) could gain relative strength as independent secondary marketplaces become less economically viable — a dynamic any primary platform operating resale/exchange features should track.

### Proposed Solution
Nothing is being built here; it's litigation and advocacy. For Etix, the trigger to watch is the Jan. 1, 2027 effective date and the injunction ruling — if the cap survives, other jurisdictions are likely to copy D.C.'s model, so Etix's own resale/exchange terms and fee disclosure should be reviewed for compliance readiness ahead of similar state/local proposals.

## Transcript

![](https://www.ticketnews.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Irving_Azoff_at_State_of_the_Entertainment_Industry_2018-1200x675.webp)

Ten prominent music industry organizations have filed amicus briefs supporting Washington, D.C.’s new ticket resale restrictions, urging a federal judge to reject legal challenges brought by ticket marketplaces and professional resellers before the law’s scheduled January 2027 implementation.

The filings, submitted Nov. 13 in two federal lawsuits, bring together artist advocacy groups, musicians’ unions, independent venue representatives and music industry organizations in support of the District’s Restricting Egregious Scalping Against Live Entertainment (RESALE) Act.

Among the participating organizations is the Music Artists Coalition (MAC), co-founded by longtime music executive Irving Azoff, whose history includes leading Ticketmaster and Live Nation and co-founding Oak View Group. The group’s participation also reflects the substantial overlap between artist-management interests, major concert industry businesses and organizations advocating tighter restrictions on independent ticket resale.

The briefs arrive amid a broader industry debate over whether limiting secondary-market prices will improve affordability for fans or instead reduce competition while leaving the primary-ticketing companies that establish original ticket prices largely unrestricted.

## Industry Groups Defend Resale Restrictions

The organizations filing the briefs include the American Federation of Musicians, Artist Rights Alliance, Fan Alliance, Future of Music Coalition, Music Artists Coalition, Music Managers Forum-US, National Independent Talent Organization (NITO), Recording Academy, SAG-AFTRA and Songwriters of North America.

Their filings support the District in separate lawsuits brought by the Ticket Policy Forum, whose members include SeatGeek, Vivid Seats, Gametime and TickPick, and the National Association of Ticketing Professionals, which is joined by ticket brokers ASC Ticket Company and Stage Front Tickets.

Both lawsuits seek preliminary injunctions that would prevent enforcement of the law while constitutional challenges proceed.

As [Digital Music News reported](https://www.digitalmusicnews.com/2026/11/18/dc-resale-act-defense-from-major-industry-bodies/?utm_source=TicketNews&amp;utm_medium=content), the supporting organizations contend that professional ticket brokers frequently purchase tickets in bulk, exploit limited inventory and profit from substantial resale markups that do not benefit the artists or workers responsible for the events.

The coalition argues that resale price caps can discourage speculative purchasing, reduce financial incentives for ticket-buying bots and prevent resellers from capturing revenue that could otherwise remain with artists, venues and their audiences.

“The D.C. Council saw predatory resellers taking advantage of fans,” Music Artists Coalition Executive Director Ron Gubitz said in a statement accompanying the filings.

NITO Executive Director Nathaniel Marro similarly characterized the legislation as an important demonstration of resale regulation, particularly because federal lawmakers and their staff could experience the law’s effects firsthand in Washington.

The organizations also challenge one of the central economic arguments advanced by the plaintiffs: that independent secondary marketplaces provide competitive pressure against Ticketmaster.

According to the amici, resale activity takes place after venues have selected their primary-ticketing providers, meaning brokers and exchanges do not directly compete for the exclusive venue contracts underlying Ticketmaster’s dominance in major concert ticketing.

They further argue that unrestricted resale can provide additional opportunities for Ticketmaster to collect fees when tickets originally sold through its platform are subsequently resold through its own marketplace.

The briefs cite existing resale restrictions in Maine and Vermont, along with an example from Western Australia in which an exchange reportedly handled more than 4,000 ticket resales for sold-out Coldplay concerts under a comparable price cap.

Attorney Katherine Van Dyck of KVD Strategies, who prepared the filings, also pointed to the long history of ticket resale regulation and previous court decisions upholding restrictions on ticket markups.

## Azoff’s Music Artists Coalition and the Wider Industry Campaign

The Music Artists Coalition’s participation is noteworthy because of its leadership and established role in the concert industry’s efforts to advance resale restrictions.

MAC [identifies Irving Azoff among its founders](https://www.musicartistscoalition.com/about?utm_source=TicketNews&amp;utm_medium=content), alongside a roster of prominent artist managers, attorneys and music executives. Azoff also remains a member of the organization’s board.

The organization was established in 2019 to advocate for musicians’ economic interests, including compensation, intellectual property rights and greater artist influence over industry policy.

Although its membership includes numerous well-known performers and songwriters, its leadership also draws heavily from the artist-management business, including individuals with longstanding relationships with Live Nation and other major entertainment companies.

MAC’s [current board roster](https://www.musicartistscoalition.com/who-we-are?utm_source=TicketNews&amp;utm_medium=content) includes Coran Capshaw, founder of Red Light Management, whose venue and festival interests have partnered extensively with Live Nation; Ali Harnell, a longtime Live Nation executive; and Susan Genco, an executive at the Azoff Company.

Those relationships have previously drawn scrutiny from consumer advocates.

In [comments concerning the District’s ticket legislation](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-119shrg64113/pdf/CHRG-119shrg64113.pdf?utm_source=TicketNews&amp;utm_medium=content), subsequently included in a U.S. Senate hearing record, the National Consumers League raised questions about the affiliations of organizations backing the RESALE Act.

The consumer group argued that MAC’s description of itself as artist-driven did not adequately communicate the business relationships of several board members with Live Nation and related entertainment companies.

[TicketNews examined those connections in earlier coverage](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/05/irving-azoff-aligned-music-artists-coalition-pushes-survey-backing-resale-caps-at-core-of-industry-lobbying-efforts/?utm_source=TicketNews&amp;utm_medium=content) of MAC’s polling and lobbying efforts supporting resale caps and restrictions on speculative ticketing.

Azoff’s own business history adds another dimension.

Before the 2010 merger of Live Nation and Ticketmaster, Azoff served as chairman and CEO of Ticketmaster Entertainment. He subsequently became executive chairman of the combined company, departing in 2012.

He later co-founded Oak View Group, the arena development and venue management company whose relationship with Live Nation and Ticketmaster has figured prominently in federal antitrust proceedings. An October 2026 securities filing confirms that Azoff continues to serve on OVG’s board.

The Department of Justice’s proposed 2026 settlement with Live Nation and Ticketmaster specifically addresses a 2022 ticketing incentive agreement with OVG. Federal documents describe payments intended to encourage OVG to advocate for Ticketmaster at venues it managed, including an upfront $20 million payment.

The [proposed settlement terms](https://www.justice.gov/atr/media/1446036/dl?inline=&amp;utm_source=TicketNews&amp;utm_medium=content) require the agreement’s termination and give certain affected venues an opportunity to reconsider their primary-ticketing contracts.

These business connections do not establish that Live Nation or OVG directed MAC’s participation in the D.C. litigation. They do, however, illustrate the close relationships among portions of the concert industry’s artist-management, venue-management and ticketing businesses.

Live Nation itself publicly supported passage of the D.C. RESALE Act and has advocated comparable restrictions in other jurisdictions.

The same general policy goals have been promoted through the broader Fix the Tix campaign, which includes organizations representing independent venues and artists.

That alignment is not universal across every industry issue. Independent venue organizations, including groups supporting resale restrictions, have also criticized Live Nation’s market power and aspects of the federal antitrust settlement.

Nevertheless, the coalition’s participation demonstrates the breadth of industry support for limiting independent resale markups, even as the economic consequences remain disputed.

## The Debate Over Pricing and Competition

The [RESALE Act](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/laws/26-181?utm_source=TicketNews&amp;utm_medium=content), enacted in September and scheduled to become applicable Jan. 1, 2027, generally limits covered ticket resales to 110% of the original purchase price, including the fees and taxes paid in the first transaction.

Secondary marketplaces may charge an additional service fee of up to 10% of that original total. The law also imposes licensing requirements on higher-volume resellers, prohibits speculative ticket listings, addresses automated ticket purchasing and requires disclosure of original ticket prices.

Some of those provisions, particularly restrictions on fraud and speculative listings, address consumer-protection concerns that extend beyond the debate over price caps.

However, the legislation’s most consequential pricing restrictions apply to resale transactions.

**Original ticket prices, including those established through dynamic or demand-based pricing, are not subject to equivalent limits.**

As [TicketNews reported during the legislative process](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/07/washington-d-c-passes-industry-backed-price-cap-bill-stripping-primary-oversight-in-last-minute-amendment/?utm_source=TicketNews&amp;utm_medium=content), the D.C. Council removed provisions that would have allowed broader oversight of primary-market ticketing fees before approving the final legislation.

That distinction is central to the lawsuits and the economic debate surrounding the measure.

Supporters argue that resale markups represent an additional cost imposed by intermediaries, and that limiting those markups protects fans while preserving artists’ ability to establish original ticket prices.

Opponents contend that a resale cap does not necessarily make tickets more affordable because it leaves the initial pricing decisions untouched.

They also argue that reducing the economic viability of independent resale marketplaces could strengthen the competitive position of primary-ticketing companies that already control ticket inventory, delivery systems and, in many cases, authorized resale channels.

The plaintiffs have warned that certain provisions could make it difficult to operate regulated resale exchanges, particularly when sellers lack reliable information about the original ticket price or are unable to recover their acquisition costs.

Those concerns are not limited to the commercial interests of ticket brokers.

A [2018 Government Accountability Office report](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-18-347?utm_source=TicketNews&amp;utm_medium=content) found that state resale price caps had often proven difficult to enforce. The report also discussed an earlier New York government study that found no definitive evidence that resale caps produced lower secondary-market prices or greater ticket availability.

More recent academic research similarly suggests that the relationship between resale caps and consumer welfare is complicated.

In a [study published in *Marketing Science*](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.2023.0623?utm_source=TicketNews&amp;utm_medium=content), researchers Da He and Song Lin modeled price caps imposed by ticket resale platforms and found that sufficiently restrictive limits could reduce seller participation and competition, potentially resulting in higher average transaction prices.

That study examined platform-imposed restrictions rather than D.C.’s specific law, so its conclusions do not establish what will happen when the RESALE Act takes effect. It does challenge the assumption that reducing permitted resale markups necessarily reduces consumers’ overall costs.

The competitive implications are particularly significant in a market where Ticketmaster’s primary-ticketing dominance has already been the subject of federal and state antitrust proceedings.

Ticketmaster itself operates a secondary marketplace, and the D.C. resale cap would apply to covered transactions on that platform as well. The law does not automatically give Ticketmaster exclusive resale rights.

However, critics argue that restrictions making independent exchanges less competitive could increase consumers’ reliance on issuer-controlled systems, especially where ticket transfers, delivery timing or authorized resale options are controlled by the original ticketing provider.

In that scenario, primary sellers could retain their existing ability to set initial prices while gaining additional influence over where and how tickets are subsequently resold.

Whether those effects materialize would depend on marketplace participation, ticket availability, enforcement and the willingness of consumers and sellers to use alternative channels.

## Court Challenges Continue Ahead of January Deadline

The industry organizations’ filings arrive as the District defends the law against constitutional challenges involving interstate commerce, due process, compelled disclosures and the scope of local regulatory authority.

Attorneys for the District have argued that the plaintiffs are attempting to overturn a legitimate legislative policy decision and have not established the grounds necessary for a preliminary injunction.

The plaintiffs maintain that the restrictions could cause lasting economic harm, interfere with lawful interstate transactions and push ticket sales into channels with fewer consumer protections.

Both lawsuits are before U.S. District Judge Carl J. Nichols, with a hearing on the preliminary injunction requests scheduled for Nov. 19.

The immediate question is whether the court will allow the law’s resale restrictions to take effect Jan. 1 while the broader litigation continues.

Beyond that decision, the dispute is likely to draw attention to a larger question confronting ticketing regulators nationwide: whether controlling secondary-market markups can meaningfully improve ticket affordability without also addressing original ticket prices and the competitive power of the companies responsible for distributing them.
