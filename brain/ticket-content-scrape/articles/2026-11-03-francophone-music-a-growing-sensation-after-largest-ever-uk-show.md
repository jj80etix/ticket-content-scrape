# Francophone music ‘a growing sensation’ after largest ever UK show

## Summary

Congolese star Fally Ipupa played the largest-ever Francophone concerts in UK history with two sold-out nights at The O2 in late October, part of a broader rise in international demand for Francophone African music, according to his London representative Rob Hallett. The O2 says 2026 is its most linguistically diverse year yet, with 18 languages represented on its stage, up nearly 30% since 2024.

### Key Points
- Ipupa's two O2 shows set a UK record for Congolese/Francophone artists; he previously sold out Paris' Stade de France and set an earlier UK record at Wembley Arena in 2023.
- Growth is attributed to years of grassroots touring plus rising crossover appeal as UK audiences diversify.
- Ipupa has European dates booked in Brussels in December and a 2027 festival run across France, Belgium and Spain.
- His representative frames the trend as part of a broader "global music" wave alongside Spanish-language and Afrobeats breakthroughs.

## Francophone music ‘a growing sensation’ after largest ever UK show

Congolese star Fally Ipupa played the largest UK French language shows ever last weekend with two nights at The O2

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/smush-webp/2026/11/Fally-Ipupa-The-O2-scaled.jpeg.webp)

© Michael Tubes

After Congolese artist Fally Ipupa set a record for the largest Francophone concert in UK history, the artist’s representative says French-language music is “finally getting attention” internationally.

After two consecutive nights at The O2 on 24 and 25 October, Rob Hallett, who represented Ipupa for the London dates, says the appetite for Congolese music has reached the point of supporting a run of European festival slots for Ipupa.

“It’s a growing sensation. Francophone music is finally getting attention,” he tells *IQ.*

The concerts also came as The O2 is experiencing its [most linguistically diverse year ever in 2026](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/10/languages-on-the-o2-stage-triple-in-eight-years/), with 18 languages represented across the year — a nearly 30% boost since 2024 alone and 200% from 2018.

The arena’s head of programming Mark Saunders also tells *IQ: “* It was such a landmark moment for The O2 to welcome Fally Ipupa as our first Congolese headline artist. We’re so proud that The O2 could host such a significant cultural moment and help celebrate the growing influence of Francophone African music on the global stage.”

Hallett adds the benchmark-setting shows were “not an overnight sensation”, but the continuation of years of work. The London shows were marketed to mark Ipupa’s 20th anniversary as a touring artist. Hallett insists, though, that two nights at The O2 is “not the peak” of this trajectory.

“I expect next time we come to London, it’ll be bigger still because he’s slowly but surely crossing over from the Congolese community,” he says, adding that both Ipupa’s international touring cachet and the growing market for Francophone music abroad are “ascending projects”.

Promoted by Congo To Global and Kilimanjaro Live, the shows marked Ipupa’s return to the UK after setting a previous record for a Congolese headliner at OVO Wembley Arena in 2023.

Since then, Ipupa has seen growing ticket sales overseas, including top-level demand in France selling out two nights at Paris’ Stade de France in May this year.

> “ Global music is upon us”

Ipupa’s overseas live career traces back to 2007, when Hallett brought the artist to the 2,800-cap Olympia Music Hall in Paris, promoted by Gérard Drouot Productions. Now, a full European tour schedule is forming for the artist, featuring two December shows at Brussels’ 15,000-cap Ing Arena, in conjunction with Greenhouse Talent, as well as 2027 festival slots.

Explaining this growth, Hallett says: “I think we’ve come to a place in the world where language is not so much of a barrier to music.”

Asked what gave the team confidence the O2 show would sell across markets, Hallett says: “If you sold off 100,000 tickets in Paris, you’ve got a good chance of doing The O2”.

He explains the London shows came not necessarily out of a strategy to target a particularly large diaspora in the city, but rather as part of a steady rise in demand for the artist spread around Europe. “In the summer, we started building in Spain, Germany, Holland; it’s building blocks everywhere. This summer he’ll be out playing festivals, some in France, Belgium, Spain, and I’m still on the lookout for more,” he says.

Nevertheless, the London audience was predominantly Congolese, he explains, which he says highlights the importance of distinguishing between different strands of Francophone music from different French-speaking cultures around the world.

“It’s really annoying when people talk about Africa as if it’s a country,” he says. “It’s a continent in the same way as Europe and, for example, Senegalese music is as different from Congolese music as English is from German.”

Following the concerts, both Hallett and Kilimanjaro Live promoter Josh Casey paid tribute to Congo To Global founder Johnny ‘Moko’ Fataki, a longtime promoter for Ipupa, who passed away this year before the singer’s London appearance.

“Sadly, there was a Congolese promoter called Johnny Moko, from Congo To Global, who passed away before The O2 happened, who was instrumental in driving the desire and the London audience,” Hallett says.

Hallett also believes the milestone concert promises a bright future for diaspora music from across the world rising to the top level of international touring.

“ It’s growing and there’ll be more acts coming from more countries, and the way that geopolitics has changed, immigration has grown around the world and it is forming audiences everywhere,” he tells *IQ.*

He adds: “ Global music is upon us. When you get a Spanish-speaking artist like Bad Bunny selling out multiple stages, being one of the biggest-selling ticket artists in the world, then why not anyone else?”
