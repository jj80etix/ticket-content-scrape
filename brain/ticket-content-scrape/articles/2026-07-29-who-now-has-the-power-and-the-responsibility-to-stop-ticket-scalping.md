# Who now has the power and the responsibility to stop ticket scalping?

## Summary

Op-ed arguing that ticket scalping is fundamentally an identity-and-acquisition problem, not a resale problem, and that primary ticketing platforms are the only stakeholders positioned to fix it. It walks through why partial fixes (resale price caps, AI fraud detection, dynamic pricing, closed-loop ticketing alone) each fail on their own, then proposes a three-part solution: verified digital identity at purchase (EUDI Wallet / OpenID4VC standards), closed-loop ticket control through entry, and face-value resale within the primary platform itself.

### Key Points
- Purchase-limit evasion via multi-account bot/AI-agent purchasing is framed as the root cause that "supplies" the secondary market, not merely fails to stop it.
- Resale price caps are argued to push scalping off-platform into an untraceable black market rather than eliminating it.
- Cites Sweden's BankID (99.7% population penetration) as evidence digital identity doesn't slow consumer adoption, and Greece's Gov.gr Wallet passport-scan flow as a model for international visitors.
- Claims verified attendee data is worth ~11x more to a primary platform's fan database than anonymous bot-driven resale turnover, reframing the "resale fee revenue" objection.
- Proposes primary platforms own all three legs: digital identity (who), closed-loop ticketing (ownership/transfer/entry), and in-platform face-value resale (legitimate flexibility).

### Highlights
- Positions AI purchasing agents as a growing threat: indistinguishable from bots without verified identity behind them, an "asymmetric" fight fraud detection alone can't win.
- Frames resale caps (e.g., 10% limits already adopted in some jurisdictions) as addressing symptoms, not the acquisition-side cause.
- Argues dynamic pricing "cannot responsibly coexist" with bot-driven demand inflation — it would need bots removed first.

### Industry Problem
Fans lose access at face value because purchase limits are enforceable only in theory (per-account, not per-person), letting coordinated buyers — increasingly AI-driven — drain primary inventory within minutes and resell it at a markup; this is core Q2 patron pain and Q6/Q11 secondary-market structure.

### Proposed Solution
The article's own solution — identity-gated purchase limits, closed-loop tickets, and primary-side face-value resale — maps directly onto what an independent primary platform like Etix could build: real per-person purchase-limit enforcement and an in-platform resale/sell-back feature, positioned ahead of AI-agent-driven bot pressure rather than reacting to it after the fact.

**Three things are happening in ticketing at the same time, and together they are reshaping the industry’s oldest unsolved problem: ticket scalping.**

AI is enabling scalping bots and automated purchasing agents to behave more like real buyers. Tickets are distributed across more partners and channels than ever, multiplying the points where control can break down. And the EUDI Wallet and OpenID4VC standards are making verified digital identity available at a global scale for the first time.

Put those three together and the industry finds itself in an unusual position: the problem is getting bigger, but so are the tools to solve it. For years, ticketing has treated scalping as a resale problem, managed with price caps and after-the-fact enforcement. That framing is starting to look outdated. Who now has the power and the responsibility to stop ticket scalping?

### Will the genuine fan accept AI-driven scalping bots?

AI purchasing agents will need to act on behalf of a verified person [if they are to be distinguished](https://www.theticketingbusiness.com/2026/06/where-will-ai-take-ticketing/) from scalping bots. Genuine fans will need that same verified identity too. If digital identity is not introduced in ticketing, there is a real risk that scalping explodes as AI-based scalping bots spread. Will genuine fans accept that, when digital identity is already adopted across society, in both public and private services?

### How the primary market creates, or prevents, the secondary market

This is the part of the problem the industry least likes to look at directly.

Follow the chain. Purchase limits are set per account. Scalpers create many accounts. Bots and AI-driven purchasing agents use those accounts to buy at volume. Inventory disappears from the primary market within minutes and resurfaces on resale platforms within hours. Fans who missed the initial sale pay multiples of face value for the same tickets they were denied the first time.

Each step is well understood in isolation. Together, they point to an uncomfortable conclusion: a primary market without enforceable, person-based purchase limits does not merely fail to stop the secondary market. It supplies it.

### The risk of an unfair commercial practice

The closest parallel is money laundering. If a bank fails to apply the required KYC controls, allows prohibited transactions to pass through its system and profits from them, it cannot place all responsibility on the customer carrying out the transactions. The bank controls onboarding, the transaction system and the preventive tools.

The same principle applies to primary ticketing. If a platform controls account registration, purchase limits, primary sale and resale, allows bots to create artificial scarcity and then earns additional fees when those tickets return to its own resale marketplace, it cannot treat the resulting scalping as someone else’s problem.

### Why resale caps and other partial fixes fall short

Several measures are commonly proposed as the answer to scalping. Each addresses a symptom rather than the cause.

**Resale price caps,** including the 10% limits adopted for certain events in some jurisdictions, restrict the scalper’s declared profit. They do nothing to stop bots, multi-account purchasing or bulk acquisition at the point of sale. In the worst case, resale caps push trading off-platform into social media groups, private payments and overseas marketplaces. The result is a hidden black market with no visibility and no consumer protection at all. Nor do they touch the deeper problem described above: the platform still earns fees on tickets it let bots capture through weak identity verification, just smaller ones. A cap on resales does not stop unfair commercial practices or ticket scalping in general. And there is a practical question regulators have yet to answer convincingly: how much police or regulatory time will realistically go into tracing thousands of transactions in a market that has simply gone underground?

At best, a cap reduces the problem. It does not fix it. That might have been good enough once, when there was no better option. Not anymore. The technology to verify every buyer and make a purchase limit real, not just declared, exists today. With digital identity, every rule, how many tickets one person can buy, when they can resell, and to whom, can actually be enforced, not just written into terms and conditions.

**AI-based fraud detection.** Without digital identity, it cannot reliably tell an AI scalping bot apart from a legitimate AI agent buying on a fan’s behalf. Both look the same: automated requests, valid payment credentials, behaviour designed to pass as human. It is also an unfair fight. A fraud detection system has to close every vulnerability in a system that keeps needing upgrades. A scalping bot only has to find one. Cybersecurity has already run into this same asymmetry and concluded that behavioural detection alone cannot win it. Only verified digital identity can stop the AI agents and bots you don’t want, while letting the ones you do want through.

**Dynamic pricing** can compress the scalper’s margin by letting primary capture more value upfront, but it does not ensure fair allocation, especially when scalping bots are the ones inflating the demand those high prices are based on. The genuine fan pays for it either way. When bots are driving that demand, dynamic pricing does not fix the unfair commercial practice already described here. It makes it worse. Dynamic pricing can only be used responsibly once scalping bots have been removed from the equation. It cannot responsibly coexist with them.

**Closed-loop ticketing on its own** controls a ticket’s transfer and use after purchase, but does not stop the same person buying through multiple accounts in the first place. Closed loop does not stop bots from capturing inventory through multiple accounts before the ticket ever enters the loop.

So none of the above, taken in isolation, can really stop ticket scalping without digital identity in primary.

### The only complete solution to ticket scalping

A durable solution has to work at three points, not one.

**Digital identity** establishes who is exercising the purchasing right, making purchase limits enforceable per person.

**Closed-loop ticketing** controls the ticket’s ownership, transfer and use afterwards. A ticket that cannot leave the system cannot feed an unauthorised resale market. To be certain who is actually presenting the ticket at the gate, entry itself can be activated with digital identity, closing the loop from purchase all the way to admission. This is the core of an identity-based ticketing system: identity represents the ticket, not an asset like a PDF.

**Resale within the primary market,** with no bots involved, ideally as face-value resale or a sell-back mechanism, gives genuine consumers flexibility without recreating the speculative marketplace that identity and closed-loop controls are designed to eliminate. This is a service for genuine fans with verified identities, not for AI-based scalping bots.

### Primary ticketing systems are the only stakeholders that can stop ticket scalping

These three functions, verified identity, closed-loop control and primary resale, can only be implemented by primary ticketing systems. That is why the ultimate responsibility for fixing ticket scalping and counterfeit tickets sits with them.

### Two objections worth answering

The evidence points the other way. In Sweden, BankID is used by more than [8.7 million people](https://www.bankid.com/en/news-press/press/statistics), a penetration rate of 99.7% of the population. Swish, built on BankID authentication, was used by 91% of Swedes in the past month, according to Sweden’s [central bank](https://www.riksbank.se/en-gb/payments--cash/payments-in-sweden/payments-report-2026/the-riksbanks-responsibility-for-payments/the-riksbanks-mandate-in-the-area-of-payments/). Digital identity did not slow that adoption down. It drove it, by turning identification into a single, familiar in-app confirmation instead of a separate, cumbersome process.

‘ **We have international guests’.** Digital identity is not necessarily a closed, domestic system. The EUDI Wallet, covering 27 European member states, is being built around the global standards OpenID4VCI and OpenID4VP. OpenID4VP is also supported by Google Wallet, while the MOSIP ecosystem provides open-source digital identity infrastructure and wallet technology based on the same broader verifiable-credential architecture. International visitors are not outside this development; cross-border interoperability is one of its central purposes. And where a visitor’s digital identity is not yet interoperable with the local system, a passport or national ID card can be used instead, since the ticket is always tied to a verified identity rather than to one specific credential format. Greece already does this in practice: foreign visitors without a Greek digital ID can verify their stadium ticket in the Gov.gr Wallet app by scanning their passport instead.

Together, the logic is straightforward: digital identity controls acquisition, closed-loop ticketing controls ownership, business rules and transfer, and primary resale preserves legitimate consumer flexibility. None of the three is new in isolation; versions of each already exist across the industry. What has been missing is a single, enforceable system that combines all three, rather than partial fixes bolted onto an unchanged purchasing model.

### ‘But we make so much money on resale fees’

A primary platform can already earn three fees on the same ticket when scalping bots are allowed: once at first sale, once when the scalping bot resells it, and again when the new buyer completes the purchase. One issue with this model is that scalping bots are anonymous, which reduces the value of primary’s customer database.

A comparison of one million tickets sold at the same price and marketing spend found that verified, near-complete attendee data produced a fan database worth roughly [eleven times more](https://www.theticketingbusiness.com/2026/05/ticketings-golden-nugget-verified-attendee-data/) than a conventional system built on partial, unverified records. For the primary ticketing system, the value of digital identity is simply far higher than the value of letting scalping bots buy and resell tickets on its own platform, and the legal exposure of allowing bots when digital identity is readily available only adds to that case.

The real choice is not between resale fees and digital identity, but between resale revenue built on bot-driven turnover and resale revenue built on legitimate customer behaviour, combined with a fan database worth many times more. Platforms that keep choosing the former risk both: growing exposure to unfair commercial practice claims, and a customer database worth a fraction of what it could be, a risk no amount of scalping-bot resale fees can offset.

Ticket scalping is not primarily a resale problem. It is an identity, acquisition and lifecycle-control problem. The industry now has the technical means to fix it. The remaining question is whether promoters, primary platforms and regulators are prepared to make verified purchasing rights, controlled ticket lifecycles and fair primary resale part of the market’s basic infrastructure.
