# UK Steps Up Work on Ticket Resale Cap Bill as Officials Consider Black Market Risks

## Summary

The UK government has assembled a dedicated civil-service bill team to draft its ticket-resale legislation, which would cap resale prices at original cost (including fees) and impose a separate fee cap on resale platforms, enforced by the Competition and Markets Authority with penalties up to 10% of global turnover. The newest wrinkle is official concern that touts and marketplaces could relocate offshore to keep reaching UK consumers while evading the cap, a risk the draft bill has not yet publicly addressed. Major secondary marketplaces (Viagogo, StubHub) continue to warn the cap will push transactions to unregulated channels, increase fraud, and could make full-service resale marketplaces uneconomic in the UK.

### Key Points
- DCMS has staffed up a bill team; draft legislation is expected this parliamentary session, per The Guardian (Sept 26).
- Cap structure: resale price limited to original cost paid (incl. fees); separate fee cap for resale platforms; CMA enforcement with fines up to 10% of global turnover.
- Government is now weighing how to stop resellers/marketplaces from basing offshore to dodge UK enforcement — no mechanism specified yet.
- The bill does not require ownership verification of tickets before listing, which could limit its effect on speculative (not-yet-owned) ticket listings.
- StubHub's UK CEO has said a hard price cap could force it to drop services like buyer guarantees or exit the UK market; Viagogo argues caps increase fraud and reduce competition.
- Government's own model estimates the cap saves fans £112M/year and lowers average resale price by £37, but doesn't quantify migration to informal/offshore channels.

### Highlights
- CMA enforcement penalty: up to 10% of global turnover for violations.
- Estimated fan savings: £112M/year; average resale price reduction: £37 (per government model).
- No ownership-verification requirement in current policy — narrows the bill's effect on speculative listings.
- Offshore-enforcement mechanism still undecided; draft bill publication is the next milestone to watch.

### Industry Problem
Resale price caps aimed at protecting fans create a structural loophole: sellers and marketplaces with little UK physical presence may simply move offshore, leaving compliant platforms constrained while harder-to-police channels absorb the same transactions — a compliance and enforcement-design problem regulators haven't yet solved.

### Proposed Solution
The bill itself proposes no offshore-enforcement mechanism yet, only platform-facing compliance obligations for marketplaces "facilitating" UK sales. For Etix, the trigger to watch is whether the draft bill (once published) adds ownership-verification or cross-border enforcement requirements — either would materially raise integration/compliance burden for any resale-adjacent product; until then, this stays a monitoring item rather than a build/improve trigger.

The UK government has intensified work on legislation that would effectively eliminate legal independent ticket resale marketplaces, assembling a dedicated bill team as officials consider how to prevent ticket sellers and marketplaces from moving offshore to evade the proposed restrictions.

The [Guardian reported Saturday](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/sep/26/work-intensifying-uk-laws-stamp-out-concert-ticket-touts) that the Department for Culture, Media and Sport has put together a sizeable group of civil servants to draft the policy and help move it through Parliament. Government officials told the newspaper that draft legislation will be brought forward during the current parliamentary session.

That timetable is consistent with the government’s King’s Speech materials from earlier this year, which included a Draft Ticket Tout Ban Bill and said it would be published for pre-legislative scrutiny. The latest reporting provides a clearer indication that drafting work is now well underway after [questions this spring about how quickly the proposal would advance](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/05/uk-ticket-resale-price-cap-reportedly-pushed-back-as-debate-over-consumer-harm-intensifies/).

Under the framework [previously announced by the government](https://www.ticketnews.com/2025/11/report-uk-to-announce-ticket-resale-price-caps-at-face-value/), tickets for live events would generally be barred from resale above their original cost, including unavoidable fees paid during the initial purchase. Resale marketplaces would also face a separate cap on service fees, while sellers would be prohibited from reselling more tickets than they were entitled to purchase from the primary seller.

## Offshore Enforcement Moves to Center of Debate

The newest wrinkle is the government’s focus on what happens if professional resellers or marketplaces attempt to operate beyond the direct reach of UK enforcement.

Sources told The Guardian that officials are considering ways to address the risk that touts and resale platforms could base themselves offshore while continuing to reach British consumers. The report did not identify what mechanism the draft legislation may use to address that problem.

That question could prove central to whether the cap changes where resale takes place rather than simply what prices are displayed on major UK-facing marketplaces.

The government acknowledged similar concerns during its consultation process. Opponents of a cap warned that restricting prices on regulated resale sites could shift transactions toward social-media platforms and other informal channels where payment guarantees, replacement tickets and other marketplace protections may be weaker or nonexistent. The government said its platform obligations are intended to apply broadly to businesses facilitating ticket resale to UK consumers and that marketplaces unwilling to comply should prevent live-event tickets from being sold through their services.

The unresolved issue is how effectively those requirements can be imposed when a seller, website or intermediary has little physical presence in Britain.

## What the Bill Could Mean for Speculative Tickets

The legislation could also materially change the economics surrounding speculative ticket listings — offers posted by sellers who do not yet possess the advertised ticket and expect to obtain one after a buyer places an order.

The Guardian cited research produced by the industry-backed anti-resale Fan Fair Alliance that it said identified apparent speculative listings among tickets offered for events at smaller Manchester venues. Viagogo disputed aspects of the group’s broader analysis and said it prohibits speculative listings, complies with UK law and found that actual transaction prices were considerably lower than some of the advertised asking prices highlighted by campaigners.

StubHub has likewise said that speculative selling is not permitted on its platform and that listings that fail to comply with its requirements are removed.

A strict original-cost cap could substantially reduce the potential upside to the practice: a seller who does not own a ticket would have little legal room to sell it at a high price and later acquire it more cheaply. Requiring platforms to verify the original purchase price before permitting a capped resale could create an additional hurdle.

But the government’s existing policy stops short of requiring platforms to verify that a seller actually owns the ticket before it is listed. Officials concluded following consultation that comprehensive ownership verification could be difficult without extensive data sharing between primary sellers and independent resale platforms. Instead, the government opted for a narrower strict obligation centered on compliance with the resale-price limit.

That will be worth watching when the draft bill is published. An explicit prohibition or ownership-verification requirement would address speculative listings differently than a system relying principally on price controls to remove their financial incentive.

## Marketplaces Continue to Warn of Fraud and Reduced Competition

Major secondary marketplaces have opposed the government’s price-cap approach while supporting stronger enforcement against fraud and unlawful selling.

Viagogo has argued that restricting regulated resale prices could move transactions to social media and other less protected channels while strengthening the position of primary ticketing companies. In written evidence submitted to Parliament this year, the company argued that the policy could increase fraud and reduce competition.

StubHub International has similarly warned that price caps could make operating a full-service resale marketplace uneconomic. Chief Executive Bob Kupbens previously told the Financial Times that a cap could prevent the company from maintaining services such as its ticket guarantee and customer support and [could ultimately force it to leave the UK market](https://www.ticketnews.com/2025/06/stubhub-warns-it-may-quit-u-k-market-if-resale-price-cap-is-adopted/). StubHub has also cited research arguing that ticket-fraud rates are higher in jurisdictions that restrict resale prices.

The government considered those arguments during its consultation. Its response acknowledged potential unintended consequences including transactions moving to less regulated channels, reduced competition among resale platforms and possible effects on primary-market pricing. Ministers nevertheless concluded that eliminating the profit incentive for professional touting outweighed those risks and said the effects would be reviewed after implementation.

[TicketNews previously examined the government’s own economic model](https://www.ticketnews.com/2025/12/uk-government-defends-ticket-resale-price-cap-with-fan-savings-model-while-critics-say-black-market-risks-go-largely-uncounted/), which estimates the policy would reduce what fans spend on live-event tickets by £112 million annually and lower the average resale price by £37, while also projecting fewer tickets sold through resale channels. That model does not quantify every potential effect of transactions migrating to informal markets.

## What Comes Next

The immediate milestone is publication of the draft bill.

The King’s Speech briefing committed the government to a draft measure rather than legislation ready for immediate enactment. That means the proposal is expected to undergo pre-legislative scrutiny before a final bill proceeds through Parliament, leaving significant details open to revision.

Among the provisions worth watching will be the precise definition of “original cost,” the level and structure of the resale-platform fee cap, how platforms will be expected to verify original prices, whether the bill adds specific restrictions on speculative listings, and what enforcement tools officials propose for websites or sellers operating outside the UK.

For consumers, another question is where legitimate resale inventory goes if major marketplaces reduce their UK operations or change their business models. For the secondary-ticketing industry, the more immediate issue is whether lawmakers can design a rule that applies effectively across borders rather than leaving compliant marketplaces constrained while transactions migrate to harder-to-police channels.

The government says the draft legislation will be introduced during the current parliamentary session.
