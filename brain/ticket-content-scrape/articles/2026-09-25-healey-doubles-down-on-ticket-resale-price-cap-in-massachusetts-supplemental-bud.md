# Healey Doubles Down on Ticket Resale Price Cap in Massachusetts Supplemental Budget

## Summary

Massachusetts Gov. Maura Healey has opened a second legislative path for her "Great Divide Act" ticket-resale restrictions by filing H.5679 as part of the state's supplemental budget, running in parallel with a different resale-cap version already passed by the Senate. Both versions would cap non-sporting resale prices at 110% of original price and ban speculative listings, but diverge on a 10% resale-marketplace fee cap, definitions of "original ticket price," venue-size exemptions and who can authorize above-cap resale.

### Highlights
- H.5679, filed Sept. 23 and referred to House Ways and Means Sept. 24, adds a 10% cap on resale-marketplace listing/service fees not present in the Senate-passed text.
- Neither version defines "original ticket price" precisely — Healey's letter calls it "face value," a term absent from the operative bill language — raising compliance-verification questions similar to those seen in Ontario's price-cap rollout.
- The Senate version exempts venues with ≤1,000 capacity from the "live event" definition; Healey's H.5679 has no comparable carve-out.
- Ticket Policy Forum (StubHub, SeatGeek, Vivid Seats, TickPick, Gametime, Events Ticket Center) is publicly opposing the bill, citing an August BTS Gillette Stadium case where resale prices (SeatGeek $24, StubHub $20) undercut Ticketmaster's primary price ($135.20).

### Industry Problem
Massachusetts is testing a resale-only price-cap regime that leaves primary-market dynamic pricing and fees untouched, while critics argue it could push transactions to unregulated social/peer channels and entrench the dominant primary platform's pricing power — a live case study in how junk-fee and price-cap regulation is evolving state-by-state ahead of any federal standard.

### Proposed Solution
Nothing in the bill directly benefits an independent primary platform, but the fee-cap and definitional gaps (no clear "original ticket price" standard) are exactly the kind of compliance ambiguity Etix should track now, since any enacted version will require resale/fee-cap logic wherever Etix touches secondary distribution. Monitor which version (or reconciled hybrid) advances out of the House-Senate conference on H.5576/S.3228, and watch Ontario's price-cap rollout as a preview of implementation friction.

Massachusetts Gov. Maura Healey has opened a second legislative path for sweeping ticket resale restrictions, placing her “Great Divide Act” into the state’s fiscal 2026 closeout supplemental budget while lawmakers continue negotiating a different version of the policy already passed by the state Senate.

Healey [filed H.5679](https://malegislature.gov/Bills/194/H5679) on Sept. 23. Section 27 would generally cap resale prices for non-sporting live events at 110% of the original ticket price, prohibit speculative ticket listings and impose a 10% cap on charges for listing tickets or providing a resale marketplace. The bill was referred to the House Committee on Ways and Means on Sept. 24, with no subsequent action posted as of Friday.

The renewed push comes amid a [broader live-entertainment campaign](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/08/resale-industry-confronts-coordinated-statehouse-campaign-to-restrict-ticket-competition/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) to restrict ticket resale prices. Live Nation and Ticketmaster have repeatedly advocated for resale price caps and greater artist control over secondary sales, while the Massachusetts proposal has drawn organized support from artist, management and venue groups including the Music Artists Coalition, National Independent Talent Organization and Future of Music Coalition, along with more than 40 artists with New England ties, including Noah Kahan, Dropkick Murphys and Aimee Mann.

When Healey originally unveiled the Great Divide Act in July, a Live Nation spokesperson told the Boston Globe that the company supported efforts to cap resale prices, although the company did not specifically endorse Healey’s legislation. At that same July rollout, Healey said her office had reached out to third-party ticket resellers about the proposed language, according to the Boston Globe.

Public materials reviewed by TicketNews do not identify the full range of ticketing-industry stakeholders consulted in developing H.5679, including which primary ticketing companies, promoters, venues, artist-management organizations or consumer groups participated.

Critics of resale price controls argue the policy would regulate prices only after the original ticket has been sold, while leaving primary-market dynamic pricing and most primary-market fees untouched. They also contend that such price controls are regularly shown to reduce inventory available through regulated resale marketplaces, push transactions toward private or social-media channels with fewer consumer protections and strengthen the position of primary ticketing companies or authorized resale channels.

The Healey administration rejects that criticism, arguing that the Great Divide Act will “lower costs for fans and crack down on outrageous resale prices and fees that are putting concerts and other live events out of reach,” according to a statement provided to TicketNews by Gov. Healey’s press secretary, Jacqui Manning.

Those competing interests are particularly notable in Massachusetts, where Attorney General Andrea Campbell has separately pursued Live Nation and Ticketmaster over their market power. A federal jury in April found Live Nation liable under state and federal antitrust law, although Live Nation continues to challenge the verdict and related remedies remain unresolved.

## Two Versions Would Set Different Ticket Rules

At their core, both proposals would generally prohibit tickets covered by the law from being resold for more than 110% of their original price, while exempting sporting-event tickets from that price ceiling. Both also prohibit speculative listings for tickets a ticket business does not possess or have under contract to receive and restrict misleading claims about marketplace affiliation and ticket availability.

But the similarities end well short of identical legislation.

Most notably, Healey’s H.5679 includes a separate provision barring a ticket business from charging more than 10% of the ticket’s listed price for listing it for resale or providing a resale marketplace. The Senate-passed text contains no corresponding 10% marketplace fee limit.

That means a fee restriction absent from the final Senate version is now again before lawmakers through the governor’s supplemental budget.

The 10% fee cap is not new to the supplemental-budget version. Healey’s original July proposal also limited resale-marketplace service fees to 10% of the listed resale price. The public announcement accompanying that proposal said many resale platforms charge service fees of roughly 20% or more and described the cap as a way to reduce those charges.

The Senate language specifically defines the “original ticket price” as the price at which a ticket was first sold to the public, including primary-market service charges, fees and surcharges but excluding taxes. It then says the resale price, exclusive of secondary service charges, fees or surcharges, cannot exceed 110% of that amount.

Healey’s [Section 27](https://malegislature.gov/Bills/194/H5679.pdf) contains no equivalent definition. It says only that a ticket may not be resold for more than 110% of “the price of the original ticket” and separately establishes the 10% marketplace fee cap. In her transmittal letter, Healey describes the benchmark as the ticket’s “face value,” terminology that does not appear in the operative price-cap language.

On its face, H.5679 treats the resale-price ceiling and marketplace-fee limit as separate restrictions: one caps the price at which a ticket may be resold, while another limits charges for listing the ticket or providing a resale marketplace.

The bill still does not define “the price of the original ticket,” however, leaving unanswered whether mandatory primary-market service charges and fees count toward that benchmark. It also does not define how an independent marketplace would verify the controlling original price when tickets for the same event may initially have sold at different prices, leading to significant potential compliance issues – similar to those which have arisen during the chaotic rollout of a price cap regime in Ontario over the last several months.

The bills also differ in who can authorize resale above the cap.

Healey’s version permits above-cap resale where a ticket is covered by a written contract with the ticket issuer allowing it. The Senate version instead requires explicit written permission from both the entertainer and venue. Section 27 does not separately define “ticket issuer.”

The exception is contract-specific on its face: it applies when a written contract with the ticket issuer allows a ticket to be resold above the 110% threshold. H.5679 does not provide a categorical exemption for any named resale marketplace. At the same time, because Section 27 does not define “ticket issuer” or specify how such agreements would operate, it leaves open whether an issuer could structure agreements authorizing certain resale channels to sell above the cap while others remain restricted.

The bill gives the Office of Consumer Affairs and Business Regulation authority to adopt regulations implementing the provision, meaning some of those details could ultimately be resolved through rulemaking.

Another difference could matter particularly to smaller venues. The Senate version excludes live events at fixed locations with capacities of 1,000 or fewer people from its definition of a covered “live event.” Healey’s version contains no comparable venue-capacity exemption.

The Senate package additionally makes several changes to Massachusetts’ existing ticket-reseller licensing laws that are not included in H.5679’s Great Divide section. Its enforcement language also differs: [the Senate text](https://malegislature.gov/Bills/194/S3228/Senate/Amendment/Text) declares violations unfair or deceptive practices under Chapter 93A, while Healey’s version expressly authorizes the Attorney General to bring a Chapter 93A action to remedy violations.

## Resale Industry Renews Opposition

The governor’s filing has also renewed opposition from businesses that would be directly affected by the proposed restrictions.

Ticket Policy Forum, a 501(c)(6) advocacy coalition whose members include StubHub, SeatGeek, Vivid Seats, TickPick, Gametime and Events Ticket Center, has emerged as one of the proposal’s most vocal opponents.

Brian Berry, Ticket Policy Forum’s executive director, [criticized the supplemental-budget language as a resale-only approach that leaves Ticketmaster’s primary-market pricing outside the proposed ceiling](https://ticketpolicyforum.org/?p=987770). Berry also argued that limiting transactions through established resale platforms could push some sales toward less-regulated social-media and person-to-person channels.

“The resale market often offers fans better deals than Ticketmaster,” Berry said in a statement Wednesday, pointing specifically to BTS’ August concerts at Gillette Stadium. “When you push legitimate ticketing companies like our members out of the market, these options to save big disappear. Ticketmaster will be the only option left standing.”

That example is one TicketNews documented in real time.

[Hours before BTS’ Aug. 5 concert in Foxborough](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/08/bts-ticket-prices-plummet-for-massachusetts-concerts-but-only-outside-ticketmaster/), Ticketmaster’s least expensive visible primary ticket was $135.20 including fees. StubHub showed tickets beginning at $20 including fees, SeatGeek at $24 and Ticket Club member pricing as low as $16. No Ticketmaster Verified Resale inventory was visible at the time.

For the second Gillette show, Ticketmaster displayed a standard-admission ticket in Section 304, Row 19 for $98.95, while SeatGeek had a ticket one row away for approximately $36 including fees.

Berry cited the concerts as evidence that resale can function as a discount market when demand softens, pointing out the side of resale that is regularly overlooked by proponents of such closed-loop regulatory systems as the price cap regime would install.

The dispute now returns to lawmakers, who have two different versions of the Great Divide Act before them through separate legislative vehicles. H.5679 remains before the House Committee on Ways and Means, while the ticket provisions contained in the Senate’s economic-development package remain part of the unresolved House-Senate conference over H.5576.

Healey’s supplemental-budget filing urges lawmakers to move quickly on the ticket provisions, but it does not resolve the substantive differences between the two versions, including the 10% marketplace-fee cap, the definition of an original ticket price, the treatment of smaller venues and the circumstances under which tickets may be resold above the proposed ceiling.

Whether lawmakers ultimately adopt Healey’s version, preserve the Senate language or reconcile the two remains unresolved as both legislative tracks continue.
