# ‘A younger generation embracing jazz’: Blue Note on opening its first UK venue

## Summary

IQ profiles Blue Note president Steven Bensusan ahead of the jazz club brand's first UK venue, opening 23 September in London's West End with a Robert Glasper residency and an opening season featuring Erykah Badu, Jamie Cullum, Nubya Garcia, and Yussef Dayes. The piece covers the brand's international expansion model (New York, Tokyo, LA, and now London), the two-room economics that let it subsidize lower ticket prices and emerging-artist bookings, and the venue's programming strategy aimed at a younger jazz audience. London bookings run through the New York flagship, with local programming led by Nick Lewis.

### Key Points
- Blue Note London opens 23 September on St Martin's Lane, becoming the ninth club under the Blue Note banner worldwide and the first company-owned (not licensed) UK outpost.
- Opening season includes Robert Glasper (creative ambassador), Erykah Badu, Jamie Cullum, Nubya Garcia, and Yussef Dayes, launching around John Coltrane's 100th birthday.
- The venue has two rooms: a 250-capacity main room (two shows nightly) and a 100-capacity "B-Side" room opening later in 2026 focused on emerging British talent.
- Bensusan says the two-room model lets fixed costs in the main room subsidize lower price points and artist risk-taking in the smaller room.
- The club uses a simple two-tier pricing structure (table price or bar price) with first-come, first-served seating within each tier, deliberately avoiding complex tiering.
- Bensusan cites a declining average audience age in Blue Note's New York club over the past five years as evidence of a broader younger-generation jazz resurgence.

## ‘A younger generation embracing jazz’: Blue Note on opening its first UK venue

Blue Note's president Steven Bensusan explains the varied economics of staging the genre and how London fits into the brand's global model

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/smush-webp/2026/08/Exterior-BN-London-HR-scaled.jpg.webp)

Blue Note London (impression) © Blue Note

As global jazz club brand Blue Note prepares to open its first UK venue in London on 23 September, becoming the ninth venue under the banner around the world, its president says “progressive” programming is at the core of its mission.

Located on St Martin’s Lane in London’s West End, the venue will open with a series of headline shows by Robert Glasper, who is also a creative ambassador to the club. Launching on the occasion of John Coltrane’s 100th birthday, the club has also revealed an opening season program including the likes of Erykah Badu, Jamie Cullum, Nubya Garcia, and Yussef Dayes.

Founded in 1981 in New York, Blue Note “ quickly established itself as the premier club in New York with artists like Sarah Vaughan, Dizzy Gillespie, Oscar Peterson, and all the legends who have played there over the years”, in the words of its president Steven Bensusan. The brand began its international expansion in 1985 with the opening of a second club in Tokyo.

The London club will be owned and operated by Blue Note, unlike some of its worldwide portfolio which are licensed to collaborators, with bookings handled through the central New York flagship club. With such global dispersal, Bensusan tells *IQ* this inevitably requires geographically specific programming tweaks, but that the London proved an attractive location due to its openness to all genres under the jazz umbrella.

“What we’re trying to do now is open in markets where we can replicate what we do in New York. New York and Tokyo are very similar. Los Angeles is very similar but maybe skews a little more R&B. Our goal with London is to do what we do in New York, LA, and Tokyo, as it’s a major market and we have the diversity to book all different subgenres of jazz and jazz‑adjacent music,” Bensusan says.

These genre possibilities mean the London location will forge its own path programming-wise, with local talent buyer and director of programming Nick Lewis taking the helm.

The new venue will boast two performance spaces. A main room with a 250 person capacity will host two shows each night, at 7pm and 9:30pm, while a second 100-cap room, known as ‘B-Side’, will open later in 2026 “with a focus on supporting emerging British talent”.

> “ There is definitely a younger generation embracing jazz and newer artists”

Bensusan said the two room model along these capacity lines, a feature seen across many jazz clubs including London’s famed Ronnie Scott’s, is a central component of the venue’s economic model, as well as its cultural mission.

“ Having two spaces definitely affects the business model because all your fixed costs are already covered by the main room. We’re hoping that this allows us to present lower ticket price points, newer artists, and take chances on artists in the smaller room because it’s not a separate venue with its own separate line of expenses and costs. We’re able to amortise those costs over the whole facility,” Bensusan told *IQ.*

And the economics of staging jazz are unique in more ways than this, he says, as set piece and equipment requirements often differ from theatre or arena touring. But Bensusan emphasises that “ when we build our Blue Note clubs, we make sure that artists don’t feel they have to compromise their set when they come into the club. The stage size is adequate, the ceiling height, the sound, the lights – everything is fixed in place,”

Blue Note London will also incorporate pop and crossover underplays in its programming strategy, which requires that “ when pop artists want to come and play an intimate jazz club, they’re also not compromising their set,” Bensusan says.

“Sometimes they’re used to touring with their boards and gear, but often they just use the house gear because it’s there, and we’re able to accommodate their needs. In general, the way we build out the Blue Note clubs allows us to bring in big‑name artists and do underplays, and we’re hoping to do a lot of that in London,” he continues.

> “ What we’re trying to do now is open in markets where we can replicate what we do in New York”

Operating this style of club also places demands on how to approach ticketing, he explains.

“ In terms of ticketing, one thing we try to do in all our clubs is avoid tiering that makes it impossible for people to get good seats. We use a very simple system: table price or bar price,” he says, noting that “the way people get better seats is simply first‑come, first‑served within their tier”.

As for the wider outlook for the genre, programming emerging artists is a strategy which aims to capitalise on a resurgent and increasingly youthful scene forming around contemporary jazz output.

“ There is definitely a younger generation embracing jazz and newer artists, and we want to capture that. In New York, for example, our average age has gone down over the past five years. We’re seeing younger audiences not just for younger‑skewing artists but even for legends like Ron Carter,” the brand’s president says.

This, he thinks, is a result of “us booking the room more progressively and bringing in younger audiences in general, which then becomes a place where they want to see what’s happening.”

“That’s what we want to achieve in London with the Blue Note and the B‑Side: exposing younger audiences to some of the bigger names as well as the younger artists,” he adds.
