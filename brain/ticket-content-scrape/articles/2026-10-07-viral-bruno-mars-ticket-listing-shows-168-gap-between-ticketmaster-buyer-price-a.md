# Viral Bruno Mars Ticket Listing Shows $168 Gap Between Ticketmaster Buyer Price and Seller Payout

## Summary

A viral TikTok showing a Bruno Mars Ticketmaster resale listing revealed a $168.30 gap between what a seller would net ($254.15 after a 15% seller fee on a $299 listing) and what a buyer actually saw ($422.45 for the identical seat). Ticketmaster says sellers set base prices and that resale fees may be shared among multiple parties and cover operational costs, but the episode lands amid an active FTC/state lawsuit alleging Ticketmaster collected $986M in resale fees and $187M in independent markups between 2019-2024 — claims a federal judge recently allowed to proceed past dismissal. It follows earlier fan complaints about primary-market prices on Mars' "The Romantic Tour" reaching $2,400.

### Key Points
- Seller listed a seat at $299, saw a $44.85 (15%) service fee deducted, netting a $254.15 payout — while buyers saw the same seat priced at $422.45, a $168.30 total spread.
- Ticketmaster attributes the gap to seller-set base price, shared resale service fees, and buyer-side charges now folded into the upfront displayed price.
- FTC's complaint against Live Nation/Ticketmaster alleges $986M in resale fees and $187M in separate markups collected 2019-2024; these remain unproven allegations.
- A federal judge last week denied Live Nation/Ticketmaster's motion to dismiss the FTC case, letting claims on ticket limits, resale activity and deceptive fee displays proceed.
- Follows earlier fan complaints about Bruno Mars primary-market prices reaching $2,400, despite Live Nation citing record-setting demand (2M+ tickets sold in a day).

### Highlights
- Concrete, documented numbers (not just allegations) showing a $168.30 spread between a reseller's net payout and buyer-facing price on a single ticket — a tangible illustration of the fee-transparency pain point regulators are litigating.
- The FTC case surviving a motion to dismiss (just one week before this story) keeps Ticketmaster's resale fee/pricing practices under active litigation risk through at least the next phase of discovery.
- Scale of alleged resale fees ($986M) and markups ($187M) over a five-year window underscores how material resale economics are to Ticketmaster's business model.

### Industry Problem
Patrons on both sides of a resale transaction — the seller netting far less than listed, the buyer paying well above that figure — experience the fee structure as opaque and feel "gross" about the spread, exactly the kind of all-in-pricing confusion the FTC's junk-fee case and rule are targeting. For a primary platform, this also illustrates the reputational/regulatory overhang any dominant player's resale practices create for the industry as a whole.

### Proposed Solution
The item itself describes no fix — only Ticketmaster's explanation of how the fees are allocated. For Etix, this is a monitor/improve signal: continue tracking the FTC v. Live Nation/Ticketmaster docket (now past motion to dismiss) for rulings that could reshape industry-wide resale fee disclosure norms, and treat full, itemized fee transparency on both buy- and sell-side of any resale flow as a standing differentiator worth highlighting versus the incumbent.

A Bruno Mars fan’s Ticketmaster resale listing is drawing attention after she said a seat she listed for $299 appeared to buyers for $422.45 — while her own projected payout would be just $254.15 after Ticketmaster deducted a seller fee.

In a [TikTok video posted by @brickbuster69](https://www.tiktok.com/@brickbuster69/video/7691812809235041549?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), the seller showed a Ticketmaster “Edit Listing” screen for Section 235, Row 19, Seat 4. The screen showed the ticket listed at $299, a $44.85 service fee and a projected payout of $254.15.

The $44.85 deduction amounts to a 15% seller fee.

The seller then said she found the same section, row and seat on Ticketmaster at a much higher buyer-facing price. [The Mary Sue reported](https://www.themarysue.com/woman-lists-her-bruno-mars-ticket-on-ticketmaster-for-299-then-finds-her-seat-listed-for-422-and-realizes-how-much-they-kept-that-is-so-gross/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) Wednesday that the price displayed for the seat was $422.45.

That creates a $123.45 difference between the seller’s chosen $299 listing price and the amount shown to a prospective buyer. Compared with the seller’s projected $254.15 payout, the total gap is $168.30.

“That is so gross,” the seller said in the video.

The numbers offer a particularly clear example of how differently the economics of a single resale ticket can look depending on which side of the transaction a fan occupies. But the $168.30 spread should not automatically be characterized as money Ticketmaster itself keeps.

[Ticketmaster says](https://help.ticketmaster.com/hc/en-us/articles/9663528775313-How-are-ticket-prices-and-fees-determined?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) sellers on its resale marketplace determine the base price of their tickets and receive those proceeds minus applicable fees. It also says resale service fees may be shared among parties involved in putting on an event and can cover operational costs as well as profits.

Ticketmaster currently tells buyers that they will generally see the total ticket price, including mandatory fees, upfront before taxes. That means at least part of the discrepancy between a reseller’s base listing and what a buyer sees can reflect buyer-side charges that are now incorporated into the displayed price instead of appearing only later in the purchase process.

What is not apparent from the seller’s video is exactly how the additional $123.45 above her $299 listing price was divided, or whether any portion reflected a markup separate from service fees.

## Ticketmaster Resale Fees Already Under FTC Scrutiny

The episode comes as Ticketmaster and parent company Live Nation are defending a federal lawsuit in which the Federal Trade Commission and seven states have challenged several of the company’s ticketing and resale practices.

Among other allegations, the [FTC’s complaint](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/FTCvLiveNation-Ticketmaster-Complaint-filed.pdf?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) claims Ticketmaster earns money on resale transactions through fees charged on both sides of the marketplace and, in some cases, has the ability to increase a resale ticket’s price independently of those fees.

The government alleges Live Nation and Ticketmaster collected $986 million in resale fees between 2019 and 2024. It separately alleges the companies collected more than $187 million from markups Ticketmaster added to resale tickets during that period.

Those claims remain allegations. They do not establish that Ticketmaster added a separate markup to the Bruno Mars ticket shown in the TikTok video.

Just last week, [a federal judge rejected Live Nation and Ticketmaster’s attempt to dismiss the FTC case](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/09/ticketmaster-ftc-bots-act-lawsuit-dismissal-denied/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), allowing claims involving ticket limits, resale activity and allegedly deceptive fee displays to move forward.

The court’s ruling was not a finding that Ticketmaster violated the law. It determined that the government had alleged sufficient facts for the case to continue.

## Bruno Mars Tour Has Already Drawn Pricing Complaints

The resale controversy follows months of attention around pricing for Mars’ massive “The Romantic Tour.”

When tickets initially went on sale in January, [TicketNews reported fan complaints about primary-market prices reaching as high as $2,400](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/01/bruno-mars-fans-call-out-ticket-prices-on-tour/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content). Live Nation subsequently said the tour generated record-setting demand and moved more than 2 million tickets in a single day.

The latest video involves a different part of the market: a fan attempting to resell a ticket she already holds. It does not establish who originally set the ticket’s face value, how much the seller initially paid or precisely how the buyer-side charges on the $422.45 listing were allocated.

It does, however, put the two sides of a Ticketmaster resale transaction into unusually concrete terms: a fan chooses a $299 resale price, stands to receive $254.15 if the ticket sells, while another fan looking at the same seat sees a price of $422.45.
