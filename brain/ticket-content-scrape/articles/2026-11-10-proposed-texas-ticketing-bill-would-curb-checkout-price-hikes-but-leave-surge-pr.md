# Proposed Texas Ticketing Bill Would Curb Checkout Price Hikes, But Leave Surge Pricing Loopholes

## Summary

Texas state Sen. Judith Zaffirini filed Senate Bill 42 (Nov. 9), which would require upfront all-in fee disclosure and bar increasing a ticket's price after a customer selects it for purchase during checkout, while carrying forward the state's existing ticket-bot penalties. However, the bill leaves dynamic pricing, inventory-tiering and pre-checkout price changes entirely untouched — promoters could still offer a cheap advertised price with limited inventory, then push buyers to much pricier "different" tickets once that inventory sells out, without technically violating the law.

### Key Points
- SB 42 amends Texas Business & Commerce Code Ch. 328: total price (incl. mandatory fees) must be shown more prominently than any sub-component before selection; price can't rise after a ticket is selected (narrow exception for physical-delivery fees)
- Builds on the FTC's existing all-in pricing rule (effective May 2025) by adding a separate itemized-fee disclosure requirement
- Does NOT set a maximum ticket price, does not regulate pre-selection price changes, inventory segmentation, or premium-ticket tiering — a promoter can still advertise a cheap tier that "sells out" into a 3x pricier tier for the same section
- Retains (doesn't expand) existing $10,000-per-violation bot penalties from 2023's SB 1639; new fee-disclosure provisions lack an explicit penalty schedule of their own
- Follows Zaffirini's failed 2025 SB 1622 attempt at the same fee-transparency requirements; a separate ticket-transfer/resale-protection bill from 2025 is not included here
- Session convenes Jan. 12, 2027; if passed, takes effect Sept. 1, 2027

### Highlights
- All-in pricing/fee-itemization mandate matches the direction of federal FTC rules and other states' junk-fee pushes
- Clear loophole: the price-increase ban only applies after ticket selection, not to pre-selection dynamic pricing or tiered/segmented inventory — the primary lever behind high effective prices remains unregulated
- No standalone enforcement penalty attached to the new fee-disclosure requirements, unlike the bot provisions

### Industry Problem
State-level junk-fee transparency laws are converging on "show the all-in price," but checkout-moment price-lock rules don't address the upstream pricing mechanics (tiered inventory, staged releases) that actually drive consumer-facing price variance — a gap regulators and platforms alike will keep revisiting.

### Proposed Solution
Track SB 42 through the Jan. 2027 session for enforcement-mechanism amendments; for Etix, all-in fee display and pre-selection price transparency are straightforward compliance/improvement items regardless of this bill's fate, since they align with the broader FTC-rule direction already in effect.

Texas lawmakers are preparing another round of ticketing legislation aimed at improving price transparency and limiting certain pricing practices, but the proposed restrictions would leave concert promoters and primary ticket sellers considerable flexibility to charge premium prices, control inventory releases and adjust prices in response to demand.

State Sen. Judith Zaffirini filed [Senate Bill 42](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/90R/billtext/pdf/SB00042I.pdf?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) on Monday, November 9, ahead of the 2027 legislative session. The proposal would require upfront disclosure of ticket fees and prohibit increasing the total cost of a ticket after a customer has selected it for purchase.

The bill would apply its new ticket-pricing rules to online primary and secondary sales for events held in Texas. It would also retain the state’s existing restrictions on automated ticket-buying software, placing both subjects in the same legislation without creating a new bot ban.

However, the proposal would not prohibit many of the pricing strategies responsible for elevated ticket prices during high-demand presales and initial onsale periods. Promoters could still establish substantially different prices for comparable seats, designate large quantities of inventory as premium tickets and adjust available prices between sales periods.

## What the Texas Ticketing Bill Would Change

SB 42 would amend Chapter 328 of the Texas Business & Commerce Code to establish new ticket-fee disclosure requirements.

Under the proposed legislation, sellers would have to clearly display the total cost of a ticket, including mandatory ancillary fees, before a consumer selects it for purchase. They would also have to separately disclose the aggregate dollar amount attributable to those fees.

The bill also would require the total purchase price to be displayed more prominently than ancillary fees or individual price components, preventing sellers from placing a base price or fee figure in type as prominent as the all-in total.

A ticket advertised at $150 with $35 in mandatory fees, for example, would need to display the $185 total alongside a disclosure identifying the $35 fee component.

The legislation also would prohibit sellers from increasing the total cost of a ticket during checkout after a customer has selected it, with a limited exception for previously disclosed charges associated with non-electronic delivery selected by the purchaser.

These provisions would build on existing federal consumer protections. The [Federal Trade Commission’s rule on unfair or deceptive fees](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/rule-unfair-or-deceptive-fees-frequently-asked-questions?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), which took effect in May 2025, already requires ticket sellers to advertise prices that include mandatory fees.

The proposed Texas legislation would go further by requiring a separate disclosure of the fee amount and expressly restricting price increases after ticket selection.

Neither the federal rule nor SB 42 establishes a maximum permissible ticket price.

## Why Promoter Pricing Practices Would Remain Largely Unaffected

One important limitation of the Texas proposal is that its price-increase restriction begins after the consumer has selected a particular ticket. It does not prohibit ticket sellers from changing advertised prices before that selection or offering different tickets in the same seating section at substantially different prices.

Consider a hypothetical concert where a promoter advertises tickets starting at $125 but allocates only a limited number of seats at that price. A significant portion of the remaining inventory in the same seating section might be designated as premium tickets priced at $375 or more.

A consumer attempting to purchase one of the $125 tickets could receive a message indicating that the lower-priced inventory is no longer available and be presented with alternative tickets in the same section costing three times as much.

Provided the original lower-priced offer was genuine and the higher-priced alternatives represent distinct inventory, that scenario would not necessarily violate SB 42. The seller would not be increasing the price of a ticket the consumer had already selected; it would be offering a different ticket at a different advertised price.

By comparison, increasing the price of the same selected ticket from $125 to $375 while the consumer is completing the transaction would fall much more directly within the conduct the legislation seeks to prohibit.

The distinction does not give sellers permission to advertise nonexistent inventory or misrepresent availability. Federal consumer-protection rules already prohibit misleading ticket offers, including advertising tickets at prices at which they cannot actually be purchased.

Nevertheless, the proposed Texas legislation does not establish rules requiring comparable seats to carry comparable initial prices or compelling promoters to make all available inventory accessible at once.

It also would not prevent promoters from setting elevated initial prices during periods of anticipated high demand, then reducing prices later as the event approaches and unsold inventory needs to be cleared.

For example, a promoter could initially offer a significant portion of an arena’s inventory at $400 during an early presale, then release additional seats or reduce prices to $150 ahead of the event if demand proves insufficient.

That approach can produce substantial differences in what consumers pay without requiring a ticket’s price to increase during an individual checkout transaction.

Such pricing can be established through advance inventory segmentation, premium-ticket designations and scheduled price adjustments rather than continuous algorithmic repricing.

The FTC’s existing guidance explicitly permits dynamic pricing based on demand or inventory, provided the resulting price representations are not misleading.

TicketNews has [previously examined how ticket holdbacks and staggered inventory releases](https://www.ticketnews.com/ticket-regulations-and-legislation-compendium/ticketing-topics-ticket-holdbacks-transparency-on-available-tickets/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) can affect consumer perceptions of availability and demand.

Those practices would remain outside the specific price-increase restriction proposed in SB 42.

## Existing Texas Bot Penalties Would Remain

The same bill also carries forward existing Texas restrictions on ticket-buying bots, including software used to circumvent purchase limits, online queues and presale systems.

Texas enacted [Senate Bill 1639 in 2023](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/BC/htm/BC.328.htm?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), establishing penalties of up to $10,000 for each knowing violation of its ticket-bot restrictions.

The new proposal would preserve those provisions while moving them into a separate subchapter of the Business & Commerce Code.

An additional question concerns enforcement of the new fee-transparency rules. SB 42’s express attorney general enforcement authority and existing civil penalties are tied to the bot provisions, while the proposed fee requirements appear in a different subchapter without a new, specifically identified penalty schedule.

Other consumer-protection laws could still apply, but the bill does not expressly extend its existing $10,000 bot penalty to ordinary fee-disclosure violations.

## Texas Revisits Previous Ticketing Proposals

The latest legislation follows an unsuccessful attempt by Zaffirini to enact substantially similar fee-transparency requirements during the 2025 legislative session.

Her earlier [Senate Bill 1622](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/SB01622I.htm?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) included the same central requirements governing fee disclosures and increases after ticket selection.

TicketNews [covered several Texas ticketing reform proposals in March 2025](https://www.ticketnews.com/2025/03/texas-lawmakers-join-push-for-ticketing-improvements/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), including separate legislation intended to protect consumers’ ability to transfer and resell tickets through competing marketplaces.

Those broader ticket-ownership protections are not included in SB 42.

The new proposal would not establish a resale price cap, prevent primary sellers from imposing ticket-transfer restrictions or require promoters to disclose how much inventory is actually available to the general public.

It also would not limit how much of an event’s potential revenue an artist or promoter could seek to capture through premium primary-market pricing.

The Texas Legislature is scheduled to convene January 12, 2027. If approved in its current form, SB 42 would take effect September 1, 2027.

Until then, the proposal highlights a recurring distinction in ticketing legislation: rules governing how prices are displayed and changed during checkout do not necessarily alter the broader pricing and inventory decisions that determine how much consumers are charged in the first place.
