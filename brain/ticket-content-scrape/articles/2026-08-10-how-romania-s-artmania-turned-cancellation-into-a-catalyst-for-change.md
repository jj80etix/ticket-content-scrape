# How Romania’s ARTmania turned cancellation into a catalyst for change

## Summary

Romania's ARTmania festival was forced to cancel its normal 2026 configuration after new national fire-safety rules shrank its usable Sibiu square from 7,120 sqm to about 1,300 sqm; founder Codruta Vulcu tells IQ how the cancellation triggered a national policy debate that produced clearer, case-by-case event-licensing rules ahead of the festival's 20th anniversary.

### Key Points
- New safety-distance regulations (enacted last May) made ARTmania's standard ticketed format "literally impossible," forcing an April cancellation and full refunds to the roughly 40% of capacity already sold.
- The cancellation sparked a nationwide debate, prompting the national code to be updated with clearer per-event licensing criteria involving third-party experts.
- Organizers pivoted to a free single-stage event ("ARTmania: In Context") that still drew 50-60% of typical attendance.
- Vulcu, also president of AROC (Romanian concert promoters' association), says the new process allows multi-year license validity as long as capacity/stage/evacuation plans don't change, adding predictability for organizers.

### Highlights
- Usable festival footprint dropped from 7,120 sqm to roughly 1,300 sqm under the new fire-safety rules.
- About 40% of festival capacity had already been sold when the 2026 edition was cancelled; organizers issued full refunds.
- The regulatory fix now allows multi-year license validity for unchanged event configurations, replacing a blanket law with case-by-case expert review.

### Industry Problem
Sudden, ambiguous safety-licensing regulation can make a long-running event configuration non-viable with little notice, forcing organizers into late cancellations, full refunds, and reputational/financial risk -- a structural pain point for promoters operating under evolving local safety codes.

### Proposed Solution
Romania's fix was industry-government collaboration to replace a blanket rule with predictable, case-by-case multi-year licensing. For Etix, the takeaway is a monitoring signal plus a readiness check: organizer clients in jurisdictions tightening safety/licensing rules may need flexible ticketing/refund tooling (bulk refund workflows, credit-to-next-edition options) ready to deploy quickly if a similar late cancellation hits a client event.

## How Romania’s ARTmania turned cancellation into a catalyst for change

Festival founder Codruta Vulcu tells IQ how it was "worth the sacrifice" for organisers to gain clarity around event licensing

![Codruta Vulcu](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Codruta-Vulcu.png)

Codruta Vulcu

After new safety regulations made ARTmania’s normal configuration effectively impossible, festival founder Codruta Vulcu tells *IQ* how its cancellation and reformatting helped bring about change for the maturing Romanian music market ahead of the event’s 20th anniversary in 2027.

The long-standing Romanian rock and metal festival looked different from previous iterations for its 19th edition this year. Instead of a ticketed, two-day event for 10,000 people, it was reformatted as a free-to-attend event from 24–25 July, featuring four bands — something that seemed inconceivable when organisers cancelled it entirely earlier this year.

Last May, authorities enacted new national regulations around fire safety, which imposed stricter safety distances around buildings for temporary installations and fenced perimeters. With ARTmania’s standard configuration, the usable area of the Piața Mare (Large Square) public space in Sibiu plummeted from 7,120 sqm to around 1,300 sqm.

“It would have been literally impossible,” Vulcu tells *IQ*, with organisers eventually cancelling the 2026 edition in April. It was a decision made after months of discussions with state officials and private-sector personnel, amid questions around licensing requirements for different types of outdoor events.

“When we decided to cancel, it practically launched a nationwide debate. How could that actually happen? How could the festival happen for 18 years? And if this is the law, how are events still going on?” says Vulcu, who is also the President of AROC (Romanian Association of Concert Promoters).

Organisers fully refunded ticketholders following the cancellation — at the time, they represented roughly 40% of the festival’s capacity — a decision that could easily have sent the event under in today’s tight market.

> “Every challenge can also be used as an opportunity”

“Financially, it wasn’t that easy. But there were a lot of people who said, ‘Hey, we don’t need the money,’ or, ‘Whenever you are coming back, just give us the tickets for the next edition,'” she recalls.

Discussions continued in the wake of ARTmania’s cancellation, with the national code quickly updated to better outline licensing requirements. In response, the festival team chose to hold a free, single-stage event with two bands each night, dubbed ARTmania: In Context, to mark the 19th edition and everything that had gone on behind the scenes.

Discussions continued in the wake of ARTmania’s cancellation, with the national code updated to better outline the licensing requirements for events of all types. As a result, the festival team decided to hold a free, single-stage event with two bands each night, dubbed ARTmania: In Context, to mark the 19th edition and everything that had gone on behind the scenes.

“We initially planned to just mark the occasion on the Square with a very famous artist from Romania, Dan Perjovschi. But when things changed, we decided to mark it with two shows every evening, and not have a lost edition. That’s why we called it In Context, because like art, you always need to adapt to the context,” she says.

“I have to say, now that it’s over, in a way it might have done us very good, because when you’re in the same place for 19 editions, you can get stuck, but this forced us to rethink. The improvements that will come for next year, the 20th anniversary, will definitely make ARTmania better than it was before. Every challenge can also be used as an opportunity,” she adds.

> “Despite the sacrifice, I think it was worth it”

The free community event drew roughly 50-60% of the festival’s typical audience, gathering to celebrate the festival and see Myrkur (Denmark), Alternosfera (Republic of Moldova), HVNDS (Romania), and Pridian (Estonia).

In the aftermath, Vulcu says the challenges have been worth the payoff, with event licensing now involving third-party experts on a case-by-case basis rather than a blanket law. She says the change will help create greater predictability for the maturing music market.

“If cancelling one edition the way we know it brought the private sector and the state sector to the same table, despite the sacrifice, I think it was worth it. Now it is clear how you can obtain the license,” she explains.

“This change didn’t help only ARTmania for the next edition, because we are going to practically be back as we used to be, but it helps everyone else in the market to have a bit clearer and more predictable pathway for their events. Based on this, it will be possible to get a license for particular locations, even if they are not fixed ones. If you have the setup of the festival in the same location, you can get one license, and as long as you do not change the evacuations, capacity, or the position of the stage, your license is valid for several editions. This is a huge plus because it gives predictability, and it can’t be changed overnight,” she says.

For Vulcu, the change demonstrates the progression of the Romanian market as a whole.

“Romania has reached the point where it has such big events that it needs to have this strong collaboration between the private sector and the state authorities. This was a step in the correct direction of people staying at the same table, having a dialogue, and whenever changes are made to the laws, it’s also a discussion with the private sector about how it is affecting them,” she says.

> “I would like us to talk a little bit more about the quality we put into the world, not only the quantity”

“It wasn’t necessarily the most orthodox solution, but I think that this was a point where we needed to stand for something, out of respect for our audience, out of respect for the artists, out of respect for ourselves, and for the rule of law. The law should be clear. I don’t say it was an easy solution, because it wasn’t; it’s my baby. It’s a hard decision to take, but sometimes you need to make hard decisions. And at least for me personally, it’s a good lesson. You can also survive, and it’s okay to do hard things, even though they seem very difficult,” she explains.

With this year’s iteration now in the rearview, the team’s sights are set on next year’s 20th anniversary edition. Early-stage plans include integrating more venues, expanding cultural offerings such as museum access, and building more discourse into the programming.

“We are going to still be around and in the Large Square, but we are going to introduce more smaller venues for niche subgenres of rock, prog, metal, and punk. We are going to also introduce more dialogue about values, about the role of art,” she says.

“Personally, I am a bit tired of the discourse in our industry, where it is only about more market share, more tickets sold, more people attending. It is like as if quantity is the only thing that matters,” she explains. “I would like us to talk a little bit more about the quality we put into the world, not only the quantity. And that’s not to say that huge events are bad. Don’t get me wrong; they have their own role, but also more niche or more boutique or more art events have their own role because they bring a different quality to the cultural experience of the audience.”
