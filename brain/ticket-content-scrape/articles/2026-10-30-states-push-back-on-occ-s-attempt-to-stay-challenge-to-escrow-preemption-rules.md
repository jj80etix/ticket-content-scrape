# States Push Back on OCC’s Attempt to Stay Challenge to Escrow-Preemption Rules

## Summary

A coalition of 10 state attorneys general asked a federal court in Oregon to reject the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency's bid to stay litigation over the OCC's new rules preempting state interest-on-escrow laws for national banks. This is a banking/mortgage-escrow regulatory dispute with no connection to ticketing.

### Key Points
- States (CA, CT, ME, MD, MA, MN, NY, OR, RI, VT) sued in August 2026 arguing the OCC's May 2026 escrow-preemption rules exceed its statutory authority and violate the APA
- OCC moved to dismiss/transfer Oct. 5, then sought a stay pending Supreme Court cert petitions on related bank-escrow preemption cases
- States argue the OCC hasn't met the Landis/Lockyer three-factor test for a stay

## Transcript

[Skip to content](#lxb_af-loop)

On October 23, 2026, a coalition of 10 state attorneys general (AG) filed a motion requesting that the U.S. District Court for the District of Oregon reject an attempt by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) to stay the states’ challenge to the OCC’s new interest-on-escrow preemption rules in *State of Oregon et al. v. OCC et al.*

As [previously discussed](https://www.consumerfinancialserviceslawmonitor.com/2026/05/occ-finalizes-escrow-rules-to-further-strengthen-federal-preemption/), on May 15, 2026, the OCC issued two rules addressing mortgage escrow accounts and federal preemption. The first rule, Real Estate Lending Escrow Accounts, amended OCC regulations for national banks and federal savings associations to codify their authority to establish and maintain real estate lending escrow accounts and to exercise business judgment over the terms of those accounts, including whether to pay interest on escrow balances and to charge related fees. The second rule, Preemption Determination: State Interest‑on‑Escrow Laws, provides that federal law preempts state laws that restrict OCC‑regulated banks’ flexibility to decide whether to pay interest on escrow balances or assess related fees. When issuing this rule, the OCC identified several state interest-on-escrow laws that, according to the OCC, deprive national banks of the flexibility to exercise the authority and discretion permitted by federal law and which are therefore preempted by federal law.

On August 11, 2026, the AGs of California, Connecticut, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, and Vermont, all of which have their own interest-on-escrow laws, filed a complaint in federal court alleging that the OCC’s escrow-preemption rules are unlawful and asking the court to set the rules aside under the Administrative Procedure Act. Specifically, the complaint claims that the rules exceed the OCC’s statutory authority and are arbitrary and capricious, and that the OCC failed to comply with procedural requirements in promulgating the rules. The states also argue that the Dodd-Frank Act requires federal preemption determinations to satisfy the *Barnett Bank* “significant interference” standard based on case-specific assessments that are supported by substantial evidence, but that the OCC’s rules do not meet this standard. The OCC moved to dismiss or transfer the case on October 5, 2026, and several days later moved to stay all aspects of the case, other than its motion to dismiss or transfer, pending the Supreme Court’s handling of three petitions for certiorari that involve private suits against banks and the validity of state interest-on-escrow laws.

In the states’ recent opposition to the OCC’s motion to stay, they argue that the OCC has not met the standard for a stay pursuant to the three-factor test set forth in *Landis v. North American Co.*, 299 U.S. 248 (1936), as articulated by the Ninth Circuit in *Lockyer v. Mirant Corp.*, 398 F.3d 1098 (9th Cir. 2005). The test requires courts to weigh whether (1) a stay would simplify or complicate the issues involved in the case, (2) a stay could cause damage to a party or to a nonparty, and (3) allowing the case to proceed would cause hardship or inequity to the party requesting the stay.

On the first factor, the states contend that a decision by the Supreme Court in the private lawsuits would not resolve or otherwise simplify their challenge to the OCC’s rules because those cases involve private contract and unjust enrichment claims and preemption as a defense, but do not test the validity of the OCC’s rules or the OCC’s compliance with the requirements under the Dodd-Frank Act. In addition, the states note that those cases only involve the interest-on-escrow laws of three of the 10 states involved in their case against the OCC. Finally, the states argue that the OCC’s claim that a stay is warranted pending the resolution of those cases depends on a “nested series of contingencies that may never occur,” including that the Supreme Court grants certiorari and that it decides the parties’ substantive preemption questions. On the second factor, the states argue that a stay would cause them substantial and indefinite harm due to their inability to enforce their own statutes. They also argue a stay will harm consumers since they will not receive interest on their escrow balances. Regarding the third factor, the states argue that allowing the case to move forward will not cause hardship to the OCC, as the only potential burden that the OCC identified in support of a stay is the cost of producing the administrative record and briefing the merits, which the Ninth Circuit has held is not a “clear case of hardship or inequity.” The states further note that both sides have agreed that no discovery beyond the administrative record is needed for the case to proceed, and that the OCC’s request to stay everything but its own motion to dismiss or transfer is one-sided and inequitable.

Any reply by the OCC to the states’ opposition is due by November 6, and the court will likely resolve the OCC’s motion for a stay and the OCC’s motion to dismiss or transfer (which the states have opposed in a separate filing) in late November. Meanwhile, there is no timeline regarding whether the Supreme Court will grant the petitions for certiorari for the private interest-on-escrow cases described above. The Supreme Court has invited the solicitor general to weigh in on whether certiorari should be granted in those cases, but it did not provide a deadline by which the solicitor general must do so.

A decision by the court to stay the challenge to the OCC’s rules will likely mean that the rules will remain in effect for the foreseeable future, thereby preventing states from enforcing their own interest-on-escrow laws against national banks and federal savings associations. On the other hand, if the court rejects the motion to stay, a substantive decision regarding whether the OCC’s rules will remain in place could be issued within the next few months.
