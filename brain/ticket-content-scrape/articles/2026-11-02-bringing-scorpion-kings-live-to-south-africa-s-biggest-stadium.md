# Bringing Scorpion Kings Live to South Africa’s biggest stadium

## Summary

IQ spoke with organizers of "Scorpion Kings Live" (DJ Maphorisa and Kabza De Small) about scaling the amapiano show from a 50,000-fan 2025 outing at Pretoria's Loftus Versfeld to a 74,000-fan 2026 show at Johannesburg's FNB Stadium, detailing the production, logistics, and sponsorship tradeoffs involved.

### Key Points
- Production/technical spend roughly tripled to quadrupled moving to the larger stadium, per organizer Katlego Malatji.
- A tougher 2026 sponsorship environment (geopolitical uncertainty, fuel costs) reduced commercial support without eliminating profitability.
- Sponsorship was credited with keeping ticket prices accessible despite rising production costs.
- About 35% of attendees traveled to Johannesburg for the show; estimated local economic impact of R200m (€10.7m).
- Organizers are eyeing UK/US expansion for the concept.

## Bringing Scorpion Kings Live to South Africa’s biggest stadium

The live show from DJ Maphorisa and Kabza De Small increased its audience from 50,000 to 74,000 between its 2025 and 2026 outings

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Scorpion-Kings-Live.jpg)

Scorpion Kings Live

A year after DJ Maphorisa and Kabza De Small brought their show *Scorpion Kings Live* to a 50,000-strong Pretoria crowd in 2025, the act returned in late September for one of the largest amapiano events ever staged in front of a nearly a 50% bigger audience, this time in South Africa’s biggest stadium.

Now, after bring the show to 74,000 fans at FNB Stadium in Johannesburg, organisers tell *IQ* about the more complex set of operational and commercial considerations behind the sequel show.

Moving between the two stadiums was not merely a matter of accommodating a larger audience. The experience at Pretoria’s Loftus Versfeld in 2025 had exposed several aspects of the production that needed reconsideration, from the timing of the event and transport arrangements to crowd movement and basic audience facilities.

[Katlego Malatji](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2019/09/new-bosses-2019-katlego-malatji-homecoming-events/), who spearheaded the project, says the previous edition informed almost every aspect of the planning for FNB Stadium. “Everything,” he says, when asked what the team took away from Loftus.

The larger venue saw promoters needing to manage a significantly higher production budget, as the show welcomed a total of more than 70 performers to the stage, including guest artists and dancers, in addition to more musical directors, choreographers, and technical personnel. Malatji estimates that expenditure on production, technical, and creative requirements at FNB was about “three to four times” what the team spent at Loftus.

The venue configuration was one of the reasons. At Loftus, the stage was positioned at the centre of the stadium, making certain technical arrangements easier to manage. At FNB, a different stage configuration meant the team needed a considerably larger sound and technical setup to maintain consistent production quality across the venue.

Scaling up the production also raised questions about how organisers could manage the increased costs without making the experience inaccessible to the artists’ audiences. Malatji says sponsorship helped reconcile production costs with the need to keep tickets affordable. The event was designed to reach a mass audience, and relying exclusively on ticket revenue to absorb rising production costs could push prices beyond what some fans can afford.

“Sponsorship allows the production to remain top tier while allowing the event organiser not to make the tickets too expensive for the common man,” he says.

This year, however, has seen a more challenging sponsorship environment. Malatji points to geopolitical uncertainty and rising fuel costs as factors affecting marketing and sponsorship budgets. The reduced availability of commercial support affected the event’s profitability, although he says it remained profitable, with returns below initial expectations.

> “ Sponsorship allows the production to remain top tier”

The 2025 concert had taken place on a Friday evening, but the team concluded that the level of demand required a Saturday daytime event. That decision also influenced how audiences would arrive, including what entertainment could be introduced before the main performances to encourage earlier arrivals. Organisers also increased the number of bars and toilets and introduced transport arrangements such as a park-and-ride service.

Organisers tell *IQ* the scale of the event also placed more demands on crowd management. About 600 event wardens were brought in to assist with crowd movement, alongside eight security companies. The Ministry of Arts and Culture also supplemented the arrangements with extra security support and shuttle services. Malatji says the team opened further exits, increased lighting and signage around the venue and deployed personnel to direct people towards the appropriate departure points.

The event’s impact may also extend beyond its direct ticketing and sponsorship revenues. The organisers are currently completing an economic impact study, with preliminary findings suggesting that an estimated 35% of attendees travelled to Johannesburg for the concert. Malatji says initial estimates indicate an economic impact of around R200m (€10.7m) on the local economy.

For the organisers, the next challenge is to build on the production without just repeating what has already been achieved. Malatji says a new concept is already in development, with the team conscious of what he calls the “law of diminishing astonishment”.

The intention is to keep finding ways to make the experience bigger, more creative and more compelling for returning audiences, Malatji says. There are also ambitions to take Scorpion Kings Live beyond South Africa, with the UK and US among the markets being considered. Malatji attributes this interest to audiences travelling into the country for the existing shows, as well as enquiries from people abroad who want the production brought closer to them.

The next phase will test whether the production can retain its scope and creative ambition as the organisers look to take it beyond South Africa.
