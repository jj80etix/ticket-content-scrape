# California Enacts AB 1349 Speculative Ticket Ban as Newsom Calls for More Work

## Summary

California Gov. Gavin Newsom signed AB 1349 into law, banning speculative ticket sales (advertising/selling tickets a seller doesn't possess or have rights to), requiring resale marketplaces to implement "reasonable measures" against such listings, expanding liability for non-delivery, and barring circumvention of ticket limits/queues/presale restrictions and deceptive "authorized reseller" advertising. Newsom signed it while explicitly calling the final bill's carve-outs "uneven" — noting exemptions for season-ticket packages and a safe harbor for pro sports teams/venues — and asked the bill's author to keep working on the policy in 2027. NIVA, an early architect and supporter of the bill, reversed course after late amendments and unsuccessfully urged a veto, arguing the final text left independent venues/promoters exposed while sports organizations got carve-outs and large resale marketplaces got insufficient accountability.

### Key Points
- AB 1349 bans speculative ticket listings (selling tickets not yet owned/rights-secured) and requires marketplaces to take "reasonable measures" to prevent them, plus expanded non-delivery liability.
- Also bars circumventing ticket limits/queues/presale restrictions and deceptive sites/ads implying unauthorized resellers are official sources.
- Carve-outs: season/multigame ticket packages, certain rights-holder-withheld inventory, playoff packages, and a safe harbor for California pro sports teams/venues/league entities managing inventory.
- Newsom's signing message calls the approach "uneven" and invites further legislative work in 2027 on the exemptions.
- NIVA co-architected the bill in March but flipped to a veto campaign Sept. 1 after amendments it says favor sports orgs and under-regulate large resale marketplaces; it has not commented since the signature.

### Highlights
- Effective as signed (Sunday); enforcement obligations on resale marketplaces begin under the reasonable-measures standard.
- Newsom publicly flagged the exemption structure as needing revisiting — a likely 2027 legislative fight to watch.
- Marketplace obligation standard is "knowingly or recklessly" facilitating a speculative sale, not strict liability.

### Industry Problem
Speculative ticket listings (selling inventory the seller doesn't yet hold) and deceptive resale advertising directly undermine ticket-buyer trust and primary-market integrity; California's law also illustrates an unresolved policy tension — carve-outs for large sports/venue operators while independent venues and promoters face fuller compliance exposure.

### Proposed Solution
AB 1349 puts the compliance burden on resale marketplaces to detect and block speculative listings ("reasonable measures") and on all sellers to avoid deceptive/circumvention practices. For Etix, this is a monitor/improve signal: any secondary-market or resale-adjacent feature touching California should build in speculative-listing detection and clear "authorized source" labeling now, ahead of likely 2027 amendments narrowing today's carve-outs.

California Gov. Gavin Newsom signed AB 1349 into law Sunday, approving new restrictions on speculative ticket sales, deceptive ticketing websites and ticket-purchasing circumvention while simultaneously warning that the final legislation takes an “uneven approach” that should be revisited next year.

The signature ends a late-session fight that saw the National Independent Venue Association reverse its support for a bill it had spent much of the year championing and urge Newsom to veto it. But Newsom’s [signing message](https://www.gov.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Sign-Msg-AB-1349.pdf) also gives some weight to concerns about the final bill’s carve-outs, even as he allowed the broader ticket reforms to become law.

“I share the author’s concern with problematic practices in both the primary and secondary ticket-selling market,” Newsom wrote, citing speculative sales, fees and deceptive advertising. He nevertheless said the measure “appears to take an uneven approach,” allowing some ticket sellers to avoid certain obligations, and encouraged Assemblymember Isaac Bryan to continue working with stakeholders on the policy in the coming year.

Newsom’s office separately described AB 1349 as prohibiting speculative ticket sales, updating restrictions involving ticket-buying technology and placing new limits on deceptive ticketing websites and advertising.

## What AB 1349 Changes

AB 1349 prohibits sellers from advertising, listing or selling tickets they do not possess or have a legally enforceable right to receive, unless they have authorization from the event presenter or venue operator. The legislation describes those transactions as speculative ticket sales.

The law separately places obligations on resale marketplaces. A marketplace may not knowingly or recklessly process payments, advertise, list, collect fees from or otherwise facilitate a speculative ticket sale, and platforms are required to implement “reasonable measures” designed to prevent those listings.

That became central to the final legislative fight.

AB 1349 also expands liability when sellers fail to deliver promised tickets, prohibits efforts to evade ticket limits, queues, presale restrictions and other distribution controls, and bars websites and advertisements designed to mislead consumers into believing a reseller is an authorized ticket source.

The final bill nevertheless contains exceptions. The speculative-ticket provision does not apply to specified season-ticket packages, multigame packages, certain inventory withheld by a rights holder or venue operator, or playoff packages sold before a season. Another section provides a safe harbor for California professional sports teams, sports venue operators and certain league-owned entities when managing or allocating ticket inventory for operational or business purposes.

Newsom did not identify particular companies or industries in describing the legislation as uneven, but said exempting some sellers from provisions of the bill “deserves further discussion.”

## NIVA Went From Support to Veto Campaign

The signature comes despite a veto campaign from NIVA, which had earlier been one of AB 1349’s strongest supporters.

In March, NIVA said it was “proud to help architect” both AB 1349 and a separate ticket-resale price-cap proposal. But after late amendments to AB 1349, [the organization reversed course and urged Newsom to veto the remaining ticket bill](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/09/niva-ab-1349-veto-late-support/).

NIVA argued that the amended bill left independent venues, promoters and festivals exposed to liability while providing greater protections to professional sports organizations and insufficient accountability for large resale marketplaces. The organization said Sept. 1 that the bill no longer represented the speculative-ticket reform it had initially backed.

The final text does impose direct compliance requirements on resale marketplaces, however, including the prohibition against knowingly or recklessly facilitating speculative sales and the requirement that platforms take reasonable preventative measures.

That disagreement over how liability is distributed among ticket sellers, marketplaces, venues and rights holders now appears likely to carry into another legislative session, particularly after Newsom explicitly invited additional work on the exemptions.

As of Sunday evening, NIVA had not posted a new statement responding to Newsom’s signature; its most recent AB 1349 statement remained the Sept. 1 veto request.

With Newsom’s signature, the immediate fight over whether AB 1349 becomes law is over. The next dispute will be how California applies the new restrictions — and whether lawmakers return in 2027 to address the uneven treatment Newsom identified in signing them.
