# From volunteering to VIP: How charity ticketing campaigns are creating new access routes

## Summary

Charity-ticketing platforms Propeller and Earnt let fans trade volunteering/community-service hours for access to high-demand shows (Harry Styles, Chappell Roan, Noah Kahan among the artists involved), offering an alternative allocation mechanism as demand for live tickets continues to outstrip supply. Earnt's Harry Styles activation drew over 16,500 applicants for roughly 200 ticket slots earned via a four-hour volunteer shift; Propeller says its model has engaged over four million fans since around 2020.

### Key Points
- Propeller and Earnt convert donations/volunteer hours into points redeemable for tickets or sweepstakes entries (e.g., two volunteer shifts ≈ one concert ticket, eight hours ≈ a festival ticket)
- Earnt's Harry Styles "Together, Together" activation: ~200 tickets awarded from 16,500+ applicants after a prior test run at a Manchester album-release show
- Propeller has worked with Justin Bieber, Shawn Mendes, George Strait, Incubus, Demi Lovato, Chappell Roan, Noah Kahan and others; serves 4M+ fans
- Framed explicitly as a democratizing alternative to giveaways/promotional sweepstakes amid high-demand-onsale scarcity

### Highlights
- Scale: Earnt's single activation produced an 80:1 applicant-to-ticket ratio, illustrating depth of unmet demand for alternative access routes
- Model substitutes time/community action for money as the access currency ("if you don't have money, you can invest your time")

### Industry Problem
Conventional onsales for high-demand tours leave most fans unable to get tickets at any price, and traditional allocation (lottery, presale codes, dynamic pricing) does little to address the affordability and "is this fair" pain points patrons raise; charity-ticketing is a structural response to both scarcity and the reputational cost of high fees/prices.

### Proposed Solution
The platforms' solution is a time-for-access exchange, run as a layer on top of existing ticketing infrastructure. For a primary platform like Etix, the signal is a #build/#improve opportunity: a native charity/volunteer-allocation ticket tier or integration (similar to presale/lottery tooling) could both diversify access and generate positive patron sentiment and AI-assistant grounding content around fairness and access.

## Transcript

![Harry Styles on Together, Together in NYC](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Harry-Styles-on-Together-Together-in-NYC-©-Anthony-Pham.png)

As the industry navigates how best to manage on-sales for high-demand tours, charity-based ticketing initiatives are emerging as an alternative solution to democratise access while giving the industry a new means of harnessing fan engagement.

With demand for live concerts outstripping supply and costs for fans only rising, platforms like Propeller and Earnt are offering a different way to reward fandom by exchanging volunteering and community action for access to high-profile shows, with Harry Styles, Chappell Roan, and Noah Kahan among those taking up the cause.

“It’s the idea of strength in numbers, of a lot of people doing a little bit,” Propeller founder/CEO Brandon Deroche tells *IQ*.

Earnt’s founder Lauren Scott-Harris echoes that idea: “I thought if you could use that fan energy, what amazing things we could accomplish with it!”

For both platforms, the model offers fans an alternative to conventional giveaways and promotional competitions, and provides the industry with a compelling means of engaging artists’ fanbases.

The desire for such ramped up around 2020, when Deroche says, “It was a time that I felt like I didn’t need to beg people to try and get them involved in something. People, both fans and artists, were looking for outlets.”

The US-based operation now serves more than four million music fans on its platform and has worked with stars including Justin Bieber, Shawn Mendes, George Strait, Incubus, Demi Lovato, Sam Smith, Chappell Roan, and Noah Kahan.

Its proposition allows fans to earn points through donations or completing actions, with those points translating into rewards such as tickets or sweepstakes entries. For example, two volunteer shifts might secure a concert ticket, while a festival ticket could come out of eight hours of service.

> “If you don’t have money, you can invest your time”

“If you don’t have money, you can invest your time,” Deroche explains.

Earnt has similarly found that the opportunity for access is a novel motivator. Harry Styles selected the UK-based platform for his global *Together, Together* run behind the partnership’s success during his [one-night-only](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/02/harry-styles-offers-20-tickets-for-one-night-only-manchester-show/) album release show at Manchester’s Co-op Live.

Those selected – about 200 from a pool of over 16,500 applicants – earned standard concert tickets from their four-hour volunteer shift, but Scott-Harris says the significance of being chosen provided a different kind of premium experience.

“They didn’t get a backstage pass or a right-to-kiss-Harry ticket,” she jokes. “But they felt like his chosen special army. They felt like they had been chosen by Harry to go and help their community.”

As such, the experience then becomes part of the reward. Deroche describes Propeller’s campaigns as having “almost fan club qualities”, with artists and audiences developing relationships through shared values.

“Artists are engaging their fans, and they’re creating all this goodwill, but they’re doing that bonding over something they both care about,” he says.

Engagement can extend beyond the initial interaction, both for fans who scored tickets and those who didn’t. And investing early in an artist’s career can be mutually beneficial for all involved, as demonstrated by Propeller’s work with Chappell Roan ahead of her rapid rise to fame following her 2024 slot at Coachella.

“Through her rise in popularity, we were able to kind of ride that wave together in a sense,” Deroche says. “She engaged about 300,000 fans on our platform, and it was something we have never experienced at that level.”

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/smush-webp/2026/10/Fans-volunteering-for-Harry-Styles-©-Earnt-430x269.png.webp)

*Fans volunteer for Harry Styles © Earnt*

For artists and their teams, there is a clear promotional incentive. Tickets are often donated as in-kind inventory from tour, album, or marketing allocations, allowing campaigns to generate attention with well-meaning intentions.

“What better way to let all your fans know that you’re going on tour while also turning that into an opportunity to make a large impact?” Deroche says.

As a business model, Propeller typically earns a percentage of the funds raised, while some non-monetary campaigns require organisations to fund guaranteed outcomes. Earnt is similarly explicit about its use, with its ability to offer brands and artists data acquisition, content opportunities, and press coverage along with measurable social outcomes.

“This is marketing that delivers on the impact as well,” Scott-Harris says. “I can’t imagine how special it must be to be a brand or an artist to get to the end of the year, see the revenue and that the company is doing well, but see they helped generate 400 hours of volunteering in food banks that were otherwise lacking.”

Championing this kind of initiative can also broaden accessibility to live shows, the entry costs of which have rapidly risen in the past decade. Since 2019, the average cost of a ticket rose by 37% for the top 100 tours, according to a recent [Goldman Sachs report](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/10/goldman-sachs-robust-live-touring-and-emerging-markets-fuel-200bn-industry-forecast/), and for some markets, volunteering has provided fans who might otherwise have little prospect of attending the chance to.

Scott-Harris says this model helps create a more “democratised” playing field, where contribution matters more than purchasing power.

> “It’s almost the antithesis of AI”

For high-profile sales, Earnt uses a 24-hour application window and legally compliant randomised ballot, rather than a first-come, first-served system. The team has also partnered with London’s Labyrinth on the Thames series, organising competition-based campaigns around donations for local partners.

Propeller, meanwhile, has aspirations of expanding into the UK, and is also working to introduce artist-centred monthly volunteer crews connected to nonprofits supported by artists’ own foundations or chosen causes.

For both operations, the longer-term measure of success is not necessarily the volume of distributed tickets, but what those tickets and the power of music fandom inspire in their communities.

As more turn to in-person, tangible activities today, with live music at the forefront of that push, there is also the argument that these kinds of initiatives offer something of increasing value, giving like-minded fans a place to meet and collaborate.

At its heart, charity-based ticketing schemes can reward fan loyalty, create meaningful engagement, and offer a different proposition as the industry looks for a new path to managing demand.

“In the age of AI, I think it’s a very interesting expression of what it is to be human, being in community and helping one another. It’s almost the antithesis of AI,” Scott-Harris says.
