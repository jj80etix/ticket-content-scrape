# SeatGeek Launches TourIQ to Value Shows and Set Seat-Level Prices Before Onsale

## Summary

SeatGeek launched TourIQ, a tool that estimates what a show is worth and recommends seat-level (not just section-level) pricing before a show even goes on sale, drawing on nearly two decades of combined primary and resale transaction data plus live demand signals. It's already in use on three unnamed stadium tours, and SeatGeek reports gains from early deployments: 29% more tickets sold and 13% more gross revenue (at a 13% lower average price) on one college-football event, and roughly $1M in identified "lost" revenue at another show versus conventional scaling. TourIQ sits alongside SeatGeek's existing real-time PriceIQ tool but operates pre-onsale, giving promoters/venues a data-driven starting scale while leaving final pricing control with them.

### Key Points
- TourIQ generates a per-event valuation and seat-level (not section-level) price/seat-assignment recommendations before onsale.
- Draws on ~20 years of blended primary + resale transaction data, live demand signals and comparable-event performance.
- Promoters/venues retain control of inputs (min/max price, number of levels, mirroring, front-to-back curve) and the final scale.
- SeatGeek frames it as correcting both underpriced and overpriced inventory, not just as a tool to raise prices.
- Reported early results: 29% more tickets sold / 13% more revenue at 13% lower avg. price (college football); 93% sell-through (+6pp vs. forecast) after a mid-tour rescale; ~$1M in identified unrealized revenue at one stadium show.
- Distinct from SeatGeek's real-time PriceIQ; TourIQ is pre-onsale but can support a deliberate post-onsale rescale.
- Extends SeatGeekIQ, which also powers the AI-driven "Ask SeatGeek" consumer search tool launched in August.

### Industry Problem
Traditional venue scaling prices tickets by broad section/tier rather than individual seat value, routinely under- and over-pricing inventory relative to actual fan demand — leaving both lower sell-through and unrealized revenue on the table, and pushing undervalued seats into the resale market where SeatGeek (not the primary platform or artist) captures the spread.

### Proposed Solution
SeatGeek's solution is a seat-level, data-driven pre-onsale pricing/scaling product built on blended primary+resale data. For an independent primary platform like Etix, the build/improve signal is to evaluate whether seat-level (vs. section-level) pricing recommendations — even without SeatGeek's resale-data advantage — could reduce the gap between initial pricing and realized resale value for promoter clients, which is also a feature that differentiates on the AI/data-tooling axis (Q14) competitors are now actively pursuing.

SeatGeek is pushing its ticket-market intelligence earlier into the live-event lifecycle with TourIQ, a new product designed to help promoters and venues estimate what a show is worth before it is booked and build the initial pricing scale before tickets go on sale.

The new SeatGeekIQ product draws on nearly two decades of primary and resale transaction data, along with live demand signals and comparable-event performance, to estimate the value of a particular artist playing a particular venue on a particular date. From there, TourIQ can recommend price levels and seat assignments based on the modeled value of individual seats rather than relying solely on broad sections or traditional pricing tiers.

SeatGeek says TourIQ is already being used on three stadium tours. The company declined to identify the promoters, artists or tours in response to questions from TicketNews, citing client and artist approval requirements.

The product represents another expansion of SeatGeekIQ, the company’s data and marketplace intelligence platform. In August, TicketNews reported on [SeatGeek’s launch of Ask SeatGeek](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/08/seatgeek-launches-ask-seatgeek-as-ai-referrals-convert-at-twice-the-rate-of-traditional-search/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), which brought the company’s intelligence layer into consumer ticket search. TourIQ puts much of that data to work on the other side of the transaction, before an event has even reached the onsale.

## From Section Pricing to Seat-Level Value

Traditional concert scaling generally groups inventory into a set of price levels based largely on sections, rows or other broad areas of a venue. SeatGeek argues that those boundaries do not necessarily match how fans value individual seats.

A center seat on an upper level, for example, may command approximately the same market value as a less desirable seat in a nominally better section. If both are priced strictly according to their section, one can wind up too expensive while the other is underpriced.

[TourIQ is designed to model those differences before the onsale](https://seatgeek.com/press/SeatGeek%20Launches%20TourIQ?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content). A promoter or venue can enter an artist, building and date to generate an event valuation based on historical marketplace performance, current demand and relevant comparisons. The same analysis is then used to recommend how inventory should be divided among price levels throughout the venue.

SeatGeek told TicketNews that promoters and venues retain control over that process. Clients can adjust inputs including minimum and maximum prices, the number of price levels, whether the scale is mirrored across the building and how prices change from the front to the back of a section.

“TourIQ generates recommended price levels, seat assignments, and revenue projections, but promoters and venues retain control over the inputs and final scale,” Cameron Papp of SeatGeek Communications told TicketNews.

Papp said the technology is intended to reduce the manual work involved in building a scale rather than replace promoter or venue judgment with an autonomous price-setting system.

That distinction matters as ticketing companies increasingly use predictive pricing tools. TourIQ is not designed to continuously alter what a fan sees while shopping for tickets, and SeatGeek says its recommendations can move prices in either direction.

“TourIQ is not a tool to raise prices,” Papp said. “When the data indicates that a conventional price is too high for the demand in a particular part of the building, the recommendation can be lower.”

That creates a somewhat different model from the simplified notion of market-based pricing as a mechanism for charging more when demand is strong. SeatGeek’s pitch is that more granular pricing can identify both seats that traditional scales undervalue and inventory that is likely to move slowly because it has been priced too aggressively.

There is also an explicit connection between primary and secondary ticket markets. SeatGeek says TourIQ uses its blended marketplace intelligence across both primary and resale transactions, though the company declined to disclose how heavily each type of data is weighted.

SeatGeek’s own [TourIQ product explanation](https://seatgeek.com/blog/introducing-touriq?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) notes that an underpriced seat can leave market value for someone else to capture through resale. Asked whether helping artists and promoters capture more of that value during the initial onsale was an objective of TourIQ, SeatGeek framed the goal more broadly as getting initial prices closer to the market value of individual seats while improving sell-through and creating fuller buildings.

## TourIQ and PriceIQ Serve Different Parts of the Sale

TourIQ also differs from [SeatGeekIQ’s existing PriceIQ technology](https://seatgeek.com/iq?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content).

TourIQ is primarily used before the onsale, when a promoter or venue is deciding what an event is worth and establishing the initial seat-level pricing structure. PriceIQ operates on ticket inventory that is already live and can respond to changing demand in real time.

There is some overlap after tickets go on sale. SeatGeek told TicketNews that TourIQ analysis can be used for a deliberate post-onsale rescale if sales develop unevenly, such as shifting seats among existing price levels to better reflect relative demand.

But Papp said TourIQ is “not a real-time engine that continuously changes prices while fans are shopping.”

SeatGeek believes the approach could ultimately shift large-venue concert pricing away from the longstanding reliance on broad sections and tiers.

“We believe TourIQ could help move large-venue concert pricing away from broad section- and tier-based assumptions toward a more predictive, seat-relative approach,” Papp said. “It is intended to give promoters, venues, and artists a stronger data-driven starting point while preserving their guardrails and judgment.”

## SeatGeek Points to Higher Sales at Lower Average Prices

SeatGeek has released several early examples to support its case for more granular scaling, although some of the underlying client information remains confidential.

For the Ole Miss-Louisville football matchup at Nissan Stadium, SeatGeek says TourIQ’s predictive scaling resulted in 29% more tickets sold and 13% more gross revenue than the pre-event forecast, even as the average ticket price came in 13% lower.

The company declined TicketNews’ request for more detail on how the forecast was constructed, how much of the event inventory was priced through TourIQ or whether it had recorded comparable results at other events, saying those details would require client approval.

That means the figures should be viewed as SeatGeek’s reported results against its pre-event forecast rather than as a controlled comparison between TourIQ and an otherwise identical event using conventional pricing.

SeatGeek also says TourIQ was used to rescale a 2026 stadium tour opener after initial sales left uneven gaps around the building. The company says the event ultimately reached 93% sell-through, six percentage points above forecast, with 49% fewer pockets of unsold inventory. At a separate stadium show, SeatGeek says seat-level scaling identified roughly $1 million in potential revenue that would not have been captured under the conventional scale.

The company has not identified either event.

There is an element of returning to SeatGeek’s roots in the new product. When the company launched in 2009, [TicketNews covered SeatGeek as a ticket-price forecasting service](https://www.ticketnews.com/2009/09/new-web-site-seatgeek-aims-to-predict-ticket-prices/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) that analyzed secondary-market information to help consumers determine whether ticket prices were likely to rise or fall.

Seventeen years later, TourIQ applies a much larger pool of primary and resale market data in the other direction: helping the people booking and pricing live events determine what the market may support before the first ticket is sold.
