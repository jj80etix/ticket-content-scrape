# Ohio Commissioner’s VIP Post Malone Tickets Draw Ethics Questions After $250K County Sponsorship

## Summary

An Ohio county commissioner's personal use of six VIP Post Malone concert tickets tied to a $250,000 county sponsorship is drawing an ethics review, though the inspector general has made no finding of wrongdoing and a formal draft referral was never sent. The dispute centers on whether sponsorship-linked event access constitutes a personal gift to an official versus a benefit belonging to the sponsoring public agency. This is a local government-ethics story about VIP ticket allocation, not a ticketing-industry product, policy, or market-structure development.

### Key Points
- Franklin County (OH) paid $250K to sponsor a veteran-focused Post Malone/HARDY concert and received 18 tickets plus VIP/suite access in return.
- Commissioner Erica Crawley personally used 6 of those tickets; no formal ethics violation has been determined.
- Ohio Ethics Commission guidance distinguishes gifts to an individual official from benefits provided to the sponsoring public agency.
- A separate, stalled Ohio bill (HB 409) would ban legislators (not county commissioners) from accepting free sporting-event tickets.

Franklin County Commissioner Erica Crawley’s use of six VIP tickets and hospitality access at a July Post Malone concert is drawing questions under Ohio ethics rules after records showed the benefits were connected to a $250,000 county sponsorship that Crawley helped facilitate.

The Columbus Department of the Inspector General [prepared a draft letter](https://www.aol.com/articles/erica-crawley-vip-tickets-post-173302000.html) asking the Ohio Ethics Commission to review the arrangement after learning about the tickets during a separate investigation into Crawley’s complaints about Columbus police officers at the July 2 Salute 250 concert. The inspector general later clarified that the letter had not been sent and that the department had made no determination that Crawley violated ethics rules. As of Sept. 30, no formal referral had been publicly confirmed.

The issue centers on what Franklin County received in exchange for its financial support of Salute 250 and whether Crawley’s personal use of some of those benefits should be treated differently from the sponsorship itself.

Franklin County, through its Veterans Service Commission, provided $250,000 for Salute 250, a veteran-focused concert at Historic Crew Stadium headlined by Post Malone and HARDY. Crawley has said the county’s support generated 18 tickets for its use, along with access to a suite and VIP area. A Sept. 30 10TV follow-up reported that Crawley used six of those tickets.

Documents obtained by WOSU show that the Veterans Service Commission asked Franklin County Administrator Kenneth Wilson to use money from its general budget allotment for the sponsorship. Wilson approved the $250,000 allocation on June 30, two days before the concert. The Board of Commissioners later approved that expenditure on July 28 as part of a broader $5 million resolution covering purchases for multiple county agencies. Crawley participated in the unanimous vote.

Event producer Zach Ruben told the inspector general that the Veterans Service Commission was the event sponsor and that Crawley “advocated and participated in procuring that partnership on behalf of the county.” Ruben also said Crawley and her guests had been invited into the VIP area.

In a subsequent response to 10TV, Ruben said Salute 250 distributed about 6,000 complimentary tickets to veterans and drew more than 18,000 attendees. ## Ohio Ethics Rules Put Focus on Who Received the Benefit

[Ohio Ethics Commission guidance](https://ethics.ohio.gov/education/factsheets/GiftsandOtherThingsofValue.pdf) specifically addresses complimentary tickets to concerts and sporting events. When public officials are offered tickets to an expensive entertainment event, the commission says they generally must pay the minimum fair-market value of a comparable ticket when the benefit comes from a source covered by the state’s ethics restrictions.

State guidance defines an improper source to include individuals or entities doing or seeking to do business with an official’s agency, regulated by that agency or interested in matters before it. The commission does not set a single dollar threshold for every gift, instead considering both the value of the benefit and its source.

But the commission’s guidance also draws an important distinction between something provided to an individual official and a donation or benefit provided to a public agency. It says the ethics statute does not generally bar donations to public agencies, with the key distinction being whether the public or an individual public servant ultimately benefits.

That distinction leaves the status of the Salute 250 credentials unresolved. The county says the tickets and hospitality were benefits associated with a sponsorship paid for with county funds, while Crawley personally used six of the tickets and brought family members and community partners. The reporting reviewed by TicketNews does not establish a standalone market value for the six tickets, suite or VIP access.

Catherine Turcer, executive director of government watchdog Common Cause Ohio, described the circumstances to 10TV as a potential “gray area.” She told The Dispatch that officials receiving event access should consider whether the special treatment resulted from public actions they had taken or decisions they might make.

The inspector general’s office said it contacted the Ohio Ethics Commission for guidance on how to handle information falling outside its own jurisdiction. The draft referral was prepared in anticipation of that follow-up but remained in the investigative file. The department said it has not concluded that any violation occurred.

The question comes amid a broader Ohio discussion over complimentary event access for public officials. [TicketNews reported last year on House Bill 409](https://www.ticketnews.com/2025/07/ohio-bill-proposes-ban-on-lawmakers-taking-free-tickets/), a separate proposal that would prohibit members of the Ohio General Assembly from accepting free or discounted sporting-event tickets. The measure remains in a House committee. It would not apply to Crawley, a county commissioner, or directly govern concert tickets, but reflects ongoing scrutiny of valuable event access provided to public officials.

Whether the Salute 250 arrangement receives a formal state ethics review remains unclear. If it does, the Ohio Ethics Commission’s own guidance suggests that the distinction between a sponsorship benefit belonging to the county and a substantial personal benefit received by an individual official will be central to the analysis.
