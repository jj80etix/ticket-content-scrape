# ‘The purpose-built music stadium is next’: John Langford on AEG’s APAC expansion

## Summary

In an interview with IQ, AEG International APAC president John Langford outlined plans to build a network of "purpose-built music stadiums" across Asia-Pacific, arguing the region's touring demand has outpaced its venue infrastructure. AEG is actively expanding in China, Thailand and Japan, with new arenas planned in Bangkok (2028) and Osaka (2030), and sees opportunity in markets including South Korea, India and New Zealand.

### Key Points
- AEG frames APAC's gap as infrastructure and systems that "don't exist in the way they do in Europe or North America."
- Current/planned builds: Mercedes-Benz Arena Shanghai (2010), UOB Live Bangkok (2024), IG Arena Nagoya, an 18,000-cap Bangkok arena (2028), and an 18,000-cap Osaka venue (2030).
- Strategy emphasizes flexible venue design and premium hospitality to meet rising fan willingness-to-pay for high-end experiences.
- Langford cites residency-style touring (e.g., Celine Dion's 26-show Paris run) as reshaping where venues get built.

### Highlights
- AEG explicitly frames the shift to fewer, bigger, longer-run shows (residencies) as reshaping demand toward "anchor" venues rather than one-off tour stops.
- Concrete pipeline: an 18,000-cap Bangkok arena (2028) and an 18,000-cap Osaka arena (2030), on top of existing China/Japan/Thailand venues.
- Premium hospitality and flexible-space design are called out as a specific gap in existing APAC venues.

### Industry Problem
APAC's live-entertainment demand has outrun the region's venue infrastructure and the commercial/premium-experience layer around it, limiting how much top-tier touring volume (and associated ticket revenue) the market can currently absorb.

### Proposed Solution
AEG's response is direct venue development plus an "anchor" touring model; for Etix this is a monitor/evolve signal -- as AEG-anchored APAC venues come online (Bangkok 2028, Osaka 2030), watch for RFPs or ticketing-partner selection processes that could be a market-entry opportunity, particularly where primary-platform competitors haven't yet established footholds.

## ‘The purpose-built music stadium is next’: John Langford on AEG’s APAC expansion

In an in-depth discussion with IQ, the leader unpacks the company's ambitions as it looks to build a network of venues to connect the region

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/smush-webp/2025/08/AEG-Europe_John-Langford-scaled.jpg.webp)

John Langford

The industry’s shift toward residency-style touring and a rising number of stadium-level acts could result in the development of ‘music-first stadiums’, says AEG’s John Langford, as the company looks to build a network of anchor venues to connect touring acts with evolving fanbases in the Asia-Pacific region.

At the top end of touring, more artists are doing more shows, but often in fewer markets, with extended runs providing a sizable economic return by bringing in external fan bases. The trend could have a hand in reshaping the venue ecosystem, specifically in a region building up its touring pipeline to match rapidly expanding demand and an increasingly affluent audience.

“Fans are coming to the bands rather than the bands going to the fans, and that presents a really interesting opportunity to develop venues that work for that kind of model – I think the purpose-built music stadium is going to be the next exciting thing,” AEG International’s president, APAC, tells *IQ*, in one of his first in-depth conversations since stepping into the role [in April](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/02/john-langford-upped-to-aeg-internationals-apac-president/).

“What we’ve seen post-COVID is fewer touring stops and bigger shows – Celine Dion is a perfect example,” says the Singapore-based leader, referencing her [26-show stand](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/06/celine-dion-adds-ten-more-dates-to-paris-residency/) at Paris La Défense Arena.

About half of the world’s population – around 4 billion people – live no more than a five-hour flight from Hong Kong, underscoring the interconnectivity of populations in India, China, Singapore, Vietnam, Japan, and the Philippines. And that audience does seem to have an insatiable hunger for live entertainment to sustain such developments, having emerged from the pandemic as [a new beast](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2025/10/inside-asias-live-boom-its-only-getting-started/).

“You’ve got this demand for live entertainment that we’ve never seen before,” he says. “That’s right across the world, that’s not unique to APAC, but I think what is unique to APAC is that the infrastructure and systems behind that to drive touring at scale don’t exist in the way that it does in Europe or North America, and that’s where the real opportunity is.”

But understanding the dynamics of each market, both from a country lens and a city-by-city view, in a region defined by fragmentation is an integral facet of the development of any venue size.

> “Disciplined, market-led development will be critical to making that growth sustainable”

“There are so many factors at play in that, and a key part of what our role is is to educate those kinds of policymakers and financiers on where the returns are, and those returns are not just commercial returns,” he explains. “Just because you build a 65,000-capacity stadium with a roof and carrying capacity, it doesn’t mean [Taylor Swift](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2024/07/philippines-taylor-swift-ready-stadium/), Ed Sheeran, or Oasis is actually going to play your venue.”

AEG International has been expanding its foothold in the region for over two decades, with the company opening its first APAC venue in 2010, the Mercedes-Benz Arena (cap. 18,000) in Shanghai, China. To be successful here, Langford shares, requires a key understanding of the market at play and the collaborators involved, with governments a frequent facilitator and commercial entities often collaborating to cash in on live.

“With demand for shared live experiences remaining incredibly strong, the key is ensuring that new infrastructure is developed in the right place, at the right scale, and in step with the market. The opportunity across the region is significant, but disciplined, market-led development will be critical to making that growth sustainable,” he says, adding that improving existing infrastructure may make more sense in certain territories.

In addition to its presence in China, the company is also actively dialling in on Thailand and Japan. In Bangkok, AEG has an alliance with local developers The Mall Group, opening [UOB Live](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/06/uob-live-success-underpins-aegs-thailand-expansion-plans/) (6,000) in 2024 and an 18,000-capacity Bangkok arena by late 2028. And over in the domestic-heavy market of Japan, AEG helped bring about Nagoya’s [IG Arena](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/07/breaking-the-nagoya-skip-ig-arena-on-goals-to-shake-up-japanese-arena-routing/) (17,500), which opened just over a year ago, and will open another 18,000-capacity venue in Osaka by 2030.

> “People want good quality and great value, but they’ll pay for high-end experiences”

Beyond that, Langford signals interest in building a pipeline to connect “everywhere from New Zealand to India” to facilitate a touring model that mirrors widespread, multi-market activity in other regions. Beyond Southeast Asia, he also nods toward the “valuable music market” of South Korea and the “fast-growing” Indian circuit as ones of note.

“What we’re trying to do is establish a number of anchors, so that you’ve got something established that your artist can build on. The benefit of that is that everybody wins. Artists get bigger guarantees, you can put your production together, you know what the established route will look like, you get wider exposure for fans, and it’s great for brands and sponsors to jump on board. That’s the goal,” he says.

AEG’s APAC development model is bolstered by fans’ rising expectations, with a shift toward premium experiences underlining the circuit’s expansion and enhancement.

“People want good quality and great value, but they’ll pay for high-end experiences. But a lot of the venue infrastructure across APAC doesn’t provide for those kinds of experiences. When we’re looking at new opportunities, we’re starting to build those and design those into venues,” Langford says, referencing the volume of premium suites and hospitality at IG Arena “that don’t exist anywhere else in Japan”.

“A lot of the existing venues have typically just been sports venues or gymnasiums. They’ve served a purpose where you’ve got a certain number of seats and a promoter can come in and deliver a show, but it’s not a show at the level where a fan would expect that kind of quality, or the band, for that matter,” he says.

> “Consider flexibility as much as possible”

More than anything, venues coming online need to be built with flexibility at the forefront, to adjust to the needs of changing content or consumer wants to be future-proofed for whatever comes next.

“How do you go from having a rock show one night where you’ve got a vodka-heavy, beer-heavy audience, and then the next night you’ve got a water-heavy K-pop audience? Are you able to flip bars into merchandise units? Can you have a hospitality space that can be a single corporate for 12 people one night, and the next night it opens up and expands into something that can host 150 people? That’s the real challenge,” he explains.

“We don’t know what the future is going to look like. But if you consider flexibility as much as possible, it will make it easier.”

That line of thinking needs to extend behind the curtain as well, with increasing volumes of production and technology requiring increased flexibility in loading capabilities, backstage facilities, and vehicle loading considerations. Langford says that in Asia, most local productions are not built for a touring model, meaning the next generation of venues need to be able to accommodate the major international tours coming through.

All in all, the key to the region’s myriad markets is understanding the audiences who power them. When asked what the industry underestimates about APAC, Langford says: “The sheer size of the audiences and their passion, it’s like nothing I’ve ever seen anywhere else in the world. “I don’t think it should be underestimated, and it should not be overlooked.”
