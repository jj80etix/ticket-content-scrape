# Kansas City Clears $600M Funding Plan for $1.9B Royals Stadium

## Summary

Kansas City's City Council approved a tax increment financing plan committing up to $600 million in public funding toward a new $1.9 billion stadium for the Royals, with total bond repayment projected near $1.4 billion over three decades. The ballpark is part of a roughly $3 billion Crown Center redevelopment and is expected to open for the 2030 MLB season.

### Key Points
- Council approved TIF plan and bonds for up to $600M toward a $1.9B Royals stadium.
- Total bond repayment costs projected at nearly $1.4B over 30 years.
- Part of a ~$3B Crown Center district redevelopment with housing and retail.
- New ballpark targeted to open for the 2030 MLB season.
- Mayor Quinton Lucas frames it as key to retaining the Royals and Hallmark Cards.

Kansas City officials have approved a financing plan committing up to $600 million in public funding toward a new $1.9 billion stadium for the Kansas City Royals, with the city’s total bond repayment costs projected to reach nearly $1.4 billion over the next three decades.

The City Council voted Thursday, Oct. 8, to establish a tax increment financing (TIF) plan and authorize bonds supporting the project, according to the [Kansas City Star](//www.kansascity.com/news/local/article317545028.html?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content"). The new ballpark is planned for the Crown Center district and is expected to open in time for the 2030 Major League Baseball season.

The city’s financing commitment is part of a larger redevelopment project expected to bring approximately $3 billion in investment to Kansas City’s urban core, including a new sports and entertainment district with housing, retail and other commercial development.

Mayor Quinton Lucas described the council’s approval as a significant step toward keeping the Royals and Hallmark Cards in Kansas City.

"Today Kansas City voted to keep the Major League Baseball and Hallmark Cards in Kansas City, clearing the way for a transformational baseball-centered district that delivers for taxpayers, workers, and neighborhoods," Lucas said.

The financing plan calls for the city to issue bonds generating approximately $537 million toward construction of the ballpark and Royals team offices. However, interest payments over the coming decades are projected to increase the total repayment obligation to approximately $1.39 billion.

Rather than issuing the bonds all at once, Kansas City plans to spread issuance across the construction schedule to manage annual debt obligations.

To cover those costs, the city intends to redirect certain tax revenues generated in and around the stadium district over 30 years. Those revenues include food and beverage taxes, hotel taxes, public safety sales taxes and parks sales taxes.

A central assumption behind the financing arrangement is that the new stadium will attract significantly more fans than the Royals’ current home at Kauffman Stadium.

A financial analysis conducted by Chicago-based consulting firm Hunden Partners projects that attendance at Royals games will increase by 20% following the move to Crown Center. That growth is expected to contribute to increased commercial activity and tax collections in the surrounding development area.

According to the Kansas City Star, Hunden Partners representatives told city officials that the stadium project is projected to generate more than $1.4 billion in revenue within the designated tax redirection areas through 2059, enough to cover the anticipated bond repayment obligations.

The consulting firm’s analysis characterized the projections as conservative, noting that they do not account for organic business growth in the district over that period.

For the Royals, the projected attendance increase represents an important component of the stadium’s financial outlook. Additional fans would mean greater demand for game tickets and could support increased spending on concessions, hospitality and other activities in the surrounding entertainment district.

However, the city’s ability to meet its debt obligations will ultimately depend on the tax revenues generated by the stadium and surrounding development over the coming decades.

City officials have presented the stadium as an opportunity to attract substantial private investment while transforming Crown Center into a neighborhood with year-round commercial activity.

Council member Wes Rogers, who represents the city’s 2nd District, praised the broader economic implications of the development.

"We’re going to change downtown for a generation, probably longer," Rogers said. "This is a good project."

The financing arrangement also anticipates support from the state, while private investment associated with the broader development is expected to total approximately $2 billion.

With the council’s approval, Kansas City has finalized a major portion of its proposed financial support for the stadium, moving the Royals closer to their planned 2030 opening.
