# State AGs Set Child-Safety and Privacy Expectations for Any Sale of Character.AI

## Summary

A bipartisan coalition of 39 state AGs filed a notice in Character.AI's Chapter 11 bankruptcy case outlining child-safety and data-privacy protections they will demand of any buyer, citing the 23andMe bankruptcy-sale precedent.

### Key Points
- Notice filed Oct. 14, 2026, covering 39 states plus D.C.
- Character.AI had 21.6M+ U.S. accounts as of March 2025, including 2.1M+ declared minors.
- Cites California's 23andMe settlement as the closest precedent for a bankruptcy-sale privacy fight.
- AI/consumer-privacy bankruptcy matter; no ticketing-industry relevance.

## Transcript

[Skip to content](#lxb_af-loop)

State attorneys general (AGs) are signaling that AI chatbot user data will face scrutiny, even in bankruptcy. On October 14, 2026, a bipartisan coalition of AGs from 39 states and the District of Columbia filed a notice in the Chapter 11 case of Character Technologies, Inc. (Character.AI), outlining the child-safety and privacy protections they will press for in any sale. The notice comes just days after California announced a $6 million settlement with the successor to 23andMe, resolving claims over 23andMe’s 2023 data breach and ending California’s appeal of the 2025 bankruptcy sale of customers’ genetic data, the closest precedent for a Character.AI sale. AI companies, acquirers, and investors should take note.

### Why the States Say the Data Is High Risk

The notice focuses on the sensitivity of Character.AI’s data: account information, including declared ages; chat text and call audio, including health disclosures; behavioral and engagement data; device and tracking identifiers; and payment information. According to the notice, Character.AI had more than 21.6 million U.S. accounts as of March 2025, including more than 2.1 million with a declared age under 18.

The states say chat logs are hard to protect in a sale. Sensitive details may be scattered across long conversations, and references to jobs, homes, or locations can identify users even after names are removed. Health disclosures in those conversations may also trigger consent requirements for sensitive data under state privacy laws, whatever the user’s age. The states also cite consumer-group testing in which “therapist” characters promised confidentiality, contrary to Character.AI’s privacy policy.

Because age verification was allegedly inadequate from 2022 through 2025, minors’ data may be labeled as adult data. This is important because several state data privacy laws require opt-in consent before selling minors’ data or using it for targeted advertising, and some of which extend protections to users up to age 18.

AI training adds another complication. If chat content was used to train or fine-tune models, deleting the underlying records may not remove the data’s influence on those models. Buyers and regulators will likely ask whether the models themselves are part of the data being sold.

### What the States Expect From Any Buyer

Character.AI has voluntarily barred users under 18 from open-ended chat, but the states allege the restriction can be circumvented, does not cover features such as “Stories,” and could be reversed by a buyer. They “would advocate” that any purchaser be prepared to comply with state consumer protection and privacy laws, including:

1. Age assurance beyond self-declared birthdates;
2. Limits on minors’ access to open-ended chats and protection from harmful content elsewhere;
3. Removal of characters posing as therapists, doctors, attorneys, or other licensed professionals;
4. Crisis protocols for self-harm, suicide, eating-disorder, and substance-abuse content that a click-through cannot bypass;
5. A privacy program with reasonable safeguards, accurate disclosures, risk assessments, and consent before processing sensitive data;
6. Disclosure of data sales and targeted advertising, with account-level opt-outs that honor prior choices;
7. Limits on using chat content for model training or advertising without valid consumer or parental consent;
8. Access rights and a chance to delete data before any sale; and
9. No misleading claims about safety, suitability for minors, whether characters are human, or their credentials.

These are potential contract terms, not court-ordered conditions. Under Section 363(b)(1) of the Bankruptcy Code, if a debtor’s privacy policy prohibits transferring personal data, the debtor can sell that data only if the sale is consistent with the policy, or if the court approves it after appointing a consumer privacy ombudsman under Section 332. The court must also find that the sale would not violate applicable nonbankruptcy law. Each state has reserved the right to request an ombudsman and to object to any sale, bidding procedures, or plan.

### 23andMe as a Possible Template

The 23andMe bankruptcy shows how this could unfold. After 23andMe filed for Chapter 11 in March 2025, a coalition of AGs objected to the sale of customers’ genetic data, and the court appointed a consumer privacy ombudsman, who recommended obtaining customers’ affirmative consent before any transfer. The court nonetheless approved a $305 million sale to TTAM Research Institute, a nonprofit formed by 23andMe’s co-founder, finding no conflict with applicable law. Both bankruptcy and district courts rejected California’s objection under its Genetic Information Privacy Act.

Nonetheless, the sale terms addressed many of the concerns raised in the AGs’ objections. TTAM agreed to enhanced data security, risk analysis, an advisory board, compliance with comprehensive state privacy laws without exception, continued deletion rights, and a requirement that any future buyer adopt the same commitments.

Bankruptcy also did not end the states’ claims over the company’s earlier data breach. In July 2026, the successor company agreed to pay $18 million to 42 states over the 2023 breach. On October 19, 2026, California announced a separate $6 million settlement resolving its breach and misrepresentation claims and ending its appeal of the sale order. The California settlement is pending court approval.

Three lessons carry over. First, states may be able to shape the terms of a sale even if they cannot stop it outright, and those terms bind the buyer. Second, the buyer matters: 23andMe’s data went to an entity tied to its founder, and commentators warn that the next sale to an unrelated buyer may carry more risk. Third, the issues in Character.AI’s case may be more complex than in 23andMe’s. Chat logs are less structured than genetic data, the users may include minors who misrepresented their ages, and the data may already be built into AI models.

### The Broader Enforcement Picture

The notice follows investigations by several states that began after a 2024 wrongful death suit alleged that a Character.AI chatbot cultivated an emotionally dependent and sexually exploitative relationship with a 14-year-old boy and encouraged his suicidal thoughts. To date, Kentucky is the only state AG to sue, naming the company and its two co-founders in January 2026. In May 2026, Pennsylvania’s State Board of Medicine sued a character posing as a licensed doctor.

While AGs and regulators pursue individual companies, lawmakers are also writing similar protections into law. California’s Adam’s Law (SB 1119), signed September 10, 2026, and taking effect in 2027, is described by its authors as the nation’s first comprehensive framework governing AI chatbots and children. It requires age assurance, risk assessments, crisis referrals, parent-controlled default settings, safeguards against harmful outputs, limits on targeted advertising to children, independent audits, and a private right of action for certain violations.

### What Companies Should Do Now

- **Diligence the data.** Buyers should map what user data exists, where it came from, the privacy policy in effect when it was collected, how users’ ages were determined, and whether it was used to train models. Buyers should also assess exposure to pending or threatened state claims based on pre-bankruptcy conduct, which 23andMe shows can survive the sale process. Review these issues early with counsel.
- **Review privacy policy transfer language now.** Policies, especially their “business transfer” clauses, will largely decide what can be sold in a future deal or bankruptcy. Companies should review them before financial distress.
- **Review chatbot representations.** Companies should evaluate chatbot claims and disclosures, including statements by AI characters themselves, for consumer protection risks.
- **Assess safeguards and commitments.** As 23andMe shows, commitments negotiated with state AGs can become binding sale terms that carry over to future buyers. Companies should consider their enforceability and long-term cost.
- **Prepare for Adam’s Law.** Companies offering companion chatbots in California should assess their readiness and work with counsel to identify necessary compliance changes before 2027.

Bankruptcy does not shield user data from state scrutiny. The Character.AI notice shows the states applying a framework developed in 23andMe, with closer attention to minors, to AI characters’ behavior, and to model training.
