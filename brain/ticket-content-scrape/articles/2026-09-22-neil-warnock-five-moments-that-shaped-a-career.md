# Neil Warnock: Five moments that shaped a career

## Summary

IQ Magazine profiles veteran UTA agent Neil Warnock MBE on his 80th birthday, recounting career-shaping moments across six decades that helped establish modern touring-agency practices, including an early shift toward agents working directly for artists rather than promoters.

### Key Points
- Profile marks Neil Warnock's 80th birthday and six-decade career as a touring agent, now at UTA.
- Highlights his early career shift to representing artists' interests directly rather than promoters'.
- Warnock is moving into a mentorship phase for younger agents while remaining active with his roster.

## Transcript

Having celebrated his 80th birthday earlier this year, the pioneering music agent recounts how he built much of the industry we know today

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Neil-Warnock-1.png)

Neil Warnock with George Benson and Michael Jackson

Having celebrated his 80th birthday earlier this year, Neil Warnock MBE is entering a new phase of his career: sharing his decades of experience to mentor younger colleagues, while devoting more time to his artist roster.

For a man who has seen everything, and literally invented many aspects of the agency business, Warnock has more energy than many colleagues half his age, and is regularly first in UTA’s office in central London.

Here are five moments that shaped Warnock’s legendary career, taken from *IQ’* s in-depth look back at the agent’s six decades in the business, which you can read in full [here](https://www.iqmagazine.com/magazine/architects-neil-warnock/).

**‘We work for the artist’  
**“When I joined the Bryan Morrison team, that all changed, because their outlook was, ‘We work for the artist. We don’t work for the promoter,’ Warnock remembers. “The promoter made money because the artist was there, not because they rented a ballroom.”

That sea-change in attitude coincided with Warnock developing his skills outside of the London club scene, booking his clients into venues elsewhere in the UK, and starting to develop their careers in Europe where the traditional jazz promoters still had the opinion that the musicians who helped them sell tickets were employees, rather than artists.

“The jazzers really didn’t understand rock & roll, but thankfully, Berry Visser and Leon Ramakers were starting out in Holland, MAMA Concerts was getting underway in Germany, and EMA was making waves in Sweden, so I came up through the ranks with them.”

> “I suggested they accompany us to the festival and temporarily unbond the gear so they could watch the band use it”

Such forays overseas led to some naïve situations. “I remember booking Pink Floyd for the Holland Pop Festival in 1970 and the production manager onsite telling me they could not get the band’s gear in because we didn’t have a carnet. To be honest, I didn’t know what a carnet was,” he admits.

Ever the problem solver, Warnock visited the port where the gear was impounded and noticed the uniformed customs officials were young and had long hair.

“They had never seen Pink Floyd, so I suggested they accompany us to the festival, temporarily unbond the gear so they could watch the band use it, then take it all back to the docks and get it rebonded. So that’s what we did.”

**Excursions to the continent  
**While with Bryan Morrison, Warnock saw a different way of working as he began to take acts abroad to Europe. At the company, he began planning more and more excursions to Europe for his clients, where venues such as the ZOOM club in Germany and Paradiso in the Netherlands would provide two shows a night for acts.

“It was still left over from that jazz way of working,” he observes. “But while in England, the band might get £150 a show, or £175 if you were lucky; in Europe, it was £50, £60, £70, but they could offer work every night, and often twice a night; the promoters would cover hotels, and it was all cash: no tax. So, it was primitive, but it was lucrative.”

Recalling those rudimentary deals, Warnock reveals, “Promoters would fly in with the cash – usually local currency –with all the deals agreed by phone, as Telex was still to be invented.”

And as the circuit of international venues expanded, the proficiency of promoters around Europe was measured very much on a gig-to-gig basis. “Once you’d done one gig with a new promoter and the band would tell you what a great show it was, you’d look to go with that promoter the next time, too.

**Across the pond: The end of the Bron days  
**Unhappy about the potential new management at NEMS (with whom he had worked since the late 60s) when it was acquired in 1973, Warnock had jumped on an offer from Gerry Bron to join his agency. Ever ambitious, the enormity of the North American business enticed Warnock on a pioneering exploration – becoming the first person to attempt agency operations on both sides of the Atlantic.

By 1980 he tells *IQ* that “toward the end of the Bron days, I’d opened Cricket Talent and Booking in New York”.

“I had good acts – Simple Minds, Orchestral Manoeuvres in the Dark, Echo & the Bunnymen, The Revillos – but they didn’t work that often in America, so I was a little naïve, and it didn’t work, ” he refelcts.

> “I was giving 50% of my profit to somebody who was not interested in what I was doing as an agent”

Bailing on that venture, it also emerged that Gerry Bron was experiencing financial issues, prompting Warnock’s 1980 departure. However, buoyed by his experience of running the shop for seven years, he launched his own company – The Agency Group.

“At Bron, I was giving 50% of my profit to somebody who was not interested in what I was doing as an agent. So when I left, I took ten agents with me. And thank god I did, because within months Gerry had gone bankrupt.”

**Launching The Agency Group  
**After leaving Bron, Warnock found day one success with the launch of The Agency Group. Having learned hard lessons via Cricket Talent, he was not going to be stumped a second time. “At The Agency Group, I had Uriah Heep, Nazareth, The Stranglers, Blue Öyster Cult, and two or three other acts who could go to America and tour for months. That gave me cash flow, which provided the basis to build a company from,” he recalls.

The pioneering move took its toll, however. “As the company developed, I bought an apartment in New York, and as we grew, we opened offices in Toronto, Los Angeles, and Nashville, building up to over 2,000 clients,” recounts Warnock.

> “the data got more important; that would also be part of our responsibility”

“I would be in America for at least one week every month, sometimes more, to stay hands-on. But it cost me two marriages.”

Personal heartbreak aside, Warnock has no regrets, and the model he created, with operations on either side of the Atlantic, has since been mimicked by all of the major agencies.

This was the moment it became clear that the business had become “more and more sophisticated, and we had to make sure that as the money got bigger, and the data got more important, that would also be part of our responsibility,” he comments, adding that a new pivotal aspect of the job also emerged around this time: “We also had to make sure we made things tax efficient for our clients, and that is complicated.”

**Merging with UTA  
**Ahead of the famed 2015 deal with UTA, Warnock reflects that he had entertained “many discussions with major companies over the years, but I could never get to a situation where I felt comfortable”. But he says that “when UTA and I started talking, they really didn’t have a music department of any sophistication, so there were a lot of reasons for us to get together”.

“In a meeting with the UTA board, the one thing that impressed me more than anything was that their mantra matched my own: if you look after the artists, the money is going to fall to you as a business. So, subsequently, we did the deal.

> “I still see myself as a jobbing agent”

“Now, with David Kramer and Bob Roback leading the UTA board, plus Sam Kirby Yoh and David Zedek heading up music, this is a vibrant and exciting time.”

Eleven years into the merger, Warnock couldn’t be happier. “I’m still looking to build out a company, and I’ve still got the enthusiasm every day to work with young agents,” he tells *IQ.*

Among his numerous claims to fame, Warnock has taken Michael Jackson to India and booked Pink Floyd into Russia when the Iron Curtain was still in place.

And decades later, his zest for helping musicians realise their dreams is undiminished. “I’m the luckiest bastard in the world,” he exclaims. “I’m still taking on new acts. I recently started working with Public Image Limited, for instance – and I love working across different genres.

“I represent Megadeth, and also did Dolly Parton – they both happened to have the same manager, Danny Nozel, who was the production manager of Slipknot, whom I also represented. I’m blessed with the fact that I can book the likes of David Gilmour, Alfie Boe, George Benson, Megadeth, because I still see myself as a jobbing agent.”
