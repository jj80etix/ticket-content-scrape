# Price cuts drive Corona Capital’s ‘best year in three years’

## Summary

Mexico City's Corona Capital festival had its strongest ticket sales in three years after promoter OCESA cut prices 10% for 2026 and made its first concerted push into the US market, selling 220,000 tickets for a lineup including The Strokes and Gorillaz. Director Leizer Guss attributes the turnaround to belt-tightening across production costs rather than competitive pressure from other festivals.

### Key Points
- 220,000 tickets sold for the Nov 20-22 edition; OCESA calls it the best year in three years, though not at post-pandemic boom levels.
- Ticket prices cut 10% for 2026, funded by savings across catering, crew scheduling, and a trimmed talent budget.
- First-ever allocation (800 passes) sold through US platform Front Gate Tickets, backed by dedicated marketing spend.
- OCESA frames the decision as a response to competing demand from touring stadium/arena shows in Mexico, not rival festivals.

## Transcript

## Price cuts drive Corona Capital’s ‘best year in three years’

The OCESA festival lowered ticket prices by 10% for 2026, made its first push into the US market, and broadened its accessibility work

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Corona-Capital.jpeg)

Corona Capital recorded its strongest ticket sales in three years, after promoter OCESA cut prices by 10% and made its first concerted attempt to sell the Mexico City festival to American fans.

According to the promoter, the 20-22 November festival sold 220,000 tickets for a line-up including The Strokes, Gorillaz, Twenty One Pilots, The xx, The Black Crowes. Manic Street Preachers, and Angine de Poitrine.

“For festivals in general it’s been a hard year, but Corona Capital is having its best year in three years,” OCESA director of festivals Leizer Guss tells *IQ*. “It’s not as good as the boom after the pandemic, but it’s better than the last two.”

Asked if the results were level with pre-pandemic numbers, he said attendance was roughly level with 2019, although inflation meant the top line looked stronger – but also explained that inflation was also hitting production costs and artist fees. At a time when many festivals are struggling to balance the books without passing too much of the burden of costs onto fans, this year Corona Capital announced its 2026 tickets would be 10% cheaper.

Memo Parra, director of international events at OCESA, says the decision was made not because of competition with other festivals, but the sheer volume of stadium and arena shows passing through Mexico.

> “We tightened our belts, we lowered ticket prices, and it worked”

“Any fan has to choose: do I go to Bruno Mars, or do I go to the festival?” he says. “We tightened our belts, we lowered ticket prices, and it worked.”

Parra says savings came from many small cuts across all budgets including catering, and crew scheduling. In Mexico, production shifts run for eight hours, and the team planned the build to avoid tipping crews into a second shift through mistakes or poor planning. The talent budget was trimmed too, with Parra spending less on artists than in 2025.

“We made it happen,” he says. “It was an overall reduction on every level of the festival – a little bit here, a little bit there, because you cannot take away big elements.”

For the first time, OCESA also gave an allocation of 800 passes for sale through US platform Front Gate Tickets and backed it with marketing spend.

“We sold them all,” says Guss. “We called it a win.”

Parra says the international share of the audience is “getting bigger and bigger” each year. Guss, though, thinks the official figures understate the reality. Since the pandemic, large numbers of Americans and other foreign nationals have relocated to Mexico, and once they open a Mexican bank account, ticketing companies log them as domestic customers.

“If I look at the demographics Ticketmaster or our marketing team give us, and then you look at the actual people out there, it’s like something’s off,” he says. “It’s because they live here now.”

**Upgrades on site**  
Elsewhere, the festival enlarged its VIP area, and Guss says 2026 is Corona Capital’s best year ever for VIP sales.

“I think it’s a trend globally that fans who go to festivals want the most comfortable experience,” he says. “We really saw it in the sales this year.”

The festival also deployed new pre-festival social media strategies, which creates a strong feeling of community at the event. Its teaser campaign invited fans to send in photos and stories from past editions. Corona Capital co-creator and OCESA’s director of festival marketing Armando Calvillo says it received between 3,000 and 4,000 submissions, some of which were used on billboards around the city.

> “We think everyone has the right to enjoy Corona Capital”

**Sustainability and access**  
Corona Capital has held ISO 20121 certification for sustainable events since 2019 and is re-audited every year. Calvillo believes only Rock in Rio shares the distinction among festivals in the Americas.

A composting team works the food areas, separating organic waste at source so that recyclables are not contaminated. Calvillo says audiences now recognise the team and help with sorting. Rather than offering merchandise in exchange for returned cups, as some festivals do, Corona Capital relies on a large cleaning crew that sweeps each area as crowds move between stages – people Calvillo calls “heroes”.

This year the festival has talked about its sustainability and access work far more prominently across its website, app and social channels. Calvillo says the festival provides a powerful opportunity to inform attendees about sustainability, adding that fans are increasingly interested in sustainable measures.

On access, the festival began working with sponsor Corona in 2018 to provide haptic vests for deaf fans. Twenty were available that first year; the programme now offers around 100. The festival also had Mexican Sign Language signers on the main and second stages for every artist for the first time, which proved popular with attendees and artists, who could be seen engaging with them as part of their performance.

Calvillo credits Coldplay’s public communication around its own haptic vest programme as an influence on the festival’s decision to talk more openly about the work. Other access measures included a raised platform, dedicated toilets, staff assigned to help people with access needs, and a quiet space for neurodiverse people or people with mental health issues.

The festival also introduced a dedicated sober area, after a fan in recovery wrote in to say that a site full of bars, and music they associated with drinking, made festivals difficult. A separate space lets any attendee rest and disconnect for a while before rejoining what Calvillo calls the “happy chaos”.

“We think everyone has the right to enjoy Corona Capital,” he says.
