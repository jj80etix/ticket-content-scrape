# New Orleans’ Howlin’ Wolf Latest Venue To Sue StubHub Citing ‘False Scarcity’ Tactics

## Summary

New Orleans venue The Howlin' Wolf sued StubHub in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Louisiana, alleging its "false scarcity" tactics violate consumer protection law. The suit cites a Sept. 7 show where all 100 GA tickets were available directly from the venue at $20, while StubHub advertised "Only 1% of tickets left!" at $76 despite the venue having sold none — evidence, the filing argues, of speculative "ghost ticket" selling. The suit also targets StubHub's "fictitious former price" and "false superlative" ("lowest price") claims as false advertising. It follows a similar suit by Vermont's The Stone Church in August; StubHub calls the claims "without merit."

### Key Points
- Filed in U.S. District Court, Eastern District of Louisiana, by Howlin' Wolf's parent company.
- Example cited: Sept. 7 Hot 8 Brass Band show — 100 GA tickets available at $20 direct from venue; StubHub listed "Only 1% of tickets left!" at $76.
- Suit alleges StubHub's listing itself proves speculative ("spec") selling since no ticket had been sold to resell.
- Also challenges StubHub's "fictitious former price" and "false superlative" (lowest-price) marketing claims as false advertising.
- Second small-venue suit against StubHub in as many months (Vermont's The Stone Church, August 2026).
- StubHub disputes the claims as "without merit"; damages sought are undisclosed.

### Highlights
- Concrete price comparison: $20 direct vs. $76 on StubHub for the same GA inventory.
- Legal theory directly implicates "speculative ticketing" — the practice named in Q11's BOTS Act/speculative-ticketing-ban tracking.
- Pattern signal: two independent-venue suits against StubHub within ~a month.

### Industry Problem
Patrons face deceptive urgency and pricing cues (fabricated scarcity, inflated "was" prices) on secondary marketplaces that can lead to paying inflated prices or receiving invalid "ghost tickets" with no real underlying inventory — a trust and entry-risk problem for buyers and a reputational one for venues whose direct-sale prices are undercut in appearance.

### Proposed Solution
The suits themselves don't propose a fix beyond legal remedy, but the pattern reinforces the case for a primary platform like Etix to market verified-inventory guarantees (no speculative listings, real-time accurate availability) as a differentiator against resale marketplaces, and to monitor whether litigation outcomes create pressure for verified-inventory standards industry-wide.

New Orleans venue The Howlin’ Wolf is the latest to take secondary ticketing platform StubHub to court, saying the seller’s “false scarcity” tactic violates consumer protection laws.

In the suit filed in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Louisiana, the club’s parent company cites as an example a Sept. 7 performance by Hot 8 Brass Band in the small “Den” portion of the club. While all 100 general admission tickets were available on Howlin’ Wolf’s site (for $20), StubHub’s page for the event said “Only 1% of tickets left” and advertised $76 tickets.

“This tactic is based on a fabricated impression that tickets will shortly be unavailable when there is, in reality, sufficient inventory, adding phrases such as ‘Only 1% of tickets left!’ or ‘Last Tickets!’ to ensure that buyers buy then and there, at whatever price StubHub advertises, or lose out on seeing their favorite band, comedian, or poet,” the suit reads, in part.

The existence of the listing itself also shows, the filing claims, that StubHub resellers are engaging in speculative ticketing. Since the Howlin’ Wolf had not actually sold any tickets to the performance, the reseller could not possibly have had a ticket to resell.

“While StubHub’s policies nominally prohibit resellers from spec-selling, those policies are rarely if ever enforced, despite the company being fully aware of this practice. A speculative seller who is ultimately unable to acquire an actual, valid ticket for the event leaves the consumer holding what is, in substance, a ‘ghost ticket’ – a purported ticket unsupported by any corresponding, valid inventory,” the filing reads. “Because this defect is frequently undetectable until the consumer arrives at the venue, the practical consequence of spec-selling is that a ticketholder can be turned away and denied entry at the door, despite having paid full price in the good-faith belief that she purchased a valid ticket.”

The suit says StubHub has no incentive to verify inventory with the venues because its revenue is generated through the resale transaction rather than the original purchase (if any exists).

The suit goes on to claim that StubHub’s use of “fictitious former price” — where it posts a comparison between a previous price on StubHub, even though the ticket may still be available for less through a primary seller — and “false superlative” — claiming “lowest price” — also violate false advertisement laws.

Damages sought are left undisclosed,

StubHub, in a [statement](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/new-orleans-venue-the-howlin-wolf-sues-stubhub-over-false-scarcity-ticket-ads-citing-a-24-94-ticket-listed-at-76/) to Music Business Worldwide, said the claims are “without merit.”

The Howlin’ Wolf is just the latest smaller venue to take on the secondary giant. Vermont’s The Stone Church also did so, [making similar claims](https://www.vermontpublic.org/local-news/2026-08-20/brattleboro-concert-venue-sues-national-ticket-reseller), in August.
