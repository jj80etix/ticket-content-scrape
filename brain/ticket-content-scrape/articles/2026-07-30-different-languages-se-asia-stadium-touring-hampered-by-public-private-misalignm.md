# ‘Different languages’: SE Asia stadium touring hampered by public-private ‘misalignment’

## Summary

DisrupTEIK Consultancy managing director Jin Teik Oon tells IQ Magazine that South East Asia is losing out on major international stadium tours because promoters and governments "speak different languages" — governments want jobs and tourism GDP while promoters want routing efficiency and risk-adjusted returns. Outside K-Pop runs and a handful of Western stadium acts (Eras Tour, Coldplay, Ed Sheeran), the region has seen few major stadium tour stops since 2024, despite a market of roughly 660 million people with a young, digitally fluent audience. Oon points to gaps in concert-capable stadium infrastructure, production knowhow, and opaque licensing/customs/content-approval processes as the structural barriers, alongside several new arena and stadium projects (Bangkok, Cebu City, Shah Alam, Hanoi) now in the pipeline.

### Key Points
- Taylor Swift's 2024 Eras Tour Singapore stops sold 300,000+ tickets and generated an estimated $257M–$330M in tourism receipts, but also sparked regional friction over Singapore's exclusivity arrangement.
- Oon cites opaque licensing, noise controls, customs clearance, and content approvals as reasons tours "can swing away from a market overnight."
- Upcoming venue projects include Bangkok's UOB Live (AEG/The Mall Group JV), the Philippines' 25,000-cap Cebu City arena, Malaysia's 45,000-cap Shah Alam venue (completing this year), and an early-stage Hanoi project targeting the world's largest stadium.
- Oon describes a shift where live entertainment and "concert-cation" tourism are treated as serious economic strategy rather than "decorative soft power" by regional governments.
- He argues promoters choose markets based on "risk-readiness" — confidence, competence, and speed — rather than raw audience demand alone.

## ‘Different languages’: SE Asia stadium touring hampered by public-private ‘misalignment’

DisrupTEIK Consultancy managing director Jin Teik Oon tells IQ about how the region could unlock its potential for international stadium touring

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/smush-webp/2026/07/©-Singapore-Sports-Hub-scaled.jpg.webp)

Singapore's National Stadium © Tha Kallang / Singapore Sports Hub

South-East Asian cities are losing out on international stadium tours as promoters fail to frame concerts “in public value terms” for governments, according to a leading events executive in the region.

So says Jin Teik Oon, managing director of the disrupTEIK Consultancy and former CEO of The Kallang complex which houses Singapore’s National Stadium, who tells *IQ* that “different languages” spoken by governments and promoters have resulted in a regional booking landscape where “ tours can swing away from a market overnight”.

Despite what Oon calls an “insatiable hunger for live acts”, South East Asia has seen a paucity of visits from the world’s biggest international stadium tours in recent years, with the exception of K-Pop global runs, such as BTS’ *Arirang World Tour,* which will make stops in Bangkok, Kuala Lumpur, Singapore, and Jakarta this December.

Outside of these runs, the powerhouse *Eras Tour* from Taylor Swift, as well as tours from Coldplay and Ed Sheeran stands out as a top-tier Western stadium act to have made stops in the region. However, the last of these took place in 2024, leading Oon to question why this has on the whole not been consolidated in the region.

Oon cites an all-too-common “misalignment” between governments and tour organisers as a primary obstacle to this, saying: “Governments want jobs, tourism GDP, national pride, and urban regeneration. Promoters want routing efficiency, production certainty, and risk-adjusted returns. Too often, both sides want the same concert but speak entirely different languages.”

“When the dust settled on Taylor Swift’s *Eras Tour* in 2024, Singapore proved it wasn’t only participating in the live entertainment business, but resetting the terms of regional competition,” says Oon.

By securing those [exclusive](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2024/02/singapore-sought-exclusivity-over-swift-gigs/) six sold-out nights in Southeast Asia, he says the city-state “sent a message that should make every government, venue owner, and investor in South East Asia sit up”. Swift’s six Singapore shows alone sold more than 300,000 tickets and helped generate tourism receipts estimated at US$257 million to US$330 million.

But this successful tour stop itself highlighted a problem still facing the wider region of South East Asia, as Thai and [Philippine](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2024/02/philippine-rep-criticises-singapore-swift-deal/) politicians accused the Singaporean government of pushing for Swift [not to play any other regional stadium shows on the tour](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2024/02/singapore-sought-exclusivity-over-swift-gigs/).

> “ tours can swing away from a market overnight”

Such stadium events for neighbouring countries could prove to be milestone moments for both pop culture and economic development. Indeed, Oon tells *IQ* that pop culture and live entertainment have transitioned to be “no longer decorative soft power” but rather a “serious economic strategy” for countries’ development programmes.

But the benefits of touring the region are reciprocal, he adds, saying that promoters can no longer afford to view South East Asia as “an optional tour stop”.

“With roughly 660 million people, \[…\] it is one of the world’s most promising live entertainment markets, powered by a young (median age 30), digitally fluent audience, and rising demand for experience-led spending.”

However, Oon identifies further areas he believe the region must address to fully unlock this potential.

“Too many cities still lack a concert-capable stadium, concert-grade rigging knowledge, or operational fluency in large-scale production,” he says.

Notable upcoming venue projects in the region are largely focused on the arena level, such as Bangkok’s UOB Live, the product of a joint venture between AEG and Thai retail firm The Mall Group, as well as the Philippines’ upcoming 25,000-cap Cebu City arena, which, while providing an attractive second tour-stop outside of Manilla for the country, falls short of the [stadium-level capacity goals that legislators set their sights on in 2024](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2024/07/philippines-taylor-swift-ready-stadium/), which aimed to make the country “Taylor Swift-ready”.

However, a string of stadium projects have earmarked completion dates in the next few years, including Malaysia’s 45,000-cap Shah Alam venue, set for completion this year, and an early stage project in Hanoi which developers Vingroup are targeting to be the world’s biggest.

> “Too often, both sides want the same concert but speak entirely different languages”

And Oon says he has seen the disparity in stadium-level operational fluency “first hand”.

“One major stadium owner proposed piling and a concrete slab for a BTS 360-degree centre stage, an expensive, asset-damaging answer to a solvable production issue. With the right stage engineering, protection, and integrated flooring solution, that answer was unnecessary,” he recalls, “The problem wasn’t demand — it was knowhow”.

Another obstacle, he says, is that across several different national markets, “ licensing, noise controls, customs clearance, or content approvals remain opaque”, which he says means tours “can swing away from a market overnight”.

“ Recently, a senior Vietnamese government official asked me why Vietnam was absent from BTS’s 2026-2027 regional routing while Singapore, Bangkok, Kuala Lumpur, Jakarta, and Manila made the cut. My answer was simple: major tours are not won by desire alone. They are won by risk-readiness before the routing conversation begins,” Oon reveals to *IQ.*

“Promoters choose markets that give them confidence, competence, speed, and reduced risk,” Oon says. But he adds that this opportunity now extends “far beyond” stadium tours, citing “major festivals in Thailand and Indonesia to niche showcases in Bali” as evidence the region is “proving depth across genres”.

One avenue for growth is the sector’s renewed focus in recent months on music tourism, or the “concert-cation”, as Oon calls it. Broad city ecosystems are increasingly the basis on which fans make the decision to travel for live music, he says, meaning “hotel nights, restaurant spend, aviation demand, and global visibility” are almost as important to securing the sale of a concert ticket than the artist or venue themselves.

“ The real question is not whether Southeast Asia will become a major touring destination. It already is. When the next wave of routing decisions is made, the real question is which governments and venue owners will build the capability to win”, he says.
