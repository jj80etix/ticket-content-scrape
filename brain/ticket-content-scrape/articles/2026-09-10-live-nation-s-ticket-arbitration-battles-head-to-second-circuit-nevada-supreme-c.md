# Live Nation’s Ticket Arbitration Battles Head to Second Circuit, Nevada Supreme Court

## Summary

Live Nation and Ticketmaster's push to force consumer ticket disputes into private arbitration is now being tested on two new appellate fronts. In New York, secondary-market ticket buyers have asked the Second Circuit to overturn a ruling compelling their antitrust claims into arbitration, arguing the Federal Arbitration Act shouldn't reach claims from resale purchases. Separately, the Ninth Circuit referred a dispute involving Live Nation subsidiary Front Gate Tickets to the Nevada Supreme Court, over whether a company can enforce an arbitration clause while reserving the right to unilaterally rewrite the contract.

### Key Points
- Second Circuit petition (Leifer, Stevens, Jacobson v. Ticketmaster) challenges a June 29 order by Judge Arun Subramanian compelling arbitration.
- Petitioners argue the FAA doesn't apply to claims from tickets bought via third-party resellers like StubHub.
- Ninth Circuit referred a separate Front Gate Tickets (Live Nation subsidiary) dispute to the Nevada Supreme Court over unilateral contract-amendment clauses.
- Extends a multi-year fight following the appellate ruling that found Ticketmaster's prior New Era ADR arbitration system "unconscionable."

### Highlights
- Two live appellate fronts (2nd Circuit; Nevada Supreme Court) testing enforceability of Ticketmaster/Live Nation arbitration clauses.
- Central legal question: does the FAA cover claims from secondary-market ticket purchases, and can a company enforce arbitration while retaining unilateral contract-amendment rights.
- Builds on a prior ruling that Ticketmaster's New Era ADR system was "unconscionable."

### Industry Problem
Live Nation/Ticketmaster's terms-of-use arbitration provisions are under sustained, multi-jurisdiction legal challenge, creating ongoing uncertainty about how enforceable such clauses are — a structural governance question for the dominant primary/secondary ticketing ecosystem.

### Proposed Solution
No solution proposed in the article; for an independent platform, this is a monitor item — outcomes could reshape norms around consumer terms-of-use and dispute-resolution clauses industry-wide, informing how Etix structures its own terms.

Live Nation and Ticketmaster’s long-running fight to force consumer ticket disputes into private arbitration has moved onto two new appellate fronts, with separate cases now before the Second Circuit and Nevada Supreme Court testing different limits on how those agreements can be enforced.

In New York, two secondary-market ticket buyers have asked the Second Circuit to overturn a June ruling that sent their antitrust claims against Live Nation and Ticketmaster into arbitration. Their petition argues that the federal judge who issued the order skipped a threshold question: whether the Federal Arbitration Act even applies to claims stemming from tickets they bought from third-party resellers such as StubHub.

Across the country, the Ninth Circuit has sent a separate dispute involving Live Nation subsidiary Front Gate Tickets to the Nevada Supreme Court. That case asks whether a company can enforce an arbitration clause while reserving the right to rewrite the broader contract at any time, without advance notice.

The cases involve different plaintiffs, contracts and legal theories. But together they extend a years-long fight over the fine print Live Nation-controlled ticketing companies use to keep consumer disputes out of court — a fight that has already produced a major appellate ruling finding Ticketmaster’s previous New Era ADR-based arbitration system “unconscionable.”

## Second Circuit Asked to Undo Ticketmaster Arbitration Win

The newest challenge comes from Abraham Leifer and Tamara Stevens, two plaintiffs in a [consumer antitrust case TicketNews covered earlier this summer](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/07/live-nation-wins-new-era-arbitration-ruling-but-ticketmasters-fine-print-fights-continue/).

On June 29, U.S. District Judge Arun Subramanian ordered Leifer, Stevens and fellow plaintiff James Jacobson into arbitration after finding that they had repeatedly agreed to Ticketmaster’s Terms of Use. Those terms included both a broad arbitration requirement and a “delegation clause” giving an arbitrator authority to decide many disputes over whether the arbitration agreement itself can be enforced.

Leifer and Stevens — but not Jacobson — are now asking the Second Circuit for a writ of mandamus directing Subramanian to vacate that order.

Their argument focuses less on whether Ticketmaster properly displayed its terms or whether New Era ADR’s current rules are unfair. Instead, they contend that Subramanian did not first establish that the Federal Arbitration Act gave him authority to compel these particular claims into arbitration.

Leifer and Stevens bought the tickets underlying their claims through third-party resale marketplaces. They allege that Live Nation and Ticketmaster’s anticompetitive conduct inflated ticket prices throughout the market, causing them to overpay when they purchased tickets from sellers on StubHub.

Ticketmaster was not the seller in those transactions. The plaintiffs argue that their alleged antitrust injury had already occurred by the time Ticketmaster later became involved in delivering or transferring the tickets.

That detail is central to their appeal.

Section 2 of the Federal Arbitration Act covers agreements to arbitrate controversies “arising out of” the contract or transaction containing the arbitration agreement. Citing a 2025 Second Circuit decision involving Grubhub, the plaintiffs contend that means there must be a causal connection between the contract and the dispute.

“The FAA’s applicability under §2 is a question for the court, not an arbitrator,” their petition argues.

The plaintiffs say Ticketmaster agreements they accepted while creating an account, logging into the platform, buying other tickets or later retrieving transferred tickets did not cause their alleged injury from purchases made through independent resale marketplaces.

Subramanian reached a different conclusion in June. He found Ticketmaster sufficiently intertwined with the transactions because the tickets were originally issued through Ticketmaster, the plaintiffs had used the platform in connection with tickets, and Ticketmaster ultimately delivered transferred tickets to resale buyers.

He also found the wording of Ticketmaster’s agreement broad enough to cover the disputes.

Leifer and Stevens now argue that this answers the wrong question. Even if Ticketmaster wrote a contract broad enough to claim nearly any ticket-related dispute, they contend private contract language cannot expand the scope of the Federal Arbitration Act itself.

The procedural vehicle they are using is unusual. A writ of mandamus is an extraordinary remedy rather than a standard appeal, and the plaintiffs will face a high bar to convince the Second Circuit to grant it.

They argue mandamus is appropriate in part because federal arbitration law generally gives a party that loses a motion to compel arbitration an immediate route to appeal, while a consumer ordered into arbitration ordinarily must wait.

The petition also points to the April jury verdict against Live Nation and Ticketmaster in the separate antitrust case brought by the Justice Department and states. Leifer and Stevens contend their private claims arise from many of the same alleged anticompetitive practices, though the government case and consumer litigation remain separate proceedings with different claims and parties.

## Ticketmaster’s Long Fight Over New Era ADR

The Second Circuit challenge is the latest turn in an arbitration fight that has changed substantially since Ticketmaster switched its chosen arbitration provider from JAMS to New Era ADR in 2021.

The change initially produced one of Live Nation’s largest arbitration defeats.

In *Heckman v. Live Nation Entertainment*, consumers challenging Live Nation and Ticketmaster on antitrust grounds argued that Ticketmaster had moved them into a highly unusual New Era process that tilted the playing field toward the companies.

U.S. District Judge George Wu rejected Live Nation’s effort to compel arbitration in 2023, finding significant problems with the arrangement. [The Ninth Circuit affirmed in October 2024](https://www.ticketnews.com/2024/10/live-nation-arbitration-appeal-rejected/), finding both the delegation clause and arbitration agreement unconscionable under California law.

The appellate court was particularly critical of New Era’s old mass-arbitration procedures. Those rules sharply limited discovery and evidence, grouped similar claims through special mass-arbitration procedures and included provisions the court found favored Live Nation.

The Ninth Circuit described the rules as “internally inconsistent, poorly drafted, and riddled with typos.” Judge William Fletcher wrote that it would be “impossible for plaintiffs to present their claims on equal footing to Live Nation.”

TicketNews later covered Live Nation’s unsuccessful effort to take that ruling to the U.S. Supreme Court. [The justices declined review in October 2025](https://www.ticketnews.com/2025/10/supreme-court-rejects-live-nation-appeal-forcing-it-to-face-antitrust-lawsuit/), leaving the Ninth Circuit decision intact.

But the loss did not end the New Era story.

New Era rewrote its arbitration rules after the litigation, and Live Nation successfully relied on those changes before Subramanian this year. The revised rules added an express right to discovery, removed some restrictions on evidence and changed the process for replacing arbitrators.

Live Nation and Ticketmaster also agreed that two controversial provisions — a mass-arbitration protocol and an asymmetric appeal provision — would not apply to Leifer, Stevens and Jacobson. They further agreed that no arbitrator with an ownership stake in New Era would hear the cases.

Those changes helped produce a very different result from *Heckman*. Subramanian acknowledged that some procedural concerns remained, including the adhesive nature of Ticketmaster’s consumer terms, but found the revised system materially different from the one the Ninth Circuit struck down.

The new Second Circuit challenge therefore does not simply ask another court to declare New Era’s rules unconscionable. Leifer and Stevens are attacking an earlier step in the process: whether federal arbitration law reaches their resale-market claims in the first place.

## Nevada Supreme Court Gets a Different Live Nation Arbitration Question

The separate *Paxson v. Live Nation Entertainment* dispute does not center on New Era ADR, but it raises another fundamental question about the contractual foundation Live Nation subsidiaries rely upon when sending consumers into arbitration.

TicketNews covered the case in June after a [Ninth Circuit panel repeatedly pressed Live Nation’s attorneys during oral argument](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/06/ninth-circuit-again-presses-live-nation-over-ticket-buyer-arbitration-terms/).

Erin Paxson bought five tickets through Live Nation subsidiary Front Gate Ticketing Solutions for the 2022 Lovers & Friends festival in Las Vegas. She later sued following an alleged security incident at the festival, asserting claims including breach of contract and unjust enrichment.

A Nevada federal judge sent the dispute to arbitration in March 2025.

Paxson appealed, arguing that Front Gate’s Terms of Use were not supported by a binding promise because the company reserved the right to change the terms “at any time,” with changes taking effect immediately when posted.

The provision did not expressly say that Front Gate could not change its arbitration clause or apply later revisions to already-existing disputes.

Instead of predicting how Nevada courts would answer that question, the Ninth Circuit on July 31 formally certified it to the Nevada Supreme Court.

The appellate panel said the answer will likely determine the outcome of Paxson’s appeal. If Front Gate’s unrestricted modification power made its promise to arbitrate illusory, the Ninth Circuit said there was no arbitration agreement and it would reverse the lower court’s order compelling arbitration.

Live Nation offered several alternative reasons why the arbitration clause could still be enforced. The Ninth Circuit called those arguments “not persuasive,” further increasing the significance of the Nevada court’s answer.

The panel also rejected the idea that an arbitrator should decide whether an agreement was ever formed. Contract formation, the court said, remains an issue for judges even when an arbitration agreement contains language purporting to delegate such questions to the arbitrator.

The Nevada Supreme Court currently lists *Paxson v. Live Nation Entertainment* on its certified-question docket as Case No. 93076. The Ninth Circuit has stayed its own proceedings while the state court determines whether to accept and answer the question.

That leaves Live Nation’s ticket arbitration strategy facing two very different tests at once.

The Second Circuit is being asked whether a Ticketmaster contract can trigger federal arbitration law for an antitrust injury consumers say occurred through a competing resale marketplace. In Nevada, the issue is whether a Live Nation subsidiary retained so much power to rewrite its online terms that there was a binding arbitration agreement at all.

The Second Circuit can deny the mandamus petition without resolving the broader legal theory raised by Leifer and Stevens. And the Nevada Supreme Court must first decide whether to take up the Ninth Circuit’s certified question.

But both proceedings put another layer of judicial review on consumer arbitration practices that have already forced Live Nation and Ticketmaster to repeatedly revise — and defend — the fine print governing disputes with ticket buyers.
