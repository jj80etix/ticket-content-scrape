# UK Competition Watchdog Offers No New Timeline for Live Nation Investigation as Lawmakers Renew Pressure

## Summary

The UK's CMA gave Parliament no updated timeline for whether it will open a formal market investigation into Live Nation's vertically integrated promotion/venue/ticketing operations, repeating language from July even as a House of Commons committee renewed its demand for action before year-end 2026. Newly published (but months-old) CMA/government correspondence shows the regulator's board was to weigh the decision in "autumn" amid competing priorities, while Live Nation disputes the committee's market-share estimates (Parliament: 58-66% of 2025 UK primary tickets; Live Nation: 7.7M tickets sold, calling its position "highly competitive").

### Key Points
- CMA has given no updated timeline since July; board review was expected in "autumn" 2026 to inform its 2027-28 work program.
- Parliamentary committee (May report) alleges Live Nation controlled 58% of UK primary tickets in 2025 (66% incl. affiliates); Live Nation disputes the methodology, citing 7.7M tickets sold.
- CMA cites resource constraints (other enforcement cases, merger reviews) as competing for bandwidth against a live-music markets probe.
- UK government is separately advancing legislation to cap resale prices at face value and restrict marketplace fees — distinct from, and narrower than, the competition-structure questions Parliament is pressing on.
- CMA's prior (2025) Ticketmaster/Oasis probe secured pricing-transparency commitments but did not address vertical-integration market power.

### Highlights
- No new CMA timeline despite a renewed October 18 parliamentary push for action "before end of 2026."
- Live Nation's UK primary-ticket market share is disputed: 58-66% (Parliament) vs. a 7.7M-ticket, "highly competitive" framing (Live Nation).
- Separate UK resale-cap legislation is advancing in parallel but doesn't resolve the competition-structure question.

### Industry Problem
Sustained regulatory uncertainty over Live Nation's vertically integrated market position leaves the competitive landscape for independent promoters/ticketing providers unresolved, while a possible future market investigation could force disclosure of commercial arrangements that currently aren't public.

### Proposed Solution
No build/improve action is indicated; this is a monitor/evolve signal — the trigger to watch is whether the CMA's board formally opens a markets study or investigation (which could produce binding remedies and reshape the UK competitive landscape for independents), versus the resale-cap bill passing on its own narrower track.

## CMA Acknowledges Resource Constraints, Anticipates Board Decision

The latest parliamentary announcement accompanied the publication of previously unreleased responses from the CMA and UK government to the committee’s May investigation recommendations.

Although [the responses were published October 18](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm5902/cmselect/cmbeis/625/report.html?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), they were submitted months earlier, with the government’s letter dated July 20 and the CMA’s response received July 24.

The CMA’s submission provides a more detailed account of its decision-making process, including an acknowledgment that resources were largely committed through the end of 2026.

The regulator identified several competing priorities, including ongoing competition and consumer enforcement cases, its digital markets responsibilities, merger reviews and market studies involving dentistry and childcare.

Those commitments were relevant to its consideration of whether to undertake additional work in live entertainment.

The CMA also explained that its board was expected to begin annual planning and prioritization in September to inform its 2027-28 work program. Potential action concerning ticketing, including the committee’s findings, would be considered during that process.

More specifically, the July response stated that the CMA was planning for its board to consider whether to undertake formal markets work in the live music sector during the autumn.

The regulator committed to writing to the parliamentary committee with an update following that consideration.

With October now well underway, the CMA has not publicly confirmed whether the anticipated board review has taken place or provided a date for announcing its decision.

That does not establish that the process has been delayed. However, it leaves unanswered whether the regulator is still evaluating the available evidence, has completed its preliminary review or has reached a decision that has not yet been publicly communicated.

The July submission also confirmed that the CMA had conducted additional research and held initial discussions with UK and international stakeholders.

Its examination included the potential impact of vertical integration across venue ownership, concert promotion and ticketing, along with market power and the use of exclusivity clauses.

The regulator identified several possible approaches, ranging from a preliminary market study to a more comprehensive market investigation or enforcement action under the Competition Act 1998.

A full market investigation would provide broader powers to examine commercial arrangements and potentially impose legally binding remedies. It would also require greater resources and a more extensive regulatory process.

The CMA has not committed to using that mechanism, despite the parliamentary committee’s insistence that a full investigation is necessary.

## Parliament Renews Demand for Action Before Year’s End

The parliamentary committee’s latest announcement reiterates the concerns detailed in its [May report](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/05/live-nation-drives-climate-of-fear-in-need-of-investigation-uk-parliamentary-committee-finds/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), which examined competition across the live music industry’s interconnected businesses.

Lawmakers identified Live Nation’s operations across concert promotion, ticketing, venues and festivals as a central concern.

The committee cited evidence alleging that Live Nation directly controlled 58% of the 23.1 million primary tickets offered in the UK during 2025, increasing to 66% when affiliated companies were included.

Those figures were based on evidence presented to Parliament and have been disputed by Live Nation.

The committee also raised concerns about contractual exclusivity, access to venues, relationships between promoters and ticketing providers, and the control of consumer data.

Its investigation received 45 written submissions, including a significant number submitted anonymously or confidentially because industry participants feared potential retaliation.

Committee Chair Liam Byrne said the findings demanded regulatory intervention.

“Britain’s live music industry should reward talent, not simply market power,” Byrne said in the committee’s October 18 announcement.

The committee said it was writing to the CMA seeking further information about its next steps and a timetable for action.

Lawmakers have continued to insist that the regulator initiate a full market investigation before the end of 2026.

## Live Nation Disputes Parliamentary Findings

Live Nation has rejected the committee’s characterization of competition in the UK live music business, arguing that its conclusions rely on inaccurate market-share estimates and misunderstandings of how the industry operates.

In a [June 16 letter to Parliament](https://committees.parliament.uk/publications/53774/standard/pdf/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), the company challenged the methodology behind several figures cited in the committee’s report.

Live Nation said its UK operations sold 7.7 million tickets for arena, stadium and outdoor concerts in 2025, a figure it argued demonstrated a substantially smaller market position than lawmakers suggested.

However, the company’s figure and the committee’s estimates are not necessarily directly comparable. Live Nation’s calculation concerns ticket sales associated with specified event categories, while the committee’s figures address broader control of primary ticket sales, including affiliated operations.

The company also said the UK ticketing market is highly competitive, with Ticketmaster facing competition from AXS, CTS Eventim, See Tickets, DICE and other providers.

Live Nation stated that Ticketmaster does not maintain exclusive venue ticketing agreements in the UK and that most Live Nation-promoted events use multiple ticketing agents.

It further argued that vertical integration allows companies to manage the substantial financial risks associated with concert promotion while supporting investment in artists and events.

The competing accounts underscore why a formal competition investigation could be consequential.

Such a review could provide regulators with access to detailed internal ticketing data, commercial agreements and other records needed to evaluate the industry’s competitive structure more comprehensively than the publicly available information permits.

The parliamentary committee’s findings are not legal determinations that Live Nation violated competition law, and the CMA has not announced any such findings against the company arising from the proposed market examination.

## Government Leaves Competition Questions to CMA

The UK government has also declined to intervene directly in the regulator’s decision-making process.

In her publicly released July 20 response to the parliamentary committee, Kate Dearden, minister for employment rights and consumer protection, acknowledged the concerns raised about the live music market but emphasized that decisions about competition intervention belong to the CMA.

The government argued that the regulator already possesses the statutory tools necessary to address potential competition problems and should independently determine whether intervention is justified.

That position leaves the decision about launching a comprehensive investigation with the same regulator that has acknowledged competing priorities and substantial resource commitments.

Meanwhile, the government is advancing a separate effort to restrict ticket resale prices and impose additional requirements on secondary ticket marketplaces.

As [TicketNews previously reported](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/09/uk-ticket-resale-cap-bill-offshore-enforcement/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), the government has been developing legislation that would generally prohibit resale above the original ticket purchase cost, while imposing additional restrictions on marketplace fees and ticket-selling practices.

Those proposals are intended to address consumer protection concerns in the secondary market. They would not, on their own, resolve questions about competitive conditions in primary ticketing, venue access or the relationships among major concert promoters and their affiliated businesses.

The distinction is significant because the parliamentary committee’s concerns extend well beyond resale pricing.

Lawmakers are asking whether the structure of the live music business itself limits competition, including through contractual arrangements and control of ticketing infrastructure.

The CMA previously investigated Ticketmaster’s handling of Oasis reunion tour ticket sales, securing commitments in 2025 to improve pricing transparency and information provided to consumers.

That consumer protection investigation did not constitute a comprehensive examination of Live Nation’s market power or its vertically integrated operations.

The regulator’s latest statement leaves unanswered when, or whether, that broader examination will begin.

The next milestone identified in the CMA’s published response is an update to Parliament following its board’s consideration of potential markets work. Whether that consideration has already occurred remains unconfirmed.
