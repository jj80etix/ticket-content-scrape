# ‘Independence cuts both ways’: Untitled Group’s secrets of success

## Summary

Untitled Group co-founder Nicholas Greco details the independent Australian festival operator's ticketing and programming strategy ahead of its flagship Beyond The Valley festival, which sold out its entire allocation within presale for a second straight year. Greco describes a deliberate shift toward tiered ticket types (70+ options), face-value resale via Tixel, and multi-event diversification (Wildlands, Drift, Pitch) to spread financial risk — while flagging rising public-liability insurance, FX exposure (buying talent in USD, selling in AUD), and weather as the biggest cost pressures squeezing independent promoters.

### Key Points
- BTV sold out in presale for the second consecutive year by shifting its entire ticket allocation into presale, incentivizing early registration
- Offers 70+ ticket types (single-day, multi-day, glamping, club access) to let attendees self-select price/experience rather than one flat price
- Partners with resale platform Tixel for face-value resale instead of leaving fans to scalpers
- Names public liability insurance premiums as "one of the biggest line items on any event budget," alongside rising site build, fuel, labor and international (USD) artist fees
- Diversifies across four events (BTV, Wildlands, Drift, Pitch) so no single ticketing or touring arm has to subsidize a weak year
- Australian festival sector hit record A$400m revenue and 2M+ attendance in 2025, but margins remain tight

### Highlights
- Public liability insurance cited as a top-line cost pressure independent of ticketing technology
- Currency mismatch (buying talent in USD, selling tickets in AUD) compresses margins as international fees rise
- Tiered/presale-weighted ticketing model (70+ ticket types, full presale allocation) is framed as the key lever against those cost pressures

### Industry Problem
Independent promoters/venues face margin compression from external cost inflation (insurance, FX, labor) they cannot pass fully onto consumers, making ticketing/programming strategy (tiering, presale allocation, diversification) one of the few levers left to protect profitability.

### Proposed Solution
Untitled Group's response is operational: maximize presale conversion, offer granular ticket tiers tied to willingness-to-pay, and partner with face-value resale rather than leaving money (and goodwill) to scalpers. For Etix, this reinforces demand for flexible tiered-pricing and presale-allocation tooling, plus face-value resale integrations, as client-facing (Q1) product differentiators for independent promoters under margin pressure.

## Transcript

Co-founder Nicholas Greco unpacks the portfolio's ticketing and programming innovations ahead of flagship festival Beyond The Valley

![Nick Greco](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Nick-Greco.png)

Nick Greco

As Australia’s festival season gets underway, Untitled Group co-founder Nicholas Greco fills *IQ* in on what’s driving the independent promoter’s festival growth and why “independence cuts both ways” as they innovate their ticketing and programming strategies.

The country’s biggest independent music operation is celebrating its [10th birthday](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/02/fiercely-independent-untitled-group-celebrates-first-decade/) this year, looking ahead to its flagship event Beyond The Valley (BTV) into its second decade while also building up its national festival business with complementary one-day events, Wildlands and the new Drift Festival.

Many of the market’s largest festivals are positioned around the New Year’s countdown, which lands in the middle of summer and in the break between Christmas and the New Year. This period will see the return of the five-day BTV in Victoria for its 11th edition, complemented by one-day Wildlands events in Brisbane (31 December) and Western Australia (2 January), with acts like John Summit, Skepta, KI/KI, Disco Lines, Overmono, and Nia Archives slated across both.

The timing means competition is steep, both for organisers’ choice of acts and audiences’ budgets, raising the stakes for teams in a notoriously tricky landscape. Greco says the “fierce” competition for programming starts 18 months out from events, with the country’s promoters chasing the same names as “there are only a handful of artists in any given year who can genuinely headline a major Australian festival”.

“It’s a fight over market share long before it’s a fight over tickets,” he tells *IQ*. “By the time two festivals are on sale against each other, the real contest finished months ago.”

When ticket sales do open, audiences are weighing up personal budgets with trips or other events, making the ticketing strategy one that can make or break an event. This year, BTV captured its second consecutive sell-out, with the Untitled team choosing to shift the entire allocation within presale.

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Beyond-The-Valley-©-Ashlea-Caygill.png)

Beyond The Valley © Ashlea Caygill

“It rewards the people who put their hand up, and that matters to us,” Greco says. “Every ticket goes into the presale, and we explain there’s no guaranteed general sale once the allocation’s gone, so the only thing you have to do to get first access is register. We’re really upfront with people about that.”

He says that rewarding that segment is “how you build an audience that turns up every year rather than one you have to buy back every December”. For those who can’t attend, face-value resale is offered through Tixel, he says, “because if you were late, we’d much rather you got a ticket off another fan than off a scalper”.

More than 70 ticket types are available for BTV, from single-day to full-fledged passes or glamping and club access. Access to the on-site leisure area, The Valley Club, which has pools and bars, has “completely changed the shape of the event” as fans arrive with a full-day plan rather than “waiting for the music to start”, Greco says.

“Choose your own adventure, as we call it internally. Someone at their first festival at 18 and someone in their late 20s who wants a bed and a pool can buy the same weekend at the price that suits them, instead of us picking one number and hoping. Selling out in the presale in August suggests we got it right,” he says, adding that the fear that prices will outpace budgets remains.

Packages also lean into travel and accommodation, and the team is actively championing “offsite culture” to spread the benefit to surrounding cities while making the event that much more of a destination. Transit passes are an additional add-on to make the interstate journey, with international attendance the “fastest-increasing” audience segment at BTV.

Ticketing is only one piece of the puzzle for BTV’s longevity. Greco boils it down to two additional considerations: an audience-steered reinvention and splitting the operational burden across multiple events.

“We’ve paid a lot of attention to understanding what that audience actually wants rather than assuming it stays still, and when it shifts, we move with it. A lot of festivals that had been around a long time didn’t move quickly enough in re-establishing what they were, and a lot of them aren’t around now,” he says.

> “On its own, no single Australian festival competes with what an artist earns on a run elsewhere”

A notable example of this is BTV’s turn to electronic in its programming, which Greco says, “we followed the audience rather than deciding it, and the audience shifted very quickly” and that the brand has “completely inverted” since indie dominance in the early years.

“Dom Dolla, Rüfüs Du Sol, Fisher, Peggy Gou and Kaytranada have all come through a few times, and Kaytranada, Dom and Rüfüs played the very first BTV in 2014, which I love,” he says.

The team is also leaning into a “pop nostalgia moment” on the Valley Stage, which will host the Black Eyed Peas and The Veronicas this year. Having curated stages with “their own identities” has opened the door for acts with “no Australia profile” to gain exposure “in front of the right 3,000 people rather than the wrong 20,000”, he says.

Despite the shifting programming strategy, he says the makeup of BTV’s bill is predominantly Australian, “and that hasn’t changed as the genre mix has”.

Australia, nevertheless, is a multi-varied market. Wildlands acts as an extension of the BTV brand while localising it to Brisbane and Perth instead of “exporting BTV north and west and hoping”. He says the stage designs and site layouts are reworked, in addition to programming taking a more centralised through-line for the one-day offshoots.

Having the extra events helps Untitled sweeten the offer deals, a distinctive draw in attracting global talent to the market and a mission that extends beyond just Untitled’s own portfolio.

“BTV, Wildlands, and \[Finely Tuned-organised\] Lost Paradise have to work together to get talent into the country at all, because on its own, no single Australian festival competes with what an artist earns on a run elsewhere, and it’s hard enough getting them to make the twenty-hour trip at the best of times,” Greco says.

> “We can’t subsidise a soft year from a ticketing arm or a global touring division”

The obstacle, and opportunity, is offering audiences something that stands out in a saturated window.

“The challenge is discipline, because the easy move is to do the same thing everywhere, and that’s the fastest way to make all four less distinctive,” he says.

Sharpening the appeal is especially important as margins get tighter. In 2025, the Australian festival sector achieved its [highest revenue and attendance levels](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/10/australian-festivals-hit-record-400m-revenue-in-2025/?utm_campaign=232706262-IQ%20Index&utm_medium=email&_hsenc=p2ANqtz-8gcg0Qk87CU1BYnO679xbuiK73VWKwinU62HUUV4Q_CbQ0ocFDEHfU-w-bRP9q-djRwE8-7WOYDv0P6V5vjNh8rGtGxg&_hsmi=2&utm_content=2&utm_source=hs_email), hitting nearly A$400 million (€250.3m) and drawing over 2 million people, attributed in part to a rise in average price and return of major events, according to Live Performance Australia.

But rising pressures around costs and inflation still remain, and have turned even the most established Australian events upside down.

“Public liability premiums are now one of the biggest line items on any event budget and that market genuinely needs intervention to be sustainable,” Greco says. “Site build, fuel, and labour have all moved against us at the same time as international fees have risen, and we’re buying in US dollars and selling in Australian ones.”

Weather remains the other major challenge, especially for BTV as a five-day camping festival, when “a bad forecast can cost you on the day with nothing having gone wrong operationally”.

“Independence cuts both ways, honestly. We can’t subsidise a soft year from a ticketing arm or a global touring division, so everything we run has to work on its own numbers,” Greco says.

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Beyond-The-Valley-©-Jordan-Munns.png)

Beyond The Valley © Jordan Munns

While the pressure is tangible, he says it buys the team speed and versatility.

“We backed an unproven bill at Pitch for a decade until it became the thing that sells out instantly. That decision doesn’t survive a quarterly review cycle, and it’s the main reason we’re still here when better capitalised festivals aren’t,” he says.

In line with its NYE-centred events, Pitch Music & Arts Festival also sold out its 10th anniversary edition, set for 5-9 March 2027 in Victoria’s Grampian Plains. Electronic frontrunners like HAAi, Denis Sulta, X CLUB, Patrick Mason, Chlär, Malugi, Peach, and DJ Boring are all lined up for the milestone.

Next year will also see the launch of Drift Festival, one-day events in Perth (28 February) and Brisbane (6 March) coinciding with the core Pitch event and sharing names like Chris Stassy, Cloudy, Chlär and more.

Untitled’s portfolio is accented by the world’s first fully inclusive music festival, Ability Festival, which also informs the way the team approaches accessibility at the other events.

As it looks ahead to its second decade in operation, the team is thinking long-term about the value of its platform for the market.

“What I’d really love is for a few of the developing artists on the side stages this year to grow and come back in ten years and do the \[New Year’s\] countdown. That’s the bit that gets me really excited,” he says.
