### Part 3: Employment and Talent Management Considerations

In [Part 1](https://www.troutman.com/insights/navigating-the-opportunities-and-obstacles-facing-quantum-sensing-companies-a-three-part-series/) of this series, we described the quantum sensing landscape, funding pathways available to companies in the sector, and broad commercial and defense applications driving demand. In [Part 2](https://www.troutman.com/insights/navigating-the-opportunities-and-obstacles-facing-quantum-sensing-companies-part-2/), we addressed intellectual property (IP); government contracting; cybersecurity; export control; foreign ownership, control, or influence (FOCI); Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States (CFIUS); and national security considerations that can shape how quantum sensing companies protect technology, pursue federal funding, and commercialize dual-use products. In this final Part 3, we turn to the people side of that strategy: how companies can recruit and structure teams, manage export control and security clearance constraints, separate commercial and defense workstreams, and align immigration planning with the realities of building a regulated, dual-use technology business.

Quantum sensing companies operate in a talent market that is inherently global — many of the leading researchers and engineers in quantum physics, photonics, cryogenics, and control systems are foreign nationals or have complex immigration histories. At the same time, the federal funding and mission sets most relevant to quantum sensing (*e.g.*, resilient positioning, navigation, and timing (PNT), advanced sensing, and surveillance applications) are subject to stringent national security and export control regimes. That tension creates a distinctive employment and workforce-planning challenge for companies in this space.

From the outset, quantum sensing companies should assume that hiring and staffing decisions may be constrained by:

- **Export control rules and “deemed exports.”** Under U.S. export control laws, including the Export Administration Regulations (EAR) and the International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR), providing certain controlled technical data or software source code to a foreign national in the United States can be treated as an “export” to that individual’s home country.[\[1\]](#_ftn1) This can trigger licensing requirements, depending on the technology and nationality. Sometimes even more challenging, dual nationals or third-country nationals outside the U.S. may be subject to “deemed reexport” restrictions based on nationality. This can create conflicts of law issues, and similar complexities. For companies building sensors, control electronics, or algorithms that fall under the ITAR or EAR classifications, this can significantly affect staffing timelines and restrictions (tied to licensing or compliance reviews), and can complicate international travel and remote work from overseas locations.
- **Security clearances and U.S.-person limitations.** Many defense and intelligence programs relevant to quantum sensing — particularly those involving classified information or sensitive operational concepts — require that key personnel hold personnel security clearances (PCLs) or, at minimum, qualify as “U.S. persons” under applicable regulations. Foreign nationals generally cannot obtain a PCL, and in some cases may be excluded from participating in specific projects or roles altogether. If a company seeks or holds a facility security clearance (FCL), FOCI and foreign staffing in certain positions can necessitate additional layers of mitigation, corporate structuring, and oversight.
- **SBIR/STTR and federal contract conditions.** While Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) and Small Business Technology Transfer (STTR) solicitations do not uniformly prohibit foreign nationals, some topics or agencies may impose restrictions on who may perform the work, where it may be performed, or how systems and data must be secured. Work that involves Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI), export-controlled technical data, or sensitive test results may require limiting access to U.S. persons, implementing robust access controls, and documenting personnel screening and training.

These constraints have practical implications for how quantum sensing companies structure their teams and growth plans:

- **Recruiting and role design.** Job descriptions and recruiting processes may need to distinguish between roles that can be performed by any qualified candidate (*e.g.*, certain commercial applications or high-level modeling) and roles that must be limited to U.S. persons or clearance-eligible personnel. Companies should not apply hiring restrictions based on national origin or clearance eligibility company-wide; instead, they should apply such restrictions only where truly necessary to comply with U.S. law or security regulations, to avoid potential employment discrimination claims. Companies should also integrate export control and security considerations into hiring workflows, including export control questionnaires, clear disclosures in job postings, and early assessment of visa eligibility, PCL considerations, and export control authorization feasibility. For many companies, this naturally leads to distinct commercial and defense “tracks,” with commercial roles designed to capture the contributions of foreign national experts who cannot support controlled defense efforts. But, for other companies, maintaining commercial uses for core technologies with defense applications can, in some cases, reduce defense product export control burdens.
- **Separate workstreams.** To leverage global talent while managing national security obligations, some companies create “clean” technical environments or teams dedicated to export-controlled or defense work — accessible only to authorized persons — while maintaining separate teams or codebases for commercial or noncontrolled projects. This can involve carefully designed IT systems (*e.g.*, segregated repositories and environments), access controls, visitor and facility policies, and configuration management to prevent inadvertent commingling of controlled and noncontrolled work. When done properly, this structure enables a company to advance commercial product lines with a multinational workforce while preserving eligibility for sensitive defense contracts.
- **Immigration strategy and lead times.** Sponsoring visas for key hires (*e.g.*, O‑1, H‑1B, or other categories) can be more complicated when the underlying work is export‑controlled or clearance-sensitive. Companies should anticipate longer lead times, coordination with immigration and export control counsel, and potential limitations on the roles foreign nationals can occupy. In some cases, it may be necessary to design career paths that initially focus foreign national employees on commercial, pre‑export‑controlled workstreams, with clear boundaries and documentation. This again underscores the value of a robust commercial roadmap alongside defense work, so that immigration-constrained hires have clear, compliant roles that still align with the company’s core technology.

To navigate these challenges, quantum sensing companies can take several practical steps:

- Build an employment and access‑control framework that aligns HR, IT, and legal functions, including policies that address export control screening, role‑based access privileges, and documentation of who can access which systems and data.
- Incorporate export‑control and security considerations into offer letters, NDAs, and employee handbooks, making clear that certain roles and projects may be restricted and that initial or continued employment in particular positions may depend on eligibility under applicable laws.
- Establish training programs so leadership, HR, and technical managers understand the basics of export controls, deemed exports, and security obligations, and can spot issues early rather than reacting after a violation or investigation.

Handled proactively, these employment and talent issues need not prevent quantum sensing companies from attracting world-class global talent. Indeed, a thoughtful dual-use strategy can turn regulatory constraints into a structured talent model that maximizes contributions from all parties. But doing so requires deliberate planning, early legal input, and an operating model that treats workforce design as a central element of the company’s national security, commercial, and government-engagement strategy.

#### Conclusion: Aligning Innovation With Federal Opportunities

Quantum sensing stands at the convergence of breakthrough science, urgent national security imperatives, and rapidly expanding commercial markets. As these technologies mature, founders will increasingly find themselves operating in dual-use environments where federal funding, regulatory regimes, and private capital converge.

Within this landscape, SBIR/STTR programs are especially significant. They offer quantum sensing companies a way to finance high-risk R&D, validate technology with sophisticated government customers, and build an IP and performance track record without immediate heavy equity dilution. Used thoughtfully, SBIR/STTR awards can complement venture financing, de-risk technical milestones, and help demonstrate durable demand in defense, aerospace, and critical infrastructure markets. But federal dollars are not frictionless, and any company seeking federal funding must ensure that it has:

1. **An intentional IP strategy**, combining patents, trade secrets, and data-rights management to protect core innovations while avoiding overbroad government licenses.
2. **Early, right-sized compliance infrastructure**, including protocols for contract administration, subcontract management, employment and recruiting, cybersecurity, export controls, and FOCI awareness, so that awards, financings, and eventual exits are not derailed by preventable compliance issues.
3. **A proactive national security planning and compliance program**, from screening foreign investment and supply chains to structuring collaborations with universities and national labs, and designing commercialization plans that reconcile dual-use ambitions with security obligations.
