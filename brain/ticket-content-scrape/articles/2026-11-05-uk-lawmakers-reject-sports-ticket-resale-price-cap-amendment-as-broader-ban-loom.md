# UK Lawmakers Reject Sports Ticket Resale Price Cap Amendment as Broader Ban Looms

## Summary

British MPs voted 286-62 on Nov. 4 against a Sporting Events Bill amendment that would have banned above-face-value resale for major sporting events, with the government instead pointing to a broader, still-undated Ticket Tout Ban Bill to cover concerts, sports, and other live events. Ministers said rejecting the narrower amendment wasn't a retreat from resale-price reform, just a preference for comprehensive legislation. The debate also surfaced renewed competition concerns, with critics arguing that focusing solely on resale ignores Live Nation/Ticketmaster's estimated — and disputed — 66% share of UK primary tickets.

### Key Points
- Nov. 4 vote: 286-62 against the Sabine amendment capping sports-ticket resale at face value plus unavoidable fees.
- Defeated amendment would have let the UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) fine violators up to 10% of global turnover.
- Government confirms the planned Ticket Tout Ban Bill (covering concerts, sports, other live events) remains its primary vehicle; no introduction date set.
- Sporting Events Bill itself proceeds, targeting UEFA Euro 2028 as its first application.
- Nov. 4 CapX commentary (Consumer Choice Center) argues regulatory focus on resale ignores Live Nation/Ticketmaster's disputed 66% share of 2025 UK primary tickets.
- CMA has not set a timetable for deciding whether to launch a formal competition investigation into live music.

### Highlights
- Nov. 4, 2026: UK Commons rejects sports-resale price-cap amendment 286-62; the broader Ticket Tout Ban Bill remains the government's main vehicle, still undated.
- Sporting Events Bill applies first to UEFA Euro 2028.
- Live Nation disputes the cited 66% UK primary-ticket market-share estimate.

### Industry Problem

UK resale regulation is moving toward a blanket face-value cap on secondary markets, while critics — and some lawmakers — warn that leaves primary-market concentration (Live Nation/Ticketmaster's dominant UK share) unaddressed, the core tension of fee/price regulation falling on one side of the market while structural competition questions on the other side stay open.

### Proposed Solution

The bill's own fix is a blanket resale price cap plus CMA enforcement powers (up to 10% of global turnover). For Etix, the #monitor trigger is the undated Ticket Tout Ban Bill's draft text — once published, assess whether its resale-platform obligations (price caps, transfer rules, anti-circumvention provisions) would apply to, or advantage, independent primary platforms operating in the UK market.

British lawmakers rejected a proposal Wednesday to prohibit above-face-value ticket resale for major sporting events, voting 286-62 against an amendment to the Sporting Events Bill even as the Labour government prepares broader legislation targeting ticket resale across the live entertainment industry.

The November 4 vote leaves the government’s planned Ticket Tout Ban Bill as the primary vehicle for sweeping changes to Britain’s secondary ticket market. Ministers insisted that rejecting the amendment did not signal any retreat from their commitment to restricting ticket resale prices, but reflected their preference for comprehensive legislation covering concerts, sports and other live events.

The defeated amendment, introduced by Liberal Democrat MP Anna Sabine, would have made it illegal to resell tickets for major sporting events above their original purchase cost, including unavoidable fees. It also proposed restrictions on resale platform charges, limits on the number of tickets individuals could resell, and monitoring requirements for ticket marketplaces.

Under the proposal, the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) would have been empowered to impose penalties of up to 10% of a company’s annual global turnover for violations.

Parliamentary Under-Secretary Stephanie Peacock similarly argued that the Sporting Events Bill was not the appropriate legislation for the broader resale restrictions.

The Sporting Events Bill establishes a framework for hosting major international and certain recurring sporting events, including measures addressing unauthorized ticket sales, advertising, commercial activities and event operations. The government expects the framework to apply first to UEFA Euro 2028.

The proposed Ticket Tout Ban Bill would go substantially further, generally prohibiting the resale of live-event tickets above their original purchase cost and imposing additional obligations on resale platforms.

As [TicketNews reported in September](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/09/uk-ticket-resale-cap-bill-offshore-enforcement/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), government officials have been developing the legislation while considering how to prevent ticket sellers and marketplaces from circumventing restrictions by operating outside the UK.

Ministers have committed to publishing draft legislation during the current parliamentary session for parliamentary scrutiny, but have not announced a specific introduction date.

## Competition Concerns Add Another Dimension to Resale Debate

The parliamentary vote coincides with renewed criticism of Britain’s proposed approach to ticket regulation, including arguments that concentrating regulatory attention on secondary marketplaces could leave more fundamental competition problems in primary ticketing unresolved.

In a [November 4 commentary published by CapX](https://capx.co/why-your-gig-tickets-cost-an-arm-and-a-leg?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), David Clement, policy director of the Consumer Choice Center, argued that the government should prioritize opening the primary ticketing market to greater competition instead of restricting resale prices.

Clement pointed to the market position of Live Nation Entertainment and its Ticketmaster subsidiary, citing a parliamentary committee report that estimated Live Nation and affiliated companies controlled 66% of UK primary tickets offered in 2025.

That estimate has been disputed by Live Nation, which argues that the committee relied on inaccurate market-share calculations and that Ticketmaster competes with several established ticketing providers.

Clement nevertheless argued that the government’s approach risks restricting competition in the resale market without addressing the market power of major primary ticketing operators.

He advocated nonexclusive venue ticketing arrangements, stronger protections against retaliation involving venue access and concert promotion, and greater freedom for fans to transfer or resell their tickets through competing platforms.

Those proposals overlap with concerns previously raised by Britain’s Business and Trade Committee, which recommended a comprehensive investigation into competition across the live music industry.

As [TicketNews reported in October](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/10/uk-cma-live-nation-investigation-timeline/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), the CMA had not provided a timetable for deciding whether to launch a formal investigation into Live Nation’s position across ticketing, promotion, venues and related businesses.

Live Nation has maintained that the UK ticketing market is competitive and has rejected allegations that its operations improperly restrict rival businesses.

Concerns about the potential consequences of limiting resale competition also surfaced during Wednesday’s parliamentary debate.

Conservative MP Nigel Huddleston, a former sports minister, warned that lawmakers should preserve a legitimate secondary market that provides consumer benefits.

Huddleston cited FIFA’s official resale system, where he said fees of 15% charged to both buyers and sellers demonstrated how costs can accumulate when consumers have limited marketplace alternatives.

His comments illustrated a distinction increasingly visible in the British ticketing debate: regulating abusive resale practices and preserving competition among ticketing providers are not necessarily competing objectives, but the design of new restrictions could affect both.

## U2 Ticket Pricing Complaints Highlight Primary-Market Costs

The debate is unfolding against continued public frustration over the original prices charged for major concerts, not simply markups on the resale market.

Fans seeking tickets for U2’s 2027 performances at London’s Tottenham Hotspur Stadium complained this week about prices reaching £437 for general admission standing tickets during presales, according to [reporting published Wednesday by the Daily Mail](https://www.dailymail.com/tvshowbiz/article-16265525/U2-Ticketmaster-greedy-fans-extortionate-437-price-tickets-London.html?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content).

Some fans questioned the difference between advertised entry-level prices and more expensive tickets they encountered during the purchasing process. The reports concern tickets offered during the original onsale process rather than resale listings, and do not establish that dynamic pricing was responsible for the differences.

The complaints follow [recent comments from U2 frontman Bono](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/10/bono-u2-ticket-pricing-2027-tour/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), who defended offering premium-priced tickets to wealthier fans while arguing that the band has historically sought to make some desirable concert locations accessible at lower prices.

The controversy illustrates a limitation of Britain’s proposed resale price restrictions: a ticket priced at £437 during its initial sale would remain a £437 ticket under legislation prohibiting resale above its original cost.

Such a law could restrict subsequent markups, but would not directly regulate an artist’s initial pricing strategy, premium inventory allocations or the fees attached to primary ticket purchases.

Supporters of resale restrictions argue that eliminating opportunities for large resale profits would help ordinary fans access tickets and reduce incentives for professional touts to acquire inventory.

Critics contend that strict resale caps could limit consumers’ ability to sell tickets through independent marketplaces, reinforce issuer-controlled resale channels or shift transactions toward less regulated platforms.

Those competing concerns will become increasingly relevant as the government releases the text of its proposed legislation.

The November 4 parliamentary vote settles the immediate question of whether a sporting-event-specific price cap will be added to the existing bill. It leaves unresolved the larger debate over how Britain intends to regulate ticket resale while addressing questions about competition, primary-market pricing and consumers’ ability to choose where they buy and sell tickets.
