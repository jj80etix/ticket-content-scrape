# FTC and Connecticut AG Reach $4M Settlement With Manchester City Nissan Dealership Involving Deceptive Fees

## Summary

The FTC and Connecticut AG reached a $4 million settlement with Manchester City Nissan over deceptive junk fees — unauthorized "certification" and add-on charges baked into financing without documented consent. The law-firm alert frames it as further proof that junk-fee enforcement is a durable, bipartisan federal and state priority, explicitly citing April 2026's $10 million FTC settlement with StubHub over undisclosed ticket fees as the ticketing-specific precedent in the same enforcement pattern.

### Key Points
- Settlement requires $4M in consumer redress, the all-in max price (minus required government charges) as the most prominent figure shown, express informed consent for every charge, plus six-year compliance reporting and five-year recordkeeping obligations.
- FTC's Unfair or Deceptive Fees rule took effect May 2025 under the Trump administration, which separately signed a March 2025 executive order directing the FTC to enhance live-entertainment ticket price transparency.
- Article directly cites the April 2026 $10M FTC settlement with StubHub over undisclosed mandatory ticket fees as the comparable enforcement precedent.
- Enforcement now flows from overlapping sources: FTC rules, a growing state statutory patchwork, city rules, and state AG UDAP authority.

### Industry Problem
Junk-fee/drip-pricing enforcement has continued seamlessly across the administration change and is intensifying at both the federal and state level, and the compliance bar keeps rising — new settlements add specific mechanics (most-prominent all-in price, explicit per-charge consent, multi-year audit trails) beyond simply "disclose the total price," which raises the operational burden for any platform showing fees at checkout.

### Proposed Solution
The settlement's remedy terms are a preview of what future ticketing enforcement actions may require: making the true all-in price the most prominent figure everywhere it's shown, and capturing express, itemized consent for every optional or mandatory add-on fee. Etix should audit checkout flows against these exact terms now — all-in price prominence, itemized consent capture, and recordkeeping — rather than wait for a ticketing-specific enforcement action to set the bar.

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and Connecticut Attorney General (AG) William Tong [announced a $4 million settlement](https://portal.ct.gov/ag/press-releases/2026-press-releases/settlement-with-manchester-city-nissan) this week with Manchester City Nissan, resolving allegations that the dealership systematically charged customers unauthorized fees.

In January 2024, the FTC and Connecticut AG’s office jointly sued Manchester City Nissan alleging that the dealership’s data reportedly showed that customers were billed thousands of dollars in “unlawful” fees. According to the complaint, these fees included charges to “certify” vehicles already advertised as certified pre-owned and add-ons, like total loss protection, inserted into financing agreements without documented consumer consent.

The proposed settlement requires $4 million in consumer redress and mandates that the maximum total price, excluding only required government charges, be the most prominently featured figure in any transaction. The proposed settlement also requires express, informed consumer consent for every charge and includes a six-year compliance reporting requirement and five-year recordkeeping requirement.

### Junk Fees: A Genuine Bipartisan Priority

The Manchester City Nissan settlement provides useful insight into the broader enforcement environment. Despite the administration change, junk fee enforcement has remained a consistent federal priority. The FTC’s Trade Regulation Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees was a Biden-era initiative, but it took effect in May 2025 under the Trump administration and has continued to receive active support. The Trump administration separately signed the Combating Unfair Practices in the Live Entertainment Market Executive Order on March 31, 2025, directing the FTC to enhance price transparency in ticket purchasing.

In April 2026, the FTC [announced](https://www.regulatoryoversight.com/2026/04/stubhub-to-refund-10m-in-junk-fees-as-part-of-latest-ftc-settlement/) a $10 million settlement with StubHub, resolving allegations that the company advertised ticket prices without mandatory fees and failed to disclose total pricing wherever prices were displayed. The enforcement pattern, and the business cost of being on the wrong end of it, is now well established.

### Why It Matters

The regulatory and enforcement landscape around junk fees and drip pricing is complex and rapidly evolving, but the underlying expectation is straightforward: consumers must be able to see the true, all-in cost of a product or service clearly and early in the purchasing process. This obligation now flows from multiple, overlapping sources: federal FTC rules, a growing patchwork of state statutes, city-level regulations, and long-standing UDAP authority that state AGs have shown a renewed willingness to deploy.

For businesses operating across multiple jurisdictions, compliance requires more than good intentions. It demands a careful, jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction audit of how prices are advertised, when and how fees are disclosed, and whether mandatory charges are properly built into, or clearly broken out from, the total price.
