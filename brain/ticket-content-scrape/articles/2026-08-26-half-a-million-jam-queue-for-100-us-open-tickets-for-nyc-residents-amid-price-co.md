# Half a Million Jam Queue for $100 US Open Tickets for NYC Residents Amid Price Complaints

## Summary

A limited-release program offering 1,000 $100 US Open tickets to NYC residents drew queue positions reportedly reaching 534,325 within 25 minutes, dramatically illustrating pent-up demand against a backdrop of fan complaints about soaring US Open ticket prices. The $100 seats carry a resale restriction preventing buyers from reselling through Ticketmaster, while unrestricted resale inventory elsewhere in the tournament runs far higher (grounds passes originally $65 listed up to $263; Ashe Stadium opening-day resale from ~$344). Mayor Mamdani, who previously pushed for discounted World Cup ticket set-asides, framed the program as an affordability initiative, but the overwhelming queue shows the allocation size fell far short of demand.

### Key Points
- Discounted allocation: 1,000 tickets at $100 (max 2/buyer) for NYC residents, Aug 30–Sept 8 sessions.
- Reported queue positions reached 534,325 by 10:25 a.m., 25 minutes after the 10 a.m. sale start.
- $100 tickets cannot be resold via Ticketmaster's official secondary marketplace.
- Comes after May 2026 reports of a 400,000+ virtual queue and finals seats jumping from ~$600 (2025) to $2,400.
- Unrestricted resale elsewhere is elevated: $65 grounds passes listed up to $263; Ashe Stadium resale starting ~$344.
- Mayor Mamdani previously pushed FIFA to reserve discounted World Cup tickets for local residents with resale caps.

### Highlights
- 534,325 queue positions reported for just 1,000 discounted tickets — a 500x demand/supply mismatch.
- US Open finals pricing referenced as up from ~$600 to $2,400 year-over-year (May 2026 reporting).
- Resale-restricted affordable inventory paired with an active, much higher-priced official resale market for the same event.

### Industry Problem
Patrons face a widening gap between escalating primary and resale ticket prices for marquee events and their ability to access affordable inventory; large virtual queues for narrow discount allocations demonstrate both severe demand and the limits of scarcity-based affordability programs, while resale-restriction policy also constrains what the affordable-tier buyer can later do with their ticket.

### Proposed Solution
The article doesn't propose a platform fix, but documents two policy levers in tension: below-market set-asides plus resale restrictions. Etix should monitor how resale caps/restrictions interact with real consumer demand (they can push transactions to unregulated channels, per TicketNews' own prior reporting) and consider whether queue-fairness/anti-bot tooling and transparent allocation communication could reduce the reputational cost of these high-demand, low-supply promotions for venue clients running similar affordability tiers.

A limited release of $100 US Open tickets for New York City residents drew extraordinary demand Wednesday morning, with prospective buyers reporting online queue positions stretching into the hundreds of thousands within minutes of the 10 a.m. sale.

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani and the United States Tennis Association [announced Tuesday](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/08/mayor-mamdani--usta-announce-1-000-discounted-tickets-to-us-open) that 1,000 tickets would be reserved for city residents at $100 each, part of a push to make one of New York’s marquee sporting events more accessible as fans continue to complain about soaring prices for the tournament.

The tickets went on sale at 10 a.m. Wednesday on a first-come, first-served basis. But screenshots reviewed by TicketNews suggest demand overwhelmed the size of the allocation almost immediately.

One screenshot timestamped just 10:01 a.m. showed **226,148 users already ahead of the prospective buyer** in the online queue for the “City of New York Residents Special Offer.”

## 1,000 Tickets, Two Per Buyer

Under the program, New York City residents were permitted to purchase as many as two $100 tickets for US Open Main Draw sessions running from August 30 through September 8. The inventory included seats in Arthur Ashe Stadium and Louis Armstrong Stadium as well as grounds passes.

“If the world’s best tennis players are coming to our city, New Yorkers should be able to see them play,” Mamdani said in announcing the offer.

USTA CEO Craig Tiley characterized the program as an extension of the tournament’s efforts to broaden access, which also include free admission during US Open Fan Week.

The discounted allocation arrives against a backdrop of unusually intense complaints about US Open ticket prices this year.

[TicketNews reported in May](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/05/tennis-fans-slam-ticket-prices-us-open-finals/) that fans encountered a virtual queue topping 400,000 during the initial presale for US Open finals tickets, only to find some tickets priced dramatically higher than in previous years and substantial amounts of resale inventory already appearing on Ticketmaster.

Fans at the time posted examples of seats they said had increased from roughly $600 in 2025 to $2,400 this year, while other users complained that after waiting more than an hour they were seeing primarily high-priced resale inventory.

The contrast between those complaints and Wednesday’s rush for $100 seats is difficult to miss. While the special allocation represents only a tiny fraction of the US Open’s overall inventory, the enormous queue provides a real-time indication of how much demand exists for access at a lower fixed price.

## Discount Tickets Come With Resale Restriction

There is also a ticketing-industry wrinkle to the program.

Reuters reported that the $100 tickets cannot be resold through Ticketmaster, limiting buyers’ ability to transfer the specially priced inventory back into the tournament’s official secondary marketplace.

That restriction comes as prices elsewhere in the US Open market remain elevated. Reuters reported ahead of the sale that opening-day Arthur Ashe Stadium resale inventory started around $344, while grounds passes originally priced at $65 had appeared on Ticketmaster for as much as $263.

The special allocation therefore combines two policy tools frequently proposed in ticketing debates: reserving inventory at a below-market price for local residents while restricting the ability of those buyers to resell that inventory through the event’s official marketplace.

For Mamdani, the initiative also follows a position he took before becoming mayor.

As a mayoral candidate last year, Mamdani [called on FIFA to reserve 15% of World Cup tickets for residents of the New York-New Jersey region at discounted prices](https://www.ticketnews.com/2025/09/nyc-mayoral-candidate-zohran-mamdani-pushes-back-on-fifas-world-cup-ticket-pricing/), while also advocating limits on World Cup resale prices. TicketNews noted at the time that resale caps can create their own consumer problems by limiting legitimate marketplace competition and pushing transactions toward unregulated channels.

City Hall says the administration has since secured 1,000 affordable tickets for World Cup matches as well as discounted or free access tied to Gotham FC, the Knicks and other sporting events.

Wednesday’s US Open sale offered perhaps the clearest demonstration yet of both the appeal and limitations of that strategy. Making 1,000 seats available at $100 generated immediate interest — but screenshots showing queue positions in the hundreds of thousands also suggest that the supply of discounted inventory was nowhere close to satisfying the apparent demand.

For the USTA, that may be a welcome indication of enthusiasm for the tournament. For New Yorkers hoping to take advantage of the affordability program, it also meant that scoring one of the discounted seats may have been nearly as competitive as the tennis itself.
