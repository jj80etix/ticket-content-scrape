# From The Beacon, To Rio, China, Fiji & Prince Rupert: Promoter Michael Cohl Talks The Rolling Stones’ Landmark ‘A Bigger Bang’ Tour

## Summary

A 20th-anniversary retrospective with veteran promoter Michael Cohl on The Rolling Stones' 2005-2007 "A Bigger Bang" tour, which grossed $558.3 million and ran long due to interruptions including Mick Jagger's health issues. Cohl recounts logistics across unusual stops including Rio, China, Fiji, and Prince Rupert.

### Key Points
- "A Bigger Bang" grossed $558,255,524 across its 2005-2007 run, the highest-grossing tour in history at the time.
- The tour's length was extended by interruptions, including Jagger-related health issues.
- Cohl discusses staging shows in unconventional locations such as Rio de Janeiro, China, Fiji, and Prince Rupert.
- Piece is a historical/nostalgia feature, not current industry news.

## Transcript

![StonestogetherGettyImages 566895539](https://static.pollstar.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/StonestogetherGettyImages-566895539-scaled.jpg)

The Rolling Stones take a curtain call at San Siro Stadium in Milan as part of their record-setting “A Bigger Bang” tour that ran 2005-2007. Photo by Brian Rasic / Getty Images

It’s been 20 years since The Rolling Stones were in the midst of rocking the world with the massive “A Bigger Bang Tour,” at the time the highest-grossing tour in history. The final tally on “A Bigger Bang” topped a half billion — $558,255,524 to be exact—accumulated between the fall of 2005 and Aug. 26, 2007, when the tour wrapped at the O2 in London after a run of European makeup dates.

The tour dragged on for so long because of some significant interruptions: Mick Jagger got sick, Ronnie Wood went to rehab, and Keith Richards \[reportedly\] fell out of a coconut tree in Fiji and hit his head, necessitating brain surgery. But the tour was also marked by incredible highlights, including a halftime performance at Super Bowl XL in Detroit, a concert at Copacabana Beach in Rio that drew a reported 1.5 million to 2 million people, and a two-night stand at New York’s Beacon Theatre filmed by Martin Scorsese made into the revered film “Shine A Light.”

This was Michael Cohl’s sixth tour helming the Stones, and his last. All were record-setting for their time, beginning with “Steel Wheels Tour” in 1989, “Voodoo Lounge Tour” (1994-1995), “Bridges To Babylon” (1997-1998), “No Security Tour” (1999) and “Licks Tour” (2002-2003), and engineered under his previous Toronto-based companies CPI and TNA. For “A Bigger Bang,” Cohl worked for Live Nation and served as Chairman of the LN board of directors, a situation that continued to June 20, 2008. Today, Cohl dabbles in live under his S2BN banner and is no longer based in Toronto, passing much of his time at his farm in upstate New York. “I know I’m physically older, but spiritually I’ve never gotten older,” Cohl tells Pollstar. When Pollstar reached out to Cohl for a look back at this milestone tour, he had just enjoyed a golf reunion with several friends from the “Bigger Bang” touring team, so Cohl was in a mood to look back.

![CohlGolfIMG 5662 (1)](https://static.pollstar.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/CohlGolfIMG_5662-1-rotated-1.jpeg)

A recent golfing reunion of the “A Bigger Bang” touring team, (from left), Michael Cohl, Mike Sexton, Kent Smith, and Mike Davis.

**Pollstar: By “Bigger Bang,” what had you learned about taking the Rolling Stones around the world?  
**Michael Cohl: To be with people that long you have to learn to get along. I had to get along with Mick and Keith and the other members of the band, and all the people reporting to me. From “Voodoo Lounge” on, we made very few personnel changes, so we were well-honed and learned to get along.

**What about things like fluctuating economies on a global basis and trying to project something that’s very hard to project?  
**We were lucky because the Stones were of such a high status we were able to protect ourselves. When the pesos went in the toilet in Mexico, we were protected, because we always had the option to take American dollars. The same thing when the yen went through the roof in Japan, we always had ourselves protected. We learned early the best thing we could do was be flexible and not try to predict.

**Wasn’t Bill Zysblat involved in that aspect?  
**Bill was there, he was definitely sharp. Joe Rascoff (partner in RZO with Zysblat) was more involved in the tours I did. Rascoff was there with his sleeves rolled up and on site much of the time. I worked with the Stones from ’74 on \[in Canada\]. I did them in Toronto and all the famous gigs. For years, Keith referred to me as his bail bondsman because I got him out of jail the night he got busted in Toronto.

**What was Prince Rupert (their manager) like to deal with?  
**I loved the Prince. It was an interesting experience. He had more of a British accent than the other Brits I worked with like Steve O’Rourke (Pink Floyd) and Tony Smith (Genesis), but was a different ilk. He wasn’t a rock and roller at all, he was a banker. He was a suit and tie, a businessman. He was manager of the Rolling Stones but a different kind of manager. I was used to guys like O’Rourke and Smith, who talked to the group and I would talk to them. The promoter’s role was to talk to the manager and he dealt with the groups and the lunacy and secrecy. Rupert was none of that. He was the business manager, but had the same title as the rest.

When it came to that first Stones gig ever in Philadelphia in 1989, I was at the rehearsals for a couple of weeks. We’re a week before the first gig, and I finally said, “I gotta call Rupert, I can’t believe he’s not here. They’re asking every question and behaving like I’m a manager.” Truthfully, I don’t want to be a manager! I’m very happy being a promoter. I called Rupert and said, “When are you coming?” He said, “I’ll be in New York.” I go, “That’s three weeks into the tour, when are you coming to Philadelphia?” He says, “Oh, I won’t be there until New York, I don’t come on the tour.”

Getting used to Rupert and that relationship was like, “holy shit.” But Rupert was great, because he was extraordinarily smart. He loved good food, so I learned to eat at five-star restaurants all over the world. He was Prince Rupert, so I got to meet all this different royalty and a whole upper echelon of people who were fascinating from a world I wasn’t familiar with and had no expectation to ever know. It was a great education and he became a great friend.

**He sure straightened the Stones’ finances out from where they were when he started.  
**I know. I remember him telling me that the best thing he ever did was get them a lawyer. They were so broke, and that all changed, obviously.

**I looked back at my *Billboard* interview with you for this tour and you described it as “Homer’s Odyssey,” based on all you had to overcome over the course of the tour.  
**That’s pretty good. I’ve seen the most recent \[“Odyssey”\] movie and that’s probably a pretty good description, although it’s more like the Story of Job when I look back 20 years later. That’s a much better description, because in “The Odyssey” the big struggle is to get back to your family, to get home, and our struggle was overcoming every challenge, which is what the Story of Job is about.

**Did you know going in that “A Bigger Bang” could be the biggest tour of all time? Did you think in those terms?  
**I expected every Rolling Stones tour to be bigger than the last, let’s put it that way. That’s the public trophy room, right? Now the public trophy room is easy, because ticket prices kept going up so quickly that you could do less people and gross way more dollars. The real trophy was always, could you do more people? Did we do more people?

**“Bang” was just under 4 million tickets, which is staggering, and a $558 million gross. But the most tickets was one of those in the middle, either “Voodoo Lounge” or “Babylon.”**  
I’m sure “Steel Wheels” did almost 7 million worldwide. It depends how you look at it. “Voodoo Lounge” was close. The way I track it, just for the fun of it, for my own ego, Taylor Swift beat “Steel Wheels” by like 125,000 or something. Now this one, if you want to include a million and a half people from Brazil, then all of a sudden you’re going to get to 6 million and change. So they are all kind of the same, it’s just a different route to that success. We did a lot of theaters and small ones \[on “A Bigger Bang”\]. We did three different kinds of shows in some cities, but in the end, they all did 6 or 7 million people. If you used today’s ticket price and not the $39.50 stupid ticket price in 1989, they would all gross the same. But today, we give up trophies for money only, which is probably wrong, but it is the way it is.

**Well, if anything, it reflects the economics of the time, but to me, especially when you put in those smaller venues like you did on “A Bigger Bang,” the metric is did you sell them all out, or almost sell them all?  
**I agree. Steel Wheels, we had over-demand. There was an early part of “Voodoo Lounge” that we were selling out but not feeling the over-demands. “Bigger Bang,” same thing. Some places we didn’t feel over-demand, but we were packing them in. Towards the end, we felt a little less over-demand. We’d probably been out there too long, the album was already two years old, and we got so many gigs in. The other problem, towards the end, was we missed a lot of gigs, whether it was Micks’ throat or someone dying or falling off a log, it was like, “Holy shit, it’s time to go home.”

**How did you tell over demand in a pre-internet age?  
**By the end there was Ticketmaster and you could tell. There was computer ticketing and you could tell how many people were in line on the phone. You’d have to send people to every outlet, because it was hard tickets and we’d have a phone room with 15 different phones. Scarborough, which is a small area in Toronto, there were 400 people there. Mississauga \[Ont.\] we got 2,000 people. And you move tickets around. Now, with computer tickets, you just dial it up and it says 67,000 people still in line. So you’d be like, “Okay, we got 1,100 tickets left and 22,000 people in line. We got 600 tickets left and 80,000 people in line.” And you’d go, “Holy shit, that over-demand is beyond belief.”

**The intensity of those moments had to be something you couldn’t find anywhere else in life.  
**It is pretty thrilling. And there’s no danger of overdosing, so I learned to really enjoy it.

**I really miss the way each tour was an event at announcement. It was an event when it came to your town for bands like Pink Floyd and the Stones. The whole thing—the on-sale, camping out for tickets, the announcement coming in on balloons or flatbed trucks, or whatever. With each market, they took over a town. Radio was meaningful and into it and all anybody talked about was this show. They owned each market, and you don’t see that today. Most people don’t even care.  
**That’s because there used to be three or four bands that would come through \[to play stadiums\] and it’d be two or three times a year at the most. Some years there’d be only one band that would play the stadium, right? Now, they’ve got 20. It’s a different world.

**I remember \[Live Nation Concerts’\] Bob Roux telling me why he thought these stadium shows were so popular now, “Because it gives people affirmation that there’s 50,000 people in this town that like the same music I do.”  
**That was always a huge part of it. Remember it was called “underground.” Underground is jazz. Rock music was this small little thing, and then holy shit, these guys, I don’t know, 400,000, 500,000 people show up and everybody had to go, “Wow, you like The Who also? You like him? Holy smokes.” In my town, Jimi Hendrix was drawing 4,000-5,000 people in those days. But nobody realized there was a hundred cities where you could drop 5,000 people and they’d all show up.

**It was always about community, but stadium shows aren’t that great. They’re a great event, but they’re not a great show.  
**If you want a great show, go to the Garden or The Beacon. Rolling Stones at The Beacon, oh my God. But there’s a sense of community in a stadium show or a festival you don’t get anywhere else.

**You had to camp out for tickets. I camped out for Pink Floyd as a kid, and that was an event in itself, a community and cultural thing.  
**My mother, whose family was originally from Russia, grew up in Winnipeg. I’m sitting around thinking our generation is so special and we do things so differently. I’m having dinner with her, she’s in her 90s, and I said, “Did you ever have an event like this where you camped out or whatever.” And she said, “Michael, in the ‘40s, Frank Sinatra was coming to Winnipeg and I went out in January, where it’s colder than the fricking frozen north, minus 20 degrees. My sisters and I stayed out all night and waited until the morning when they put tickets on sale. We went to see Sinatra in May of that year in Winnipeg. So think again, son, there’s not a whole lot new you’re doing.” Frank Sinatra, he was Mick Jagger.

**There was probably the greatest caveman who banged on the stump better than anybody that all the other tribes came to see in the natural amphitheater.  
**You’re probably right. If you watch him dance, he’ll maybe even take off his little fur.

**Do you remember anything about the initial rehearsals for “A Bigger Bang” in Toronto?  
**It was at a school called Greenwood. The guy who ran it was a really good friend of mine. It worked out great, because the facility was good and the band liked it. He was just starting the school and the rent helped start the school. It was sooner after the previous tour than normal, and yet everybody was so happy to be doing it and seeing each other. Everybody was excited, it was a bit shorter than usual, but it was great.

**Then you did the pop-up show at the Phoenix.  
**Yeah, we did the pop-up show, $10 a ticket because we always did those. They were tests. Mick always liked to do them, the band liked to do them a week to two before, because it was a live show with an audience and it let them know where they were at with the rehearsals. The Phoenix was probably 800 or 900 \[capacity\]. We always did the show based on whatever the normal cover charge was \[at that venue\]. I would keep like 20 tickets, and let the rest all go. I couldn’t accommodate all my friends, I remember a few of them being pissed off and not talking to me for three or four weeks.

**They’d get over it when they come back.  
**They’d get over it when you come back and you look after them.

**And you had the Beacon, did you like “Shine a Light?”  
**I loved the film. I’m a night owl, I’m always up to say, 1:30, 2 until 6:30. I don’t work in the middle of the night anymore. I used to work all night and get into work and be pissed off at everybody, because “Doggone it, you’re here at 10 o’clock and why aren’t you fucking ready?” Anyways, I’m flipping channels about four weeks ago and watched the whole show. That’s got to be the first time in maybe 15 years I’ve watched an entire Stones concert and it was absolutely great.

**He captured them and they were on point. There were a couple of seldom played songs in there. I just thought it was perfect.  
**I agree. At breakfast the next day I said to \[wife\] Lori, “You won’t believe what I did last night. I watched ‘Shine a Light and I can’t believe how much I enjoyed it.”

![GettyImages 56908862](https://static.pollstar.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/GettyImages-56908862-1-scaled.jpg)

Picture taken during a massive show during the the Rolling Stones’ “A Bigger Bang” world tour, at Copacabana Beach in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil on Feb. 18, 2006, where more than 1.2 million fans attended one of the biggest concerts ever. (Photo by Caio Leal / AFP / Getty Images)

**And then you had a Super Bowl performance.  
**We had Super Bowl. In one month or so we did China for the first time, Super Bowl, and a million and a half people on the beach in Rio. That was the all-time month of anything I ever did with the Stones. That’s not really repeatable, at least under those kinds of circumstances. We were never going to get to China, we were never going to play a free show in Brazil, and we were never going to do the Super Bowl. Three “nevers” that all happened. It’s funny, they say things come in threes, well there you go.

**Then right in the middle of it, you get Keith’s accident and everything comes to a halt.  
**We were on a two- or three-week break, I was home. \[Richards\] was in Fiji, Mick was off back in England, everybody was home and we needed it. Then I got a call in the middle of the night, holy moly, Keith’s had an accident. We’re on the phone, we got a medical plane. It’s a tough decision, because at the time they weren’t sure if they could fix it in Fiji, but they didn’t think he could survive the plane ride. The decision was made and they said they’ll do their best and put him on a plane, because if they can’t fix it in Fiji, it’s for sure death and if they can get him to Australia. We know the result, he made it. We had to postpone, cancel a few shows, we had to move the tour back a bit, and it’s really why 2007 came about. Because they never liked to leave loose ends, that was really something admirable in the band.

In their history, there were dozens and dozens of shows that we missed for whatever reason, but out of those 25-30 shows, we only ever couldn’t make up two, and that was because we couldn’t, not because they didn’t want to. We were willing to make them up. They did a whole tour in ’07 that was motivated by, “We need to make it up to those fans.”

**There were also poor weather conditions, I remember seeing the show in Churchill Downs and it was misty, rainy. A case could have made to pull that one, but they did a hell of a show. And that was a massive production to set up in the rain as well.  
**We never lost one to rain. They were protected and nobody was going to get electrocuted. We almost lost one in Parc des Princes in Paris to mud. It was raining so hard and everything around was mud. We couldn’t get the trucks in. Ultimately, they figured it out with plywood and air blowers and made it happen. The rain was never going to stop it. It made it miserable at times, but it didn’t stop it.

**And that probably cut into the bottom line a bit, bringing in all that stuff?  
**Yes. We always looked at it as one show. The tour was whatever, 150 shows, one was 120, one was 130, but it was one P&L, one show; without that, you couldn’t do 25 little theaters and 10 little clubs, right? But if you just have a plan that says, “Okay, we’re going to go up for a couple of years, we’re going to do a bunch of shows and we’re going to make the best time of it we can and we’re going to make X dollars each,” then you’re free to think of, “Well, let’s do a theater and an arena and a stadium and let’s make a different show.”

**Generally, how close were your projections to how you ended up when you were looking at it as one show?  
**On “Steel Wheels” I was way off, because they did way, way, way better than anyone ever expected. By the end of “Urban Jungle” we learned, we were always within 5%.

**With that number of shows, the different valuations of the dollar, the unforeseen contingency like bringing in plywood and people coming and going and delays, to project within 5% is ridiculous.**  
It’s a little bit of skill and a lot of luck.

**I think you make your own luck, man.  
**Well, I’m not sure about that. Dennis Arfa once nicknamed me “Lucky Lefty,” I think it’s more luck and being in the right place at the right time, and skill.

**You always reported grosses, I think you did on Streisand, why did you do that?  
**Earlier in my career, I went off to Europe and learned that everybody was so secretive, and I realized it was really stupid, because the thing between Donald (Tarlton) and I, we were enemies and just fighting each other, and then we became partners. And when we became partners, we shared the information. And then in Canada, we added 12 other promoters, and we shared all the information. And we knew long before there was an internet or computer that information was king. So then we went down to the Billboard conventions and saw the Americans who were so freaking secretive, but it was to their own detriment. All it did was help the agents and the managers and everybody, who were trying to divide and conquer, but the promoters didn’t share the information. So I was willing to open up and share whatever information. So when I found an outlet where I could report, as long as the band wasn’t offended, because the odd band told us don’t report. But I thought it was the smartest thing we could do. Now, it turned out that it was a good thing for you guys, and you were good forus and we were good for you, and that was good.

I still keep my hand dabbling in. I back a few people, I back the odd show here and there, the first thing you do is go to Pollstar and get the reports. To this day they still don’t do it in Europe, that’s beyond dumb.

**It’s a battle we continue to fight and the festivals, as well. But I’ll say this, it’s a lot easier to report sellouts than it is the dogs.  
**I agree, but it’s more important to report the dogs than it is the sellouts, because if you want to tell somebody “don’t buy an act,” report the dogs. Everybody thinks everything’s so successful. The real value’s in reporting the dogs.

**100%, but it’s a hard argument to make. Now, you, the champion debater, could probably make that argument better than me.  
**I don’t know, that was 60 years ago.
