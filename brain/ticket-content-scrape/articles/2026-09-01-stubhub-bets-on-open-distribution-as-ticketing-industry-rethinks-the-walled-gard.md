# StubHub Bets on Open Distribution as Ticketing Industry Rethinks the Walled Garden

## Summary

StubHub is scaling "Open Distribution," a strategy that plugs the resale marketplace directly into primary ticketing systems (now ~10 live integrations, including recent AudienceView and Spektrix deals) so organizers can sell official inventory through StubHub/viagogo without giving up their existing ticketing provider or exclusivity. The move — echoed by vivenu, TicketNetwork, and TicketSocket — challenges the exclusive-venue-contract model underpinning Ticketmaster's dominance, and lands against the backdrop of the proposed DOJ/Live Nation antitrust settlement, which would itself require Ticketmaster to build a standardized interface for third-party primary distribution.

### Key Points
- StubHub now has ~10 live primary-ticketing integrations; recent additions are an expanded AudienceView Unlimited API deal and a Spektrix integration covering 600+ arts organizations across the US/UK/Canada/Ireland.
- StubHub's self-service Distribution Manager (launched March 2026) lets rights holders list official inventory without a full API integration; the company reports 35+ Open Distribution partners, 84% YoY growth in directly-integrated sales, and one MLB team generating $40M+ through the program.
- StubHub told investors its 2026 guidance assumes no material revenue yet from "Direct Issuance," despite calling it a potentially "transformational" long-term opportunity.
- Peers moving the same direction: Spektrix also distributes via TicketNetwork; vivenu integrates with both TicketNetwork and StubHub; TicketSocket added StubHub/viagogo distribution.
- The proposed DOJ/Live Nation settlement (pending Tunney Act review) would require Ticketmaster to build a standardized third-party primary-distribution interface and offer non-exclusive contract options for future major venue deals — a regulatory mirror of what StubHub is building voluntarily.
- AXS, despite criticizing Ticketmaster's exclusivity, still competes via its own exclusive deals (e.g., a new exclusive primary/secondary partnership for the Wrangler National Finals Rodeo).

### Highlights
- ~10 live primary-ticketing integrations for StubHub as of this report, with AudienceView and Spektrix as the newest.
- 84% YoY growth in StubHub's directly-integrated ticket sales; one MLB team over $40M in sales through the program.
- Pending DOJ/Live Nation settlement would mandate a standardized third-party distribution interface for Ticketmaster — a regulatory push toward the same open-distribution model StubHub is building commercially.

### Industry Problem
The primary-ticketing business is fracturing the long-standing bundle of "back-end technology + exclusive distribution channel," with resale marketplaces and neutral ticketing platforms racing to become the open-distribution layer before regulation (the pending Ticketmaster settlement) potentially forces something similar — a structural shift affecting how any primary platform without exclusive venue lock-in competes for distribution reach.

### Proposed Solution
Build/improve signal: evaluate whether a StubHub/viagogo-style non-exclusive distribution integration (comparable to the AudienceView/Spektrix deals) would extend Etix client inventory's reach without requiring clients to give up pricing control or their primary ticketing system; watch the DOJ/Live Nation settlement's standardized-interface requirement as the trigger that could make this table stakes rather than differentiation.

StubHub is accelerating its effort to become a distribution partner for primary ticketing companies, a strategy that increasingly puts the resale marketplace at the center of a broader industry push toward open, non-exclusive ticket distribution.

Shaun Stewart, StubHub’s vice president of Open Distribution, told TicketNews that the company now has roughly 10 integrations with primary ticketing systems live and no longer views the initiative as an experimental extension of its resale business.

“Open Distribution has moved from concept to a core part of how we operate,” Stewart said. “When we started, we were making the case that primary ticketing systems and global distribution didn’t have to be mutually exclusive. Now we’re seeing that validated in real time.”

The latest examples include [integrations with AudienceView](https://audienceview.com/press-room/audienceview-expands-ticket-distribution-through-stubhub-partnership/) [and Spektrix](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/08/stubhub-viagogo-target-arts-sector-spektrix-integration/) that allow organizations to send official primary inventory to StubHub and international sister marketplace viagogo while continuing to operate through their existing ticketing providers.

## Open Distribution Moves Beyond Individual Deals

StubHub has been building toward that position throughout 2026.

In March, the company [launched Distribution Manager](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/03/stubhub-launches-ai-powered-distribution-manager-for-artists-teams-and-venues/), a self-service system allowing rights holders to place official inventory directly on StubHub without completing a full API integration. At the time, StubHub said it had signed more than 35 Open Distribution partners over the previous 18 months, with directly integrated ticket sales up 84% year over year. One Major League Baseball team had generated more than $40 million in ticket sales through the program, according to the company.

StubHub has also told investors that it is shifting what it previously called Direct Issuance away from a business-development-heavy approach centered on signing individual rights holders and toward a technology-driven system capable of scaling across far more organizations.

The company described non-exclusive distribution of originally issued tickets as a potentially “transformational” long-term opportunity while acknowledging that resale remains overwhelmingly its primary business. StubHub told shareholders earlier this year that its 2026 guidance assumed no material revenue contribution from Direct Issuance, despite its longer-term expectations for the model.

The recent platform integrations provide one avenue for reaching that larger group of rights holders.

Spektrix, which works with more than 800 theaters, museums, venues, attractions and other cultural organizations worldwide, announced an integration with StubHub and viagogo in August that makes direct marketplace distribution available to more than 600 organizations across the United States, Canada, United Kingdom and Ireland. Participating organizations retain control of pricing and inventory, and the arrangement does not require exclusivity.

AudienceView followed with an expanded API integration covering its AudienceView Unlimited customers. Organizations can decide which official tickets reach StubHub and viagogo while AudienceView remains their primary ticketing system and system for managing the event.

The AudienceView relationship itself is not new. StubHub and AudienceView announced a primary-ticket distribution partnership as far back as 2014. The latest agreement expands and updates that connection rather than marking StubHub’s first entry into primary distribution through the platform.

What has changed is the number of systems being connected under essentially the same premise.

“The AudienceView expansion, the Spektrix integration, they’re not one-off deals,” Stewart told TicketNews. “They’re part of a deliberate build toward something bigger, given we have about ten primary integrations now live.”

## Primary and Resale Inventory Under One Roof

For consumers, the architecture can mean primary inventory issued directly by an organizer appears in the same marketplace as tickets listed by individual or professional resellers.

Stewart argues that putting both forms of inventory together gives buyers a wider view of available tickets while allowing organizers to compete for those buyers without surrendering control over the original inventory.

“They set the price, they control the supply, and they keep their fan data,” he said of participating organizers. “We provide the reach.”

The result also complicates some of the conventional boundaries around secondary ticketing. A resale marketplace connected directly to the primary ticketing system becomes a distributor of original inventory as well as a venue for tickets being resold by their original buyers or other sellers.

Whether that produces a cheaper ticket for an individual fan will still depend on the event and market conditions. Primary inventory can compete with resale listings above or below the issuer’s asking price. The more fundamental change is that buyers and rights holders are no longer necessarily confined to the consumer marketplace operated by the company providing the underlying primary ticketing infrastructure.

StubHub says its integrations are deliberately non-exclusive.

“If a partner wants to sell through multiple channels, that’s fine with us,” Stewart said. “What we’re betting on is that our reach, our data, and our buyer experience are compelling enough that partners choose to work with us because it drives results, not because they’re locked in.”

That approach is increasingly visible beyond StubHub. Spektrix’s separate TicketNetwork partnership gives its clients access to another large distribution network, while vivenu similarly allows organizers to choose which inventory flows to outside channels. Vivenu described its own system to TicketNews in August as “the exact opposite of a closed ticketing silo.”

## Exclusivity Remains the Industry Fault Line

That puts the growing open-distribution model against a different structure still deeply embedded in U.S. venue ticketing: long-term exclusive contracts.

Ticketmaster’s venue exclusivity became a central issue in the Justice Department and state antitrust case against parent company Live Nation Entertainment. A [proposed federal settlement awaiting judicial approval](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/07/doj-filing-opens-public-review-period-for-live-nation-ticketmaster-settlement/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) would require Ticketmaster to build a standardized interface allowing qualifying third-party primary marketplaces to distribute tickets while Ticketmaster continues providing the venue’s back-end ticketing technology.

The proposal would also loosen portions of existing venue exclusivity and require Ticketmaster to offer non-exclusive and partially non-exclusive options for future major concert venue contracts.

The proposed settlement has not yet taken effect and remains under Tunney Act review.

Nor is exclusivity solely a Ticketmaster practice.

AEG-owned AXS has repeatedly argued that Ticketmaster’s long-term exclusive contracts hinder its ability to compete for major venues. But AXS also competes through exclusive primary-ticketing arrangements of its own. In August, for example, AXS became the exclusive primary and secondary ticketing partner for the Wrangler National Finals Rodeo. Trial materials from the Live Nation case also included testimony acknowledging that AXS frequently provides financial inducements in connection with exclusive contracts for large venues.

The contrast illustrates a broader structural divide rather than a simple fight between individual companies. Major integrated ticketing operations continue to derive significant value from winning the underlying venue relationship, often on an exclusive basis. A growing collection of independent primary systems and outside marketplaces, meanwhile, is betting that the ticket itself can be distributed through more places without requiring the organizer to replace its core technology.

Stewart compared the model StubHub is pursuing to travel distribution, where inventory from different suppliers can flow through multiple consumer channels.

“A real open ecosystem looks a lot like what travel built decades ago,” Stewart said. “Any inventory, from any supplier, distributable through any channel the organizer chooses, with pricing and data ownership staying with the rights holder.”

Getting there, he acknowledged, would require overcoming technical integration work as well as entrenched commercial relationships.

“The first is structural,” Stewart said. “Exclusive long-term contracts between venues and primary ticketing providers have defined this industry for a long time.”

He also pointed to a cultural resistance to giving up control over where tickets are sold, arguing that parts of the business have benefited from keeping ticket distribution less transparent and more tightly controlled.

“The industry moving on its own terms will always be faster and better for everyone than waiting for a mandate,” Stewart said. “We’d rather be the company building toward that future than the one being dragged into it.”
