## How the Polish Jazz Festival ‘mobilised the diaspora’

The festival is preparing an expanded second edition in London next month on the tails of the growing 'brand' of Polish jazz

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/polish-jazz-festival.jpg)

© Polish Jazz Festival

As it prepares to return for its expanded second edition, London’s Polish Jazz Festival says its proposition of packaging the genre’s artists into a festival has in some cases tripled or quadrupled the live audiences for the style.

Returning between 11-27 September to multiple venues across London, organisers say highlights will include “pioneering” vocalist Urszula Dudziak, pianist Leszek Możdżer, contemporary groups EABS and Wojtek Mazolewski Quintet, and *Chopin Residue*, an ambitious multidisciplinary project reimagining the music of Frédéric Chopin through electronics, improvisation and contemporary composition.

The edition will take place across London heritage venues Cadogan Hall, Union Chapel, both between 900 and 1,000-capacity, as well as the 450-capacity King’s Place, which festival director Łukasz Droździel says marks a significant step-up in venue level for the style.

“I noticed that even though Polish artists turn up every one to two years on the scene, no one has ever slid them together into a single week lineup to make it a festival format,” says Droździel, when asked to recall the origins of the festival.

“I took a bit of a gamble, but seeing how they are invited and do sell out concerts, I thought: ‘why not bring them together and present them as a singular theme rather than particular artists?’,” he adds.

Asked what signs he saw that gave him confidence that now was the right time, and London the right place, to launch the festival in 2025, he says: “ There was no certainty it would be successful, but it quickly transpired there was an appetite for it. By putting those concerts closer together and promoting them in a singular action, it mobilised the diaspora into a patriotic movement.”

But benefits of the joined-up offering quickly became clear, as he says “the quality of the artists is stronger than just them separately”. Local music critics in London noticed it quickly, with the event garnering “a lot of write‑ups and collaborations” after its debut year.

This traction gave the festival the runway to incorporate British artists to ensure the event represents cultural artistic exchange, rather than being an isolated event.

> “ the theme would sell in larger venues, but generally artists would play smaller venues”

“The formula is such that we invite a British artist for each concert to widen the appeal and create unique collaborations,” Droździel tells *IQ.*

The specificity of the programming around Polish jazz played a large part in the attention it received, with the founder saying that a core mission of the event is an educational one for those unfamiliar with what is not just a national portion of jazz music, but a distinct brand.

“ The genre originally started by mimicking the beginnings of American jazz — Gershwin and such. Then the war put a halt on development of the scene, which was still fermenting slightly underground,” he explains.

“The interesting and sad part was that immediately after World War II, it had to go underground, and that period of Polish jazz in the mid‑to‑late 50s was called its catacomb era — its underground era — because playing anything reflective of Western culture and influence was considered anti‑revolution or anti‑patriarchic,” he adds. “ Those people were playing and composing under threat of imprisonment, which is not the greatest environment for producing music, but it made the underground scene quite rebellious.”

The London festival is harking back to these early days of the style by bringing “one of the pioneers”, scat singer Urszula Dudziak, to perform at Cadogan Hall on 12 September. The singer is known for collaborations with American giants of the genre including Nina Simone and Herbie Hancock.

Droździel also points to wider momentum behind the subgenre as it emerges into something of a brand, both across live and recorded music.

“ From the 60s onward, Polish jazz started expanding and, because of its unique brand — a slight weirdness to it, I suppose, it started to be perceived as a brand. Over the last five years, Warner bought a label called Polish Jazz and releases Polish jazz under that label,” he says.

Asked to compare the footing that Polish jazz now finds itself on to the landscape before the festival launched, he says only a handful of the artists within the style have played at the level of venue to which the festival now brings them.

> “ the quality of the artists is stronger than just them separately”

“I know that Anna Maria Jopek, who will be one of the guests at this year’s opening gala, also played with us last year. She has played Union Chapel once before, which was great. But beforehand, any larger venues hosting Polish artists would be thematic concerts — for example, BBC produced a concert in 2018 devoted to the music of Tomasz Stańko in in Cadogan Hall,” the director recalls.

“ So the theme would sell in larger venues, but generally artists would play smaller venues like Jazz Café or the Polish Social and Cultural Institute in Hammersmith — normally 200–300 people, whereas now we have six concerts around the 1,000‑capacity mark in Cadogan Hall, Union Chapel, and one in Kings Place. They have played large events before, but generally it would be much smaller,” Droździel adds.

And with this step-up in venue size, he tells *IQ* that despite having a television festival in Poland under his belt in his promoting career, “ that didn’t carry a lot of weight when knocking on doors like Cadogan Hall or Union Chapel” when plotting the festival’s launch.

“Their requirement is: you have to have done something in London to get something in London,” he says.

Changes and additions for the upcoming second edition include a new gala event, as well as specially written music being performed exclusively for the festival. Last year the festival focused solely on jazz, from avant‑garde to more popular jazz, but in its second run-out Droździel says 2026 will “increase the variety”.

Among his anticipated highlights are also a specially produced performance known as *Chopin Residue,* set for Cadogan Hall on 26 September.

“ The producer, Marcin Masecki, deconstructed Chopin’s music, took away the piano, and wrote an entire album from the reverberations — creating a Pink Floyd‑ish, surreal experience. He cut 100 vinyls, magnified the shavings into fingerprint‑like images, and created a travelling art exhibit.” he explains.

“He invited musicians from around the world — Thurston Moore from Sonic Youth, Adrian Utley from Portishead, the bass player from David Bowie’s band — 30 musicians in total. We will have the first European premiere of that concert,” he says.
