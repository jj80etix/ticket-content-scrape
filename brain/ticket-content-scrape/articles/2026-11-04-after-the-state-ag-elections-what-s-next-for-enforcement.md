# After the State AG Elections: What’s Next for Enforcement

## Summary

Democrats flipped five state attorney general seats (Georgia, Iowa, Kansas, Ohio, Texas) in the Nov. 3 elections, shifting the national AG split to 29 Democratic-22 Republican, with incoming officeholders largely experienced politicians/prosecutors who may ramp up enforcement faster than a typical first-year transition. Troutman Pepper Locke flags four things to watch to gauge how campaign promises translate into real enforcement: senior staffing appointments, handling of inherited investigations, multistate coalition participation, and first enforcement actions.

### Key Points
- Democrats won 20 of 31 AG races; net gain of 5 seats flips Georgia, Iowa, Kansas, Ohio, and Texas from Republican to Democratic control.
- Texas and Kansas had anchored Republican-led multistate litigation coalitions — their flips could reshape which states lead national enforcement actions.
- New AGs inherit active investigations/litigation and must decide whether to continue, expand, settle, or drop them.
- State AGs are expected to continue leading consumer-protection enforcement nationally given divided federal government.
- Firm recommends tracking senior appointments, inherited-matter decisions, multistate coalition moves, and first filings as leading indicators.

### Highlights
- Net 5-seat Democratic AG gain (Georgia, Iowa, Kansas, Ohio, Texas flip); national split now 29D-22R.
- Texas and Kansas, prior anchors of Republican multistate litigation, now under new Democratic leadership.

### Industry Problem
A primary ticketing platform's compliance posture depends heavily on which state AGs lead multistate consumer-protection/junk-fee coalitions; a shift of this scale changes which states' enforcement priorities (and settlement leverage) matter most.

### Proposed Solution
Monitor — watch incoming AGs' senior appointments and first enforcement filings (especially in Georgia, Texas, Kansas, Ohio) as the trigger for reassessing pricing-disclosure and consumer-protection compliance posture.

Voters in 30 states and the District of Columbia chose attorneys general (AG) yesterday, delivering the strongest Democratic down-ballot result in a generation. Assuming the current figures hold in Kansas:

- Democrats will have won 20 of the 31 AG races.
- There will be 13 new AGs.
- Five states — Georgia, Iowa, Kansas, Ohio, and Texas — will have flipped from Republican to Democratic control.
- This net gain of five seats inverts the national map to 29 Democratic and 22 Republican AGs.

For businesses, the consequences will extend beyond which party controls each office. Incoming AGs will inherit investigations, litigation, and multistate initiatives while assembling new leadership teams and setting their own priorities. Their senior appointments, resource commitments, decisions in inherited cases, and multistate participation will offer concrete tests.

### The Incoming Class

- **New Democratic AGs:** Jena Griswold (Colorado), Tanya Miller (Georgia), Nate Willems (Iowa), Chris Mann (Kansas), John Kulewicz (Ohio), Eli Savit (Michigan), Nathan Johnson (Texas), Nicole Cannizzaro (Nevada), and Kimberly Ahern (Rhode Island).
- **New Republican AGs:** Katherine Robertson (Alabama), Jon Echols (Oklahoma), David Stumbo (South Carolina), Lance Russell (South Dakota).

The class is notable for how much public-office experience it carries. Griswold moves from Colorado secretary of state, and Cannizzaro was majority leader of the Nevada Senate. Johnson and Miller are sitting state legislators, and Savit won a Michigan race that pitted two county prosecutors against each other — a prosecutorial profile shared by Ahern (RI), Stumbo (SC), and Russell (SD).

Most of these AGs are not first-time officeholders. In most flipped and open states, the incoming AG arrives with a legislative or prosecutorial network already in place, which could shorten the usual first-year lag in enforcement staffing and coalition sign-ons, so changes in enforcement may arrive sooner than usual.

### The Election Resets the Multistate Landscape

State AGs often coordinate investigations and lawsuits, so a new AG’s choice to join, leave, or lead a coalition reaches beyond one state. The flips in Georgia and Texas are especially consequential: Texas has long anchored Republican multistate litigation, both states have robust staff, and both new AGs will decide which inherited matters to continue. Kansas and Iowa, meanwhile, have spent the past four years as conservative leaders on several issues, including litigation against companies alleged to be Chinese-owned or Chinese-aligned. Past transitions show that change can come quickly: last year, Virginia’s new AG Jay Jones reversed the state’s position in more than a dozen federal cases during his first month.

### New AGs Inherit an Active Enforcement Docket

New AGs will not take office with a blank slate. Many will inherit pending investigations and lawsuits and must decide which matters to continue, expand, settle, redirect, or discontinue. Although Democrats will now control Congress, President Donald Trump has made clear that he will readily veto Democratic legislation. We therefore continue to expect state AGs to lead the nation in consumer protection enforcement, an area where bipartisan coalitions have long operated regardless of party control.

For companies with pending matters, the transition raises a timing question: whether to pursue discussions with the outgoing administration or prepare to engage its successor. Deadlines, procedural posture, and what is known about the incoming team should drive that choice.

### What Businesses Should Watch Next

These developments apply to the industries identified in our [pre-election analysis](https://www.regulatoryoversight.com/2026/10/election-day-is-enforcement-day-what-the-2026-state-ag-races-mean-for-your-business-in-2027-and-beyond/): technology and AI, consumer protection, privacy, gambling and prediction markets, health care, and consumer financial services. Inclusion does not mean every office will pursue every sector.

Businesses should track the gap between what incoming AGs promised and what their offices do. Four developments will help distinguish continuity from a change in enforcement:

1. **People and resources.** Watch senior appointments, such as chief deputy, solicitor general, consumer protection, and antitrust leaders, and funding for promised initiatives. Colorado and Michigan’s new AGs proposed workers’ rights offices, and Georgia’s new AG proposed a Small Business Protection Unit. The test is whether those proposals become staffed, funded units.
2. **Inherited matters.** Look for public filings and settlement announcements on existing investigations and litigation. Companies already involved should identify the new decision-makers and reassess the next milestone, not assume the election resets the case.
3. **Multistate participation.** Track which matters an office joins, continues, or leads — and which offices assume the lead roles Texas and Kansas held in Republican coalitions. Signed filings and announced coalition roles show more than party affiliation or a general pledge to cooperate.
4. **First enforcement actions.** Compare early complaints, settlements, and guidance with campaign commitments, including the conduct challenged and relief sought. A speech signals interest; a filing shows how the office will use its authority.

The election identified the next officeholders; their appointments, resource allocation, and decisions in actual matters will show whether campaign promises become enforcement changes.
