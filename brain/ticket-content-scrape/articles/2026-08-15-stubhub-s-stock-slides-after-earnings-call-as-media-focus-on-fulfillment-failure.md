# StubHub’s Stock Slides After Earnings Call as Media Focus on Fulfillment Failures Continues

## Summary

StubHub's Q2 earnings showed record World Cup-driven growth — GMS up 34% to $3.1B, revenue up 33% to $573.1M — but executives confirmed on the call that widespread ticket-fulfillment failures during the tournament (cancellations, delays, non-delivery) carried a real financial cost: operations/support spend rose to $18.8M from $14M, including $5.2M in added outsourced customer support. CEO Eric Baker repeatedly declined to disclose an actual fulfillment success rate even after an analyst cited a competitor's disclosed 99.7% figure, and the stock fell as much as 20% premarket before closing the week down ~5%, with BofA downgrading to Underperform.

### Highlights
- Q2 GMS $3.1B (+34% YoY), revenue $573.1M (+33%), adjusted EBITDA nearly doubled to $105.7M — largely World Cup-driven.
- Ops & support expense rose to $18.8M from $14M YoY, including $5.2M in added outsourced customer support tied to fulfillment problems.
- California attorney Bradford Clements is handling 275 cases against StubHub from World Cup customers and says he's recovered ~$2.2M for clients to date.
- StubHub declined to disclose its World Cup fulfillment success/failure rate despite direct analyst questioning referencing a competitor's disclosed 99.7% rate.
- Full-year GMS guidance raised to $10.1–10.3B, but EBITDA guidance held at $400–420M, which CFO Connie James attributed partly to "incremental World Cup-related customer support, as well as regulatory advocacy costs."
- Stock fell up to 20% premarket post-earnings, ended the week ~5% down; BofA cut rating from Neutral to Underperform.

### Industry Problem
Operating a resale marketplace at high-demand scale (compressed onsale windows, third-party ticket-transfer infrastructure like FIFA's) exposes real fulfillment risk — canceled/undelivered tickets — that generates direct patron harm, litigation, and now a disclosed financial hit even for a well-capitalized platform. StubHub's refusal to publish a fulfillment rate despite mounting lawsuits underscores that this pain point remains largely unmeasured and unaccountable industry-wide.

### Proposed Solution
StubHub's own response was reactive (surge customer-support spend, no committed SLA or public fulfillment metric). An independent primary platform like Etix, which controls inventory end-to-end rather than depending on third-party transfer systems, could differentiate by publishing fulfillment/on-time-delivery metrics and building explicit guarantees around ticket delivery for high-demand onsales — a concrete trust signal resale marketplaces are currently avoiding.

StubHub’s blockbuster World Cup quarter delivered record ticket sales, but the same tournament also exposed fulfillment problems that became expensive enough to show up in the company’s earnings.

The resale marketplace reported $3.1 billion in gross merchandise sales during the second quarter, up 34% from a year earlier, while revenue jumped 33% to a record $573.1 million. StubHub attributed much of that growth to extraordinary World Cup demand.

But the tournament also brought weeks of complaints from fans whose tickets were canceled, delayed or never delivered, along with lawsuits and regulatory scrutiny. StubHub executives acknowledged on the company’s earnings call that those problems required additional spending on customer service and fulfillment and contributed to pressure on margins.

StubHub’s SEC filing puts a number on at least part of that response. Operations and support expenses increased to $18.8 million from $14 million a year earlier, with the company attributing the increase primarily to an additional $5.2 million spent on outsourced customer support during the quarter.

“As with any event of this scale and complexity, a small subset of orders experienced fulfillment issues, and we chose to increase our investment in customer support and fulfillment to address them,” CEO Eric Baker told investors.

The acknowledgement offers the clearest evidence yet that a controversy previously visible mostly through consumer complaints, negative headlines and litigation also carried a measurable financial cost for StubHub.

Investors reacted sharply. StubHub shares closed at $8.54 before the earnings release Wednesday, then dropped to $7.68 Thursday after falling as much as 20% in premarket trading. The stock recovered 5.2% Friday to close at $8.08, but still finished the week about 5% below its pre-earnings price.

## World Cup Problems Carry a Measurable Cost

[![StubHub](https://www.ticketnews.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/StubHub-desktop-1.webp)](https://stubhub.prf.hn/click/camref:1100l9NoF)

TicketNews reported throughout the World Cup on StubHub customers who said tickets they had purchased were never delivered, including buyers who received repeated assurances from customer-service representatives before orders were ultimately canceled.

StubHub has maintained that it prohibits speculative ticket listings and has pointed toward FIFA’s unusual ticket-transfer infrastructure as an important contributor to the problems. Baker again described FIFA’s tournament-specific ticketing system on the earnings call as an additional layer of complexity for a marketplace attempting to process transactions across more than 75 matches in a compressed period.

The disputes have since expanded into proposed class-action litigation and government scrutiny. Global News reported this week that California attorney Bradford Clements is handling 275 cases against StubHub involving World Cup customers and says he has recovered approximately $2.2 million for clients to date. Clements said one recurring issue involved tickets being listed before FIFA had actually issued the underlying inventory.

Those problems occurred alongside — rather than instead of — massive demand.

Fans from more than 150 countries attended World Cup matches using tickets purchased through StubHub, according to Baker. Adjusted EBITDA nearly doubled to $105.7 million, while adjusted EBITDA margin climbed to 18% from 13% a year earlier.

Some conventional profitability measures moved in the opposite direction, however. Total costs and expenses increased nearly 37% to $553.6 million, faster than revenue growth, while operating income fell to $19.5 million from $25.1 million a year earlier. StubHub reported $14.6 million in consolidated net income, but after preferred-stock impacts the result attributable to common shareholders was essentially breakeven.

The World Cup also compressed StubHub’s adjusted gross margin to 82%. CFO Connie James said higher World Cup-related direct costs reflected both payment-processing and fulfillment dynamics as well as StubHub’s decision to invest more heavily in the customer experience. Management said those costs were already beginning to normalize after the tournament.

## StubHub Won’t Say How Many Orders Failed

Perhaps the most revealing exchange on the earnings call came when an analyst asked StubHub to quantify exactly how successfully it fulfilled World Cup orders.

The analyst noted that a competitor had disclosed a 99.7% successful World Cup fulfillment rate and asked Baker whether StubHub would provide its own figure. He also asked how much additional customer-support spending had been planned in advance versus added in reaction to the widely reported problems.

Baker did not provide a fulfillment rate.

Instead, he repeatedly stressed that StubHub’s goal is ultimately to successfully complete every transaction and said even an extremely high success percentage leaves a meaningful number of disappointed customers at the company’s scale.

“If you do not get to 100% and it is not zero, which is what we strive for, you are going to have upset people, and we understand why,” Baker said.

Baker acknowledged that the absolute number of failures can attract significant attention even when the percentage is small.

“We understand that that will get attention in the press,” he said. “We understand why, and we want to do everything we possibly can, proactively and intelligently, to try and make sure that we can drive that to 100%.”

StubHub repeatedly characterized the affected population as a small subset of World Cup customers and Baker referenced hypothetical fulfillment rates above 99%, but the company did not disclose its actual World Cup success or failure rate.

That omission becomes more notable as documented disputes accumulate. The earnings call offered management a direct opportunity to quantify a problem that has generated lawsuits, regulatory investigations and millions of dollars in customer recoveries. StubHub again declined to do so.

The financial impact is also visible in StubHub’s updated outlook.

Before the World Cup, StubHub expected full-year GMS of $9.9 billion to $10.1 billion and adjusted EBITDA of $400 million to $420 million. Following the record second quarter, the company increased its GMS forecast to $10.1 billion to $10.3 billion.

Its EBITDA forecast did not change.

James specifically said the unchanged $400 million to $420 million forecast incorporates “incremental World Cup-related customer support, as well as regulatory advocacy costs.”

That does not mean fulfillment failures alone consumed all of the potential earnings from StubHub’s increased sales forecast. Management is also taking a cautious approach to second-half consumer spending following the unusually concentrated World Cup demand.

Still, the contrast is notable: StubHub now expects hundreds of millions of dollars more transaction volume than it did three months ago without expecting additional full-year adjusted EBITDA.

Legal and regulatory pressure is adding other costs. StubHub’s SEC filing shows professional-services fees increased $11.2 million year over year during the quarter. James told investors that elevated G&A spending was driven primarily by professional fees related to the evolving regulatory environment and associated legal matters. The company also recorded $3.4 million in litigation reserves during the quarter and $7.9 million during the first half of 2026, though those amounts are not specifically attributed to World Cup disputes.

Wall Street’s response underscored the concern. Analysts had expected StubHub to earn approximately 11 cents per share rather than effectively break even for common shareholders, and BofA Securities downgraded the stock from Neutral to Underperform following the report.

The selloff moderated by Friday, suggesting investors did not treat the quarter as an existential problem. StubHub still ended June with approximately $1.7 billion in cash, continued paying down debt and improved its net leverage from 4.5 times adjusted EBITDA at the end of 2025 to 3 times.

That makes the takeaway from the quarter less about financial distress than the cost of operating a resale marketplace at enormous scale when fulfillment breaks down.

The World Cup generated record business for StubHub. It also drove millions of dollars in additional customer-support spending, pressured margins, produced mounting legal disputes and forced executives to field direct questions from investors about how many customers actually received the tickets they purchased.

StubHub has now acknowledged the problems and the added spending required to address them.

What it still has not disclosed is how often those problems actually occurred.
