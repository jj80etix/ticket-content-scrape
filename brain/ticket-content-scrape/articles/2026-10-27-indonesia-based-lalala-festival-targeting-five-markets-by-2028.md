# Indonesia-based LaLaLa Festival targeting five markets by 2028

## Summary

Indonesia-based LaLaLa Festival is accelerating international expansion, targeting five markets by 2028 after its first overseas event (Manila) this year, with a January run planned across Jakarta, Manila and Bangkok aiming for 100,000 combined attendees.

### Key Points
- Targets five international markets by 2028; made its overseas debut in Manila in 2026.
- January 2027 run spans Jakarta, Manila (Philippine Arena) and Bangkok with market-specific lineups.
- Partners with local promoters (Wilbros Entertainment, PK Entertainment) in each new market.

## Transcript

Promoter Donny Heru outlines the multi-genre brand's rapid expansion plans following the launch of a new association with Asian organisers

![The Flaming Lips at LaLaLa Festival in Jakarta](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/The-Flaming-Lips-at-LaLaLa-Festival-in-Jakarta.png)

The Flaming Lips at LaLaLa Festival in Jakarta

Indonesia-based LaLaLa Festival is accelerating its international expansion, with an ambition to reach five markets by 2028, aiming to draw 100,000 fans across three cities in January, just five months after its first overseas event this year.

Established in 2016, LaLaLa has grown from a niche Indonesian forest gathering into a multi-country, multi-genre festival brand. It moved to Jakarta in 2024, where it became a three-day, 25,000-capacity event, and this year, the brand made its international debut in Manila, the Philippines, headlined by Steve Lacy and Two Door Cinema Club.

The decision to extend beyond Indonesia was driven in part by its roughly 20% international audience, with promoters The Group noting a sizable portion coming out of Manila. Partnering with local promoter Wilbros Entertainment, LaLaLa launched in the Philippines in August, with organisers now targeting an expansion to the 55,000-capacity Philippine Arena for its return on 14 January.

The January run will also see LaLaLa return to Jakarta at ICE BSD City on 17 January, before it makes its debut in Bangkok on 20 January at Impact Challenger Hall. Each city will feature a different lineup led by Olivia Dean at the helm, with other acts lined up including BINI and Lola Amour in Manila, Jungle and St. Vincent in Jakarta, and Milli and Jungle in Bangkok.

“Every market is different. St. Vincent is popular in Indonesia, and [BINI](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/06/is-p-pop-asias-next-major-music-export/) is really big in Manila. We’re trying to get more acts, but it all depends on the schedules,” The Group CEO, Donny Heru, tells *IQ*.

> “We don’t want our brand to last one or two years and then be gone”

Organisers will lay the groundwork for future growth in the Thai market with a 20,000-cap, single-stage edition in Bangkok, dubbed LaLaLa Presents, being delivered in partnership with local promoter PK Entertainment.

“When we find partners in different regions, we need to know the background of the market as well. We don’t want our brand to last one or two years and then be gone; it has to be sustainable,” he says of The Group’s alliances.

The accelerated expansion has also prompted a change in the festival timeline, as it is traditionally positioned in August around Summer Sonic Japan, with a move to January to avoid Manila’s typhoon season and open up capacity with an outdoor setting.

“But we’re looking at Jakarta for August as well,” he points out, adding that they are looking to add two more markets in the mix by 2028.

Cross-border collaboration is a common practice for Asian festivals, and increasingly so. Co-founded by Heru, the Asia-Pacific International Music Festival Association (APIMFA) launched in August to facilitate talent exchanges, share industry expertise, pool resources, and develop more commercially viable offers for acts coming from abroad.

APIMFA’s founding members include leaders from Indonesia’s Java Jazz Festival, Japan’s CUEW, Thailand’s Maho Raspo and Bangkok Music City, China’s Strawberry Festival, and South Korea’s Zandari Festival. The initiative builds on LaLaLa’s existing exchanges with South Korean events Busan Rock and Pentaport.

> “For the headliners, you need some extra money to convince them to come to Asia”

The association is aiming to boost both the draw for global acts and to give rise to talent from nearby Asian markets, too.

“We want to make the industry better. Normally, every festival is always dependent on a big name, but in Asia, there are lots of very talented bands here. It can be difficult for them to get a chance to perform, not only internationally, but sometimes locally,” Heru says.

Beyond programming, APIMFA aims to share best practices in festival management and develop an integrated public relations network to coordinate cross-market communications, with plans in the works to organise an annual conference.

For LaLaLa, the network complements the festival’s programming strategy that spans “rock, hip hop, R&B, and pop” and brings together “all-time artists, new ones, and TikTok trending ones”, he says.

For reference, this year’s Jakarta edition was led by Rex Orange County, Steve Lacy, and The Flaming Lips.

Despite its rapid growth, Heru says that financial pressure is only increasing, with artist fees remaining the largest contributor.

“The cost is getting high, and then of course, for the headliners, you need some extra money to convince them to come to Asia. It’s further away, logistically, from going across Europe or America. To sustain our best business, we have to increase the ticket prices a little bit,” he says.

> “If you only depend on the lineup, it’s difficult to make your brand sustainable”

As such, he says ticket prices are rising from their 2026 levels by around 20% in Jakarta and 30% in Manila.

Additionally, the compressed timeline has presented logistical challenges, particularly in coordinating international acts’ schedules across the three markets. Artist availability resulted in the Manila and Bangkok editions being moved to weekdays, while Jakarta is set for a Sunday, Heru says.

To help bridge the gap, Heru says the festival is working to broaden its revenue base through sponsorships, including regional partnerships with brands interested in multi-market expansions. Doing so can also help improve the long-term stability of the brand.

“If you only depend on the lineup, it’s difficult to make your brand sustainable. Sometimes you have a you can’t get a very good lineup – the schedule isn’t really good, or some artists aren’t not touring – it can be difficult. If you really want to get in with a good lineup, then you might have to throw huge money to convince them to come,” he says.

While LaLaLa is aiming to establish another two outposts in the next two years, Heru says that growth must be matched by a sustainable model.

“Festivals are a tough business, and a lot will not survive,” he says.
