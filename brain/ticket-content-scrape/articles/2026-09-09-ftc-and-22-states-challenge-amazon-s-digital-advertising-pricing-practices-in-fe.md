# FTC and 22 States Challenge Amazon’s Digital Advertising Pricing Practices in Federal Court

## Summary

The FTC and 22 state attorneys general sued Amazon in federal court, alleging it covertly overrode its own "second-price" ad auctions with a hidden "soft reserve" surcharge for more than seven years, extracting over $20 billion from roughly 1.2 million advertisers without disclosure. The complaint alleges Amazon rolled out the surcharge gradually and obscured reporting to avoid detection.

### Key Points
- Filed Aug. 31, 2026 in the Western District of Washington; allegations unproven, Amazon has not yet responded.
- Alleges Amazon told advertisers it ran standard generalized second-price auctions while secretly inflating winning prices via an undisclosed "soft reserve" surcharge since 2018-2019.
- Alleged tactics include gradual rollout to avoid complaints, opaque aggregated reporting, and raising surcharges during peak events like Prime Day.
- Estimated overcharge exceeds $20 billion across ~1.2 million advertising customers.

On August 31, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), joined by the attorneys general (AGs) of 22 states, filed a [complaint](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/AmazonAds-Complaint.pdf) in the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Washington alleging that Amazon.com, Inc. overcharged its approximately 1.2 million advertising customers by manipulating the auctions it uses to set ad prices on its platform. The complaint alleges that, for more than seven years, Amazon covertly inflated advertising prices and that the alleged practice likely extracted more than $20 billion from its advertising customers. Amazon has not responded to the complaint yet, and the allegations have not been proven in court.

### Background: How Amazon Represents Its Ad Auctions

Amazon generates more than $68 billion in annual advertising revenue, primarily through online auctions for ad placements. Its core products — Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, and Display Ads — appear alongside search results on Amazon.com.

Since at least 2012, Amazon has told advertisers it runs a “generalized second price” (GSP) auction, the industry standard in digital search advertising. In a GSP auction, the winning bidder pays only the minimum amount needed to beat the next-highest bid — typically one cent more — incentivizing advertisers to bid their true valuation. According to the complaint, Amazon’s training materials, YouTube channel, online portals, and account representatives all consistently reinforced this message.

### Allegations

The complaint alleges that in late 2018 and early 2019, Amazon began overriding the prices its auctions produced and replacing them with higher prices Amazon set itself — without disclosure, and while continuing to tell advertisers it ran second-price auctions.

The mechanism, allegedly referred to internally as “soft reserve” pricing, worked by applying a hidden surcharge on top of the legitimate auction result, inflating the final price charged to the advertiser. According to the complaint, Amazon did not disclose that its auction pricing had “a surcharge hidden in it” and actively worked to prevent advertisers from discovering it.

Key allegations include:

- **Incremental rollout to avoid detection.** Amazon initially limited the percentage of auctions where it raised prices artificially, rolling out surcharges gradually to avoid triggering advertiser complaints;
- **Opaque reporting to prevent scrutiny.** Amazon controlled and limited the auction data available to customers, with its aggregated price reporting described internally as “opaque,” preventing advertisers from having visibility into the behavior of individual auctions;
- **Exploiting peak shopping days.** Amazon raised its maximum surcharge levels significantly during peak shopping events such as Prime Day and the holiday season, believing that increased shopping activity would “obfuscate” the resulting price increases; and
- **Active cover-ups when complaints arose.** When Amazon received nearly two dozen complaints from large customers during a December 2021 “CPC Spike” incident, it allegedly sent customers a false explanation attributing the price spike to higher advertiser and shopper demand rather than disclosing its surcharge practices.

The complaint allegedly cites internal documents in which Amazon employees raised concerns about the practices. A purported 2023 internal memo, as quoted in the complaint, acknowledged that the use of undisclosed reserve prices was not “doing the right thing for the advertisers” and that while the practices “increase our short-term revenue,” they would “hurt us in the long run.”

### The Alleged Harm and Relief Sought

The complaint alleges that Amazon’s advertising customers — including more than 500,000 small and medium-size businesses — incurred substantial overcharges, and that those costs were ultimately passed through to Amazon shoppers in the form of higher product prices. The FTC brings claims under Section 5(a) of the FTC Act and the 22 plaintiff states bring claims under their respective consumer protection statutes. The complaint seeks a permanent injunction, monetary judgment, disgorgement, civil penalties, and restitution.

### Why It Matters

Regardless of how the litigation is resolved, the complaint raises several issues worth watching:

- **Representations about pricing mechanics may be legally material.** The complaint treats Amazon’s descriptions of its auction model not as marketing language but as material statements that shaped advertisers’ bidding strategies and spending decisions. Companies that describe how their prices are determined should ensure those descriptions match actual practice;
- **Pricing transparency in closed-loop auction environments is under scrutiny.** The complaint highlights the structural tension inherent in platforms that serve simultaneously as the auctioneer and the seller of advertising placements. Regulators appear increasingly focused on whether such arrangements are accompanied by adequate transparency;
- **Internal documentation carries significant legal risk.** The complaint draws heavily on alleged internal communications reflecting awareness that public-facing representations did not match actual pricing practices. Documents expressing concern about the gap between what customers are told and what is happening are exactly the kind of evidence regulators look for; and
- **Multiforum enforcement by the FTC and state AGs amplifies exposure.** The 21-state coalition reflects the continued trend of coordinated federal and state enforcement in consumer protection matters. Companies facing investigations by either the FTC or a state AG should assess the likelihood of parallel or joint proceedings and the cumulative exposure they create.
