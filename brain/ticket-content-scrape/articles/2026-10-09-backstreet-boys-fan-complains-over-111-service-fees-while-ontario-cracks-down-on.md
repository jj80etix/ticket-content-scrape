# Backstreet Boys Fan Complains Over $111 “Service” Fees While Ontario Cracks Down on Ticket Resale

## Summary

A Backstreet Boys fan's viral complaint over C$111 in service fees on two Toronto residency tickets exposes a gap in Ontario's new ticket resale regulation: the province caps resale prices at the original purchase total but places no limit on primary-market fees, which both Ticketmaster and the venue (Rogers-owned Rogers Centre) can set freely. Ontario has separately fined SeatGeek Canada (C$25,000) and StubHub (C$20,000) for resale-cap violations, even as it leaves original-sale fees largely unaddressed. TicketNews notes Ticketmaster, Rogers and Ontario regulators did not respond to requests for comment.

### Key Points
- Ontario's Ticket Sales Act (effective April 24, 2026) caps resale price at the original total purchase price (ticket + mandatory fees + taxes), but does not cap original-sale fees themselves.
- Ticketmaster Canada says it keeps ~5–7% of ticket price as its fee; venues generally set and retain most service/facility fees.
- Ontario fined SeatGeek Canada C$25,000 and StubHub C$20,000 for resale-cap violations; StubHub said it will appeal.
- A separate viral case (Bruno Mars resale listing) showed a $168.30 gap between a $422.45 buyer price and a seller's $254.15 payout on the same $299 ticket, split across seller and buyer fees.
- The law gives Ontario's cabinet regulation-making authority over fees/service charges, but no general fee cap has been issued yet.
- Ticketmaster previously said it waived Ontario seller fees temporarily while adapting to the law in May; current status of that waiver is unclear.

### Highlights
- Ontario's resale cap (effective Apr. 24, 2026) regulates only resale price, leaving primary-market mandatory fees (service, facility) entirely uncapped.
- C$25,000 (SeatGeek Canada) and C$20,000 (StubHub) penalties show active state-level enforcement against secondary marketplaces, with StubHub appealing.
- Ticketmaster self-reports retaining ~5-7% of ticket price as its own fee; most service/facility fee revenue goes to the venue.
- Ontario holds unused regulation-making authority to cap fees/service charges directly — a lever that could be pulled with little notice.

### Industry Problem
Patrons experience primary-market "service" fees as opaque and unexplained even when all-in pricing is technically displayed, while asymmetric regulation (strict resale caps, no primary-fee cap) lets platforms that operate in both markets — like Ticketmaster — collect fees on a ticket's first sale and its resale without a comparable ceiling on either side. This is a core driver of patron fee-fatigue (Q2) and shows regulators are willing to target secondary marketplaces for compliance failures well ahead of addressing primary-market fee structure (Q9/Q11).

### Proposed Solution
The article itself describes no product fix, only the regulatory gap. For an independent primary platform like Etix, the response is to get ahead of this asymmetry: lead with transparent, simple all-in pricing and a clear fee breakdown at every step (ticket vs. facility vs. platform fee) now, rather than waiting for Ontario (or a similar U.S. state) to legislate a primary-fee cap reactively. Given Ontario's standing authority to regulate fees directly, monitor for a rule change and be positioned to comply immediately rather than scrambling like the incumbents did with the resale cap.

## Ontario Capped Resale Prices, Not Primary-Market Fees

Ontario’s revised [Ticket Sales Act](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/17t33?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), which took effect April 24, prohibits tickets from being resold for more than their original total purchase price, with allowances for applicable resale fees, service charges and taxes.

Although frequently described as a “face-value” cap, the legal ceiling is based on the amount originally paid to the primary seller, including the initial ticket price, mandatory fees and taxes.

The legislation also requires resellers and secondary marketplaces to obtain proof of that original purchase price, giving regulators a mechanism to enforce the restriction.

When [announcing the legislation in March](https://news.ontario.ca/en/release/1007203/ontario-protecting-fans-by-capping-ticket-resale-prices?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), Ontario Minister of Public and Business Service Delivery and Procurement Stephen Crawford said the changes were intended to protect consumers from excessive resale markups and prevent professional resellers from exploiting fans.

But the legislation draws a consequential distinction between two groups selling access to the same event.

A venue, artist, promoter or authorized primary ticketing platform can establish an original ticket price based on demand, including through premium or dynamically priced inventory, without being subject to the resale ceiling.

Once a consumer purchases that ticket, however, the amount paid in the original transaction establishes the legal maximum for a subsequent resale, apart from permitted additional charges.

That disparity has already generated controversy elsewhere in Ontario.

In August, [TicketNews examined complaints from Toronto Maple Leafs fans](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/08/leafs-fans-cry-foul-over-sky-high-ticket-prices-despite-resale-cap/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) who encountered original ticket prices reaching hundreds of dollars despite the province’s much-publicized resale restrictions.

The Maple Leafs openly use dynamic pricing for individual games, allowing original prices to respond to market demand. Season-ticket holders and other consumers attempting to resell their tickets face substantially different restrictions.

The Backstreet Boys controversy illustrates a related problem involving fees rather than the underlying ticket price.

Ontario can prevent an individual from reselling a concert ticket above the statutory ceiling while allowing substantial fees to be collected when that ticket is first purchased.

The distinction is particularly notable because the 2026 legislation also established regulation-making authority allowing Ontario’s cabinet to govern ticketing fees and service charges, including limiting their amounts. The province’s resale-price restrictions have not been accompanied by an equivalent general cap on mandatory primary-market fees.

## Ticketmaster Can Collect Fees on Multiple Transactions

The financial implications extend beyond the first sale.

Ontario’s legislation explicitly incorporates the fees paid during the original purchase into the amount used to establish the resale ceiling. It also allows additional marketplace fees and applicable taxes to be charged on subsequent resale transactions.

That structure means a ticket can carry fees from the original transaction into the price of a resale, while the resale marketplace may collect additional charges when the ticket changes hands again.

Where seller-side commissions also apply, the individual ticket holder can receive less than the resale listing price, even when attempting to recover the original purchase cost.

Ticketmaster operates in both the primary and secondary ticketing markets, giving it opportunities to earn revenue from successive transactions involving the same ticket.

The company has faced particular scrutiny over its resale-fee structure.

Earlier this week, [TicketNews reported on a viral Bruno Mars ticket listing](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/10/bruno-mars-ticketmaster-resale-fees-listing/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) that illustrated how dramatically the economics of a resale transaction can differ for buyers and sellers.

In that example, a fan listed a ticket for $299 on Ticketmaster. The platform displayed a $44.85 seller fee, leaving an expected payout of $254.15.

The same ticket was shown to prospective buyers for $422.45, creating a $168.30 difference between the buyer-facing price and the seller’s expected proceeds.

That entire difference cannot automatically be characterized as revenue retained by Ticketmaster, because fees can be shared with other parties. The example also does not establish the fee structure applicable to Ontario events.

Nevertheless, it illustrates the potential for ticketing intermediaries to collect charges from multiple participants in a resale transaction.

The U.S. [Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/ticketmaster?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) has separately alleged that Live Nation and Ticketmaster benefit from collecting fees on original ticket purchases and subsequent resales, including charges assessed to both resale buyers and sellers. Those allegations remain contested and do not establish that the same fee arrangements apply to Ontario transactions.

There is also an important unresolved question about Ticketmaster’s current Ontario policies.

In May, Ticketmaster said it was temporarily waiving seller fees while adapting its resale marketplace to Ontario’s new restrictions. The company also acknowledged that temporary technical limitations could prevent some consumers from recovering their complete original purchase costs.

It remains unclear whether that seller-fee waiver continues or what charges currently apply to resale transactions involving tickets originally purchased through Ticketmaster in Ontario.

The broader regulatory distinction remains regardless: Ontario’s restrictions limit the resale price an individual can establish, but they do not prohibit ticketing platforms from collecting permitted fees on subsequent transactions.

## Resale Marketplaces Face Penalties as Primary Fees Continue

Ontario has meanwhile begun imposing financial penalties on independent ticket resale platforms for violations of its new restrictions.

The province assessed C$25,000 in administrative penalties against SeatGeek Canada over alleged violations involving resale prices, original-price disclosures and proof-of-purchase requirements.

Ontario also imposed C$20,000 in penalties against StubHub, which said it intended to appeal.

As [TicketNews reported in its coverage of the StubHub penalties](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/09/stubhub-ontario-ticket-resale-fine-appeal/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), both companies have raised concerns about requirements that independent marketplaces verify original ticket prices when that information may be controlled by competing primary ticketing platforms.

Ticketmaster has disputed suggestions that the information is inaccessible, noting that original purchasers receive transaction records documenting what they paid.

The enforcement actions demonstrate how aggressively Ontario is pursuing compliance in the secondary market, even as substantial primary-market ticket prices and service fees remain outside the scope of the resale cap.

Supporters of the restrictions argue that preventing resale markups can protect consumers from excessive prices for high-demand events. A buyer able to obtain a ticket at the legally permitted resale price rather than an unrestricted market price can benefit from that protection.

Critics, however, argue that regulating only subsequent resales leaves the original sellers with considerably greater pricing flexibility while imposing additional restrictions and compliance costs on independent competitors.

Those concerns become particularly relevant when a company participates in both the original ticket sale and the resale marketplace, potentially earning fees at multiple stages of a ticket’s life.

## TicketNews Seeks Answers From Ticketmaster, Rogers and Ontario

TicketNews contacted Ticketmaster, Rogers Communications and Ontario’s Ministry of Public and Business Service Delivery and Procurement seeking comment on the Backstreet Boys fees and the broader regulatory issues.

Ticketmaster was asked to clarify which parties determine and receive the service fees associated with the Rogers Centre concerts, whether its previously announced Ontario seller-fee waiver remains in effect, and how it responds to concerns about fees collected on successive transactions involving the same ticket.

Rogers was asked whether Rogers Centre establishes or approves the mandatory charges, what portion of those fees is retained by Rogers-affiliated entities, and whether the venue receives additional revenue when originally purchased tickets are subsequently resold.

Ontario officials were asked to explain the policy rationale for imposing strict resale-price limits without comparable restrictions on original ticket prices or mandatory primary-market fees, and whether the province intends to use its regulatory authority to address those charges.

**None of the three parties had responded to TicketNews’ requests for comment as of publication.**

Ticketmaster previously told Yahoo News Canada that its Canadian platform has displayed all-in ticket prices, including mandatory fees and taxes, since 2018. The fan’s complaint therefore does not, by itself, establish that any fees were hidden or that the transaction violated Canadian pricing laws.

But transparency and affordability are separate issues. Showing consumers a C$111 service charge upfront does not reduce the amount they must pay.

For the Backstreet Boys concerts, the outstanding question is not simply why the charges are so high, but how much of that money is collected by each company involved in selling access to the event.

Ontario’s resale restrictions provide no equivalent ceiling on those original charges, even as the province continues imposing financial penalties on competing marketplaces for exceeding limits on subsequent ticket sales.
