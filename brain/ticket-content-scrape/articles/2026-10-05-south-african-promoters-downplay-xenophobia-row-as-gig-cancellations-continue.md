# South African promoters downplay xenophobia row as gig cancellations continue

## Summary

South African concert promoters are downplaying the business impact of a wave of gig cancellations tied to rising anti-immigration tensions, after Malian singer Salif Keïta indefinitely canceled South African shows and Tyla dropped a Lagos date amid cross-border backlash.

### Key Points
- Keïta cited "xenophobia tensions," calling for peace rather than rejecting South Africa outright.
- Tyla's Lagos date vanished amid Nigerian calls for a boycott over South Africa's treatment of migrants.
- Local promoters say international bookings and artist relationships remain unaffected so far.
- South African authorities confirm anti-immigration protests have included isolated violence and looting.

## Transcript

Live sector figures say pan-African booking continues after high profile acts have scrapped shows in protest of alleged anti-immigration violence

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Salif-Keita-Yannis-Adelbost.jpg)

Salif Keïta © Yannis Adelbost

Following a recent surge in concert concert cancellations both in South Africa and for South African artists abroad, promoters in the country have downplayed the effects of an ongoing xenophobia row on their pan-African show bookings.

One figure in the national market says that recent concert cancellations by international acts have “not impacted” their ability to work with international touring acts.

It comes after a series of high-profile cancellations from African artists of shows in South Africa. Most notably, Malian singer Salif Keïta has indefinitely cancelled upcoming South African performances, citing “xenophobia tensions in South Africa” and calling for peace and unity. Keïta said he could not “ignore reports of xenophobic violence against African migrants”, with his manager, Carolina Vallejo of One World Records, subsequently confirming the decision.

In July, South African artist Tyla’s scheduled Lagos date [disappeared](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/08/tyla-cancels-nigeria-tour-date-as-tensions-with-south-africa-rise/) from her A\*POP World Tour itinerary after the singer faced calls from some Nigerians for a boycott of the concert over South Africa’s treatment of African migrants. Neither Tyla nor her management publicly explained the removal, but it followed mounting pressure from Nigerian fans and commentators urging the Grammy-winning artist to cancel the performance amid renewed allegations of xenophobic attacks against foreign nationals in South Africa.

Keïta, however, stressed that his move was not a rejection of South Africa. “I am not closing the door on South Africa. I am closing the door on violence,” Keïta wrote, adding that the situation did not reflect the South Africa he knew. He had been scheduled to perform at the Mangaung African Cultural Festival.

The decision came amid a period of heightened tension around migration in South Africa. Anti-illegal immigration campaigns and protests have taken place across the country this year, with some demonstrations involving intimidation, violence, and disruption. South African authorities have [reported](https://www.gov.za/news/speeches/natjoints-security-and-anti-illegal-immigration-07-aug-2026) that while the majority of protests were peaceful, incidents of violence, looting and criminal activity occurred.

“Our ability to work with international artists, agents, or managers has not been impacted,” says Ajani Sandridge, founder of Fairwave Media South Africa. Sandridge, who has worked in southern Africa for over a decade, says his business continues to operate across the continent. He adds that tensions are being driven by a relatively small group and that the situation has been magnified by media coverage and some level of miscommunication.

> “I am not closing the door on South Africa. I am closing the door on violence”

Another South African live promoter, who asked not to be identified in this story, equally pushed back on the idea that the current situation represents the reality of the country’s live market. He says that while he understands why Keïta made his decision, the cancellation should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that international artists are avoiding South Africa.

“The reality is different,” he told *IQ*, arguing that social media coverage and individual cancellations can create a perception that is different from what is happening on the ground. For him, recent Pan-African bookings made through his company across South Africa, Ghana and Uganda suggest that African touring relationships are continuing despite the wider tensions.

“I am also planning to tour Nigerian acts and make offers to them here. Nigerians who meet the necessary requirements are more than welcome, as are Zimbabweans, Malawians and other Africans. I also employ a number of Africans, all of whom have appropriate documentation,” he adds.

Amid the row, South African Justice Minister Mmamoloko Kubayi has also said that South African live performers are now facing cancellations in other African countries.

“The majority of our artists perform on the continent and many of them are seeing their gigs being cancelled,” she said. The minister also said that an unnamed South African artist had contacted her to tell her that “all her gigs were cancelled on the continent”.

> “South Africans are not xenophobic, but we are also saying to South Africans, ‘let’s be responsible'”

“South Africans are not xenophobic, but we are also saying to South Africans, ‘let’s be responsible”, she said.

Some of the live figures argued that the industry must be careful about turning individual cancellations into definitive evidence of a wider market trend with Sandrige adding: “In the absence of data and the presence of fear-mongering and exploitation, this *‘Afrophobia’* narrative fills the void.”

But there is already evidence that the tensions can cross borders. Nigeria’s parliament [suspended](https://punchng.com/just-in-national-assembly-suspends-official-visits-to-south-africa/) official visits and legislative participation in South Africa last month, citing concerns over anti-migrant violence, while Nigerian authorities have since been involved in repatriation efforts.

The fallout recalls a similar period in 2019, when xenophobic violence also spilled into the live entertainment business. At the time, Burna Boy withdrew from the Africa Unite concerts in Cape Town and Pretoria amid mounting security concerns and controversy surrounding comments he made during an earlier wave of alleged xenophobic attacks, where he said he would not return to South Africa until the government addressed the issue.

As a result, promoters issued a statement saying: “Following extensive engagement with Burna Boy’s management team, Phambili Media & Play Network have decided to withdraw Burna Boy from the Africans Unite concert.” At the time, Tiwa Savage also pulled out of Johannesburg’s DStv Delicious Festival, saying she could not perform while Nigerians were being “targeted”.

Keïta’s most recent cancellation therefore remains a notable data point, particularly given his stature and the reason he publicised for stepping away. For now, cross-border touring between these African markets continues, even as the tensions surrounding it raise questions about how long that balance can hold.
