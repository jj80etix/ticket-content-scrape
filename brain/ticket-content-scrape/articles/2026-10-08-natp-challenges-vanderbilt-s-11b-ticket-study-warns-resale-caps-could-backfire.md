# NATP Challenges Vanderbilt’s $11B Ticket Study, Warns Resale Caps Could Backfire

## Summary

The National Association of Ticketing Professionals (NATP) published an October 6 rebuttal to Vanderbilt University's "$11 Billion" policy paper, arguing that banning above-face-value resale and capping platform fees at 10% ignores resale's liquidity and allocative-efficiency value and could push sellers out of the market, raising average prices. The dispute lands as Massachusetts Gov. Maura Healey revives the Great Divide Act — a 110%-of-face resale cap plus a 10% marketplace-fee cap for non-sporting events — in a supplemental budget bill, making the methodology fight directly relevant to pending state legislation.

### Key Points
- NATP cites a *Marketing Science* study (He & Lin) finding that sufficiently strict resale price caps can reduce seller participation and raise average transaction prices, even though modest caps can help buyers.
- NATP argues ~40% of secondary-market tickets sell at or below face value, challenging the framing that resale is purely an added consumer cost.
- Both sides cite Leslie & Sorensen (2014, *Review of Economic Studies*): resale raised gross surplus ~4.1%/net ~2.9%, but attendees' own surplus fell ~2% — NATP stresses the efficiency gain, Vanderbilt the consumer-surplus loss.
- Vanderbilt's $11.3B estimate ($4.5B resale markups + $6.8B fees) partly rests on 2004 and 2010–2014 resale data and an NITO study whose underlying data hasn't been independently verified — a methodology issue TicketNews first raised in September.
- Massachusetts' Great Divide Act (H.5679) would cap non-sporting-event resale at 110% of face value and marketplace fees at 10%, paralleling Vanderbilt's nationwide proposal.
- TicketNews' own BTS/Gillette Stadium data point: Ticketmaster's cheapest primary ticket was $135.20 while StubHub/SeatGeek resale started at $20–24, illustrating cases where resale undercuts primary price.

### Highlights
- Vanderbilt proposal: ban above-face resale, cap platform fees at 10%, claims $11.3B/year in consumer savings.
- NATP rebuttal (Oct. 6, blog.natp.org): disputes the savings math and warns strict caps shrink seller participation/liquidity.
- Live legislative vehicle: Massachusetts H.5679 (Great Divide Act) — 110%-of-face resale cap + 10% fee cap, pending in a supplemental budget bill.
- Key empirical tension: He & Lin find strict caps can raise average prices by driving out sellers; Leslie & Sorensen find resale raises aggregate surplus but lowers attendees' own surplus ~2%.

### Industry Problem
Policymakers are moving to cap resale prices and marketplace fees nationally (Vanderbilt's proposal) and state-by-state (Massachusetts) based on contested economic estimates, while leaving primary-market dynamic pricing untouched — a regulatory posture that could reshape secondary-market economics and compliance obligations for any platform touching resale listings or marketplace fees.

### Proposed Solution
No product fix is proposed in the piece — the live battleground is legislative/legal advocacy (NATP vs. Vanderbilt methodology, Massachusetts bill language). For Etix: keep monitoring whether price-cap/fee-cap proposals like H.5679 expand beyond "non-sporting events" or get adopted elsewhere, since a 10%-fee-cap precedent would be directly operationally consequential; escalate from monitor to action if such a cap passes in a state where Etix operates.

The National Association of Ticketing Professionals (NATP) is challenging a Vanderbilt University policy proposal that claims restrictions on ticket resale prices and platform fees could save consumers more than $11 billion annually, arguing that the research overlooks important economic functions of the secondary market and the potential consequences of eliminating professional resale.

In an [October 6 commentary published on the NATP website](https://blog.natp.org/vanderbilt-ticket-resale-competition/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), Zach Anderson, writing on behalf of the professional ticket resale trade association, argues that the Vanderbilt Policy Accelerator’s proposed restrictions could reduce competition, limit ticket availability and potentially produce higher average prices under certain market conditions.

The response follows the August publication of Vanderbilt’s *How to Save Audiences $11 Billion on Live Events*, which recommends prohibiting ticket resale above face value and capping online ticket platform fees at 10%.

Vanderbilt estimates those changes would save American consumers approximately $11.3 billion annually, including $4.5 billion attributed to resale markups and another $6.8 billion in what researchers characterize as excessive ticketing fees.

NATP’s response moves the discussion beyond those methodological questions to a related issue: Even if resale markups and fees could be accurately measured, would restricting them produce the projected consumer savings once the market responded?

## Resale’s Economic Value Goes Beyond the Markup

Anderson argues that Vanderbilt’s analysis treats professional ticket resale primarily as an additional consumer expense while giving insufficient consideration to the services and economic value created by competing secondary-market sellers.

Professional resellers provide liquidity, acquire inventory before demand is fully known, assume the risk that tickets will decline in value and compete to connect available seats with prospective buyers, according to NATP.

The association also argues that the secondary market provides consumers with purchasing opportunities unavailable through the original onsale, including the ability to shop for tickets after an event initially goes on sale and to obtain discounted inventory when demand weakens.

NATP cites industry transaction data indicating that approximately 40% of tickets sold on the secondary market are purchased at or below their original face value.

That distinction matters because resale is not exclusively a market for tickets that appreciate in value. Sellers also absorb losses when demand falls, creating opportunities for consumers to purchase tickets below their original prices.

“You cannot count only one side of the ledger,” Anderson writes.

The broader economic argument is that a resale markup does not automatically represent an equivalent loss of economic welfare. Some of the additional payment represents a transfer between buyer and seller, while the transaction itself may create value by moving a ticket to someone who places greater value on attending the event.

That does not mean individual buyers cannot be financially worse off when they pay substantial markups. Rather, NATP argues that calculating the overall economic consequences of resale requires examining more than the difference between the ticket’s original price and its eventual resale price.

Anderson also challenges the distinction between primary- and secondary-market pricing under Vanderbilt’s proposal.

Primary sellers increasingly employ dynamic pricing and premium products that allow ticket prices to rise in response to demand. Vanderbilt’s proposal would leave those original pricing decisions largely intact while restricting what a subsequent ticket owner could charge.

NATP argues that a $500 ticket remains a $500 expense for the consumer regardless of whether the additional revenue goes to the original seller or a secondary-market participant.

The association does not advocate government restrictions on primary ticket prices. Instead, it questions whether limiting market-based pricing only after the initial transaction would improve affordability or shift greater control over ticket distribution toward primary sellers and their authorized channels.

## Academic Research Identifies Potential Risks of Price Caps

One of the more consequential elements of NATP’s response is its reference to academic research examining how resale price restrictions can affect competition.

A [study published in the journal Marketing Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/mksc.2023.0623?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) by economists Da He and Song Lin examines the consequences of ticket resale platforms voluntarily imposing price ceilings.

Using a model of buyers and sellers participating in a two-sided marketplace, the researchers identified circumstances in which price caps can produce unintended results.

Relatively modest restrictions can lower transaction prices and benefit buyers. However, sufficiently stringent caps may cause sellers who would otherwise command higher prices to leave the marketplace.

With fewer competing sellers, the researchers found that average transaction prices can, under certain conditions, end up higher than they would have been without a price restriction.

The finding does not establish that all resale price caps increase prices. The study models voluntary platform restrictions rather than directly measuring the effects of a nationwide legislative ban on above-face-value resale.

Nevertheless, it identifies a potential market response that is relevant to evaluating such proposals: restricting the prices sellers can charge may also change which sellers participate, how much inventory remains available and the competitive conditions faced by buyers.

NATP argues that those consequences are especially important because [Vanderbilt’s proposal](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-URL/wp-content/uploads/sites/412/2026/08/03190151/How-to-Save-Audiences-11-Billion-on-Live-Events.pdf?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) explicitly seeks to eliminate the professional broker business model while preserving face-value-or-below resale between consumers.

Anderson contends that removing professional sellers would not eliminate the scarcity of tickets to high-demand events. Instead, it could reduce competing sources of inventory and shift more transactions toward primary-market channels or less-regulated alternatives.

The latter possibility also raises questions about consumer protections, including guarantees, payment security and ticket delivery, if transactions migrate away from established marketplaces.

## Two Interpretations of the Same Economic Research

NATP and Vanderbilt also reach different conclusions from an influential academic study that both organizations cite.

Economists Phillip Leslie and Alan Sorensen examined primary- and secondary-market transactions for major rock concerts in research published in the *Review of Economic Studies* in 2014.

Their [analysis of ticket resale and economic welfare](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~atsorensen/papers/leslie_sorensen_2014.pdf?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) found that resale improved the allocation of tickets by moving them toward consumers with higher willingness to pay.

The researchers estimated that resale increased gross economic surplus by approximately 4.1%, with a net gain of approximately 2.9% after accounting for transaction costs and additional efforts to acquire tickets in the primary market.

But the benefits were not distributed evenly.

The same study estimated that the net surplus retained by consumers who actually attended concerts declined by approximately 2%, even as the overall market became more economically efficient.

Vanderbilt’s white paper discusses both findings, emphasizing the reduction in concertgoers’ surplus and the economic value captured by resellers. NATP places greater emphasis on the overall gains from more efficient ticket allocation and the marketplace functions performed by professional sellers.

Those conclusions are not necessarily contradictory. They reflect different measures of economic benefit and different priorities in determining whose welfare should carry the most weight in ticketing policy.

The study also relied on transactions from concerts held in 2004, making it an important examination of resale economics but not a contemporary measurement of the entire ticketing marketplace.

What remains unresolved is how the various economic effects would change under the specific restrictions Vanderbilt proposes, particularly in a market where primary pricing practices, ticket distribution and resale technology have evolved substantially.

## The Debate Has Immediate Legislative Implications

The disagreement is particularly relevant as lawmakers consider proposals that would restrict resale prices without imposing equivalent limits on the original price of tickets.

In Massachusetts, Gov. Maura Healey has renewed her effort to enact the Great Divide Act, which would generally restrict resale prices for covered nonsporting events to 110% of the original ticket price.

As [TicketNews reported in September](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/09/healey-massachusetts-ticket-resale-cap-budget/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), Healey included the proposal in a supplemental budget bill, H.5679, with an additional provision limiting resale-marketplace fees to 10%.

Although the Massachusetts proposal differs from Vanderbilt’s recommended nationwide restrictions, both reflect the growing legislative interest in regulating secondary-market prices while allowing primary sellers to continue determining original ticket prices.

The distinction between primary and resale pricing has become a central point of contention, particularly when the secondary market offers tickets at substantial discounts.

During BTS’ August concerts at Gillette Stadium, TicketNews documented resale listings substantially below the lowest available primary-market prices.

Hours before the August 5 performance, Ticketmaster’s least expensive visible primary ticket was $135.20 including fees, while StubHub showed resale tickets starting at $20 and SeatGeek at $24, also including fees.

Those observations illustrate how secondary-market competition can benefit consumers when demand softens, even as high-demand events can produce substantial resale premiums.

Vanderbilt maintains that its proposed restrictions would make live events more affordable by eliminating above-face-value resale and reducing ticketing fees.

NATP’s response argues that any calculation of the potential savings must also consider how the restrictions could change seller participation, available inventory, competition and consumer purchasing behavior.

Those questions remain relevant to policymakers considering the Massachusetts legislation and similar proposals elsewhere. The eventual effect on ticket buyers would depend not only on the prices the law prohibits, but also on the transactions, inventory and competitive options that remain available after the restrictions take effect.
