# What can the industry learn from the Sheeran/Macklemore fallout?

## Summary

Legal and insurance experts tell IQ that Macklemore's removal from Ed Sheeran's Loop Tour — after stadium owners objected to his pro-Palestine onstage remarks — exposes a gap in standard touring contracts around who controls onstage speech and who bears the cost if a show is pulled or an act exits in protest. Sources expect more artists and their teams to negotiate explicit speech protections into future contracts, and for venues to correspondingly push for stronger reputational-risk clauses.

### Key Points
- Venues can generally enforce reputational-risk cancellation rights contractually; sources say "the First Amendment doesn't override what a contract says."
- If a venue forces a cancellation over reputational concerns, the promoter — not the artist or venue — is likely to absorb losses, and such claims are typically hard to insure.
- Artist-initiated cancellations in protest are generally uninsurable and may relieve promoters/venues of paying the artist's fee.
- Rare precedent exists: Ye's contract for a Tampa Loop Tour stop reportedly barred cancellation over his "identity, prior public statements, political viewpoints, or anticipated expressive content."
- Expected industry shift: greater scrutiny of support acts' public statements before booking, and clearer upfront agreement on who holds cancellation/approval authority.

### Industry Problem
Touring contracts largely lack defined terms for onstage political speech, leaving promoters exposed to uninsurable financial risk when a venue or artist pulls out over reputational concerns — a structural gap the Sheeran/Macklemore fallout has made newly visible.

### Proposed Solution
No ticketing-platform action indicated; this is a contract/insurance practice shift for promoters, artists and venues to negotiate directly. Worth monitoring as grounding context for how show cancellations and refund triggers get decided upstream of the ticketing system.

## Transcript

IQ spoke with several legal and insurance experts about how tours can protect themselves in fallouts with venues over onstage speech

![Ed Sheeran's Loop Tour](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Ed-Sheerans-Loop-Tour-©-Mark-Surridge.png)

Loop Tour © Mark Surridge

The fallout around Macklemore’s removal from Ed Sheeran’s US stadium tour has prompted complex questions around artistic freedom and the power large venues can wield over tours, with experts warning that artists may need to start building speech protection into their contracts. *IQ* has spoken to a range of industry experts to assess what issues have been raised for the industry.

Earlier this month, [Macklemore’s departure](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/09/fans-political-forum-sheeran-macklemore-tour/) from Ed Sheeran’s  *Loop Tour* over pro-Palestine statements made on stage resulted in the rest of the [supporting musicians](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/09/ed-sheerans-support-acts-quit-in-solidarity-with-macklemore/) dropping out, with Sheeran saying his fans “do not expect a political forum” at his shows.

Speaking on Saturday (19 September) at his first show since the situation unfolded, Sheeran said he was “deeply worried about the idea that venues should pre-approve content of a performance,” and that “it should never happen in the free world”.

Sheeran faced calls from some quarters to cancel remaining shows in solidarity, after it became clear that the removal of Macklemore from the tour came at the request of a group of stadium owners, led by Gillette Stadium owner Robert Kraft. While that situation didn’t play out, legal and insurance sources tell *IQ* that promoters would likely be on the hook if cancellations had occurred.

In a situation where a venue had pulled the plug on a concert, such as Sheeran potentially faced if he did not remove Macklemore, the question of who absorbs the loss would come down to what grounds a venue had invoked to stop the show. With a concert cancelled due to a venue claiming reputational damage, one source tells *IQ* this is likely to see the promoter lose out.

“If the promoters had to give lots of ticket money back, and they had to absorb some costs, it probably would have been quite hard to insure for those costs because no one can get insurance against racial damage claims”, they said, referring to Kraft’s claim that Macklemore’s remarks “crossed a line” toward making his stadium “a platform for hate speech”.

“It’s always very difficult. I imagine no-one got insurance for that, so the promoter would have been left holding the costs, and then that would have affected the revenue share between \[the artist\] and the promoter,” the source added.

Most of the questions around liability in Sheeran’s situation will likely go unanswered, due to contractual specifics between the various parties. But to see what the industry could learn from the situation, *IQ* spoke with several legal and touring experts off the record, all of whom point to the same takeaway: artists may need to start building speech protection into their contracts, even if they’ve never thought they needed it.

> “I am deeply worried about the idea that venues should pre-approve content”

**Artists’ bid for greater protection  
**The clearest takeaway for artists and their teams is that contractual protection around speech may become a more important and necessary negotiating point, not only for headliners but also for support acts.

At the highest level of touring, there are myriad contractual layers between artists, promoters, venues, operators, owners, sponsors, partners, and public authorities. Each has different stipulations when it comes to conduct, reputation, morality, and cancellation, with provisions around political views or anticipated speech being uncommon up to this point.

One legal expert says artists should negotiate whatever protections they consider necessary, particularly where the venue itself has significant cancellation or approval rights. If the artist’s contract is with the promoter rather than the venue, it should also be “clearly understood” at contract signing who has authority to remove an opening act or other performer.

A-list headliners have greater negotiating power than support acts, but the Macklemore situation could put a larger emphasis on how the latter is handled. If an opener’s speech can have a domino effect on the headliner, promoter, and venue, there is a strong reason for establishing those rights before a tour begins, experts say.

Take, for example, the [reported](https://www.fox13news.com/news/kanye-west-tampa-concerts-why-bulletproof-contract-prevents-cancellation) contractual language for Ye (formerly known as Kanye West) around Tampa’s Raymond James Stadium, one of the venues on Sheeran’s *Loop Tour*. Agreements for the Ye shows reportedly included protections that prevented cancellation based on his “identity, prior public statements, political viewpoints, or anticipated expressive content”.

Such a provision is unusual, experts say, but it demonstrates that speech protections can be negotiated into the contract. The question now is whether similar provisions will start appearing for self-professed non-political artists like Sheeran or further down the touring ladder.

**Support acts may be a bigger risk than thought  
**The choice of support act may now carry much more weight than previously. In this case, four support acts and an accompanying band walking away led to widespread questions on whether the tour would or could continue.

Sources say this balance could lead promoters and venues to increasingly scrutinise support acts before confirming bookings, including previous public statements, political activity, and potential reputational issues.

Nobody has a crystal ball to see what an act might say on stage in the future, with the added element of time an increasingly tricky one to navigate as tours are booked further and further in advance.

One source says that understanding who you are entering business with – both on a personal and contractual level – could become increasingly important as artists, promoters, and venues try to avoid later disputes.

> “ It’s a commercial decision because the promoter would want to continue working with that venue in the future”

**Protest decisions and insurance  
**Insurance is unlikely to cover any event cancellation which is the artist’s decision, experts tell *IQ.* In the heightened political climate, however, the *Loop Tour* is an example of artists dropping out of shows as an act of protest or solidarity. Accordingly, *IQ* hears that if an artist cancels shows in protest of a venue’s actions, this is likely to allow promoters and venues to avoid paying the performer’s fee.

“The cancellation has to be necessary. If \[an artist\] decided to just not do the show, for whatever reason, that’s within their control; \[…\] insurance is unlikely to pay,” says one expert in the field.

If an act on the tour can be argued to bring a venue into disrepute, however, one source says: “I suspect that would mean the promoter is in breach of their contract and probably would still be on the hook to pay \[an artist\] their fee”.

One insurance sector source says of the case of the *Loop Tour*: “ It’s a really tricky situation. Ultimately, I’m guessing the promoter had to bow down to a bit of pressure there. And that’s probably a commercial decision because the promoter would want to continue working with that venue in the future.”

Meanwhile, Henry Long, insurance adviser for music and live events at Tysers, tells *IQ* options exist for cancellations which are in an artist’s control.

*“* Insurance coverage is available which either is beyond the control of the insured person, or within the control of the insured person. That makes a difference. The contracts that are drawn up between the parties are key, though. They would define where the financial loss would be in the event of cancellation or non-appearance of the artist or support artists,” he says.

“Reputational risks can also be covered, but these are not usually taken out by the insured person – it would be the promoter or producer that would take it out,” he adds.

> “the First Amendment doesn’t override what a contract says”

**Venues may push for greater control**  
But if artists are looking to gain protection over what they can say on stage, venues may look to gain stronger clauses around reputational risk.

Major stadiums have relationships with sponsors, corporate entities, local authorities, and other stakeholders. Their owners and operators, which in the US are often a mix of public and private players, may want the ability to intervene over presumed reputational problems, sources explain.

But ultimately, one legal source explains that depending on contractual specifics, venues already hold the cards when it comes to controlling what is said on stage, if they so choose.

Asked whether an artist or tour in the US could invoke First Amendment rights if threatened with cancellation from a venue over onstage speech, the source says “the contract is very different, and the First Amendment doesn’t override what a contract says”. In this case “the venue would be well within their rights” to prevent an artist performing who they could prove would cause reputational harm to their business.

Sources tell *IQ* that in today’s vociferous political climate, one likely recourse for a venue to do this would be by arguing that onstage speech qualifies as incitement. One insurance expert, however, tells *IQ* the bar for a venue to prove this is very high.

“ Quite apart from cancellation or appearance insurance, \[artists and venues\] can buy terrorism insurance — it covers terrorism, civil commotion, war, things like that,” they say. They believe that in the case of Macklemore’s remarks, this clause would have been extremely difficult to argue, explaining this would require the audience to be “in danger”.

Ultimately, though, they say venues don’t want to lose business with promoters and artists, but vice versa, promoters and artists need venues to stage shows. Multiple experts say thatpromoters are likely to prioritise preserving a future working relationship with a venue over choosing the resource-heavy option of challenging a venue’s invocation of a contractual clause to remove a support act.

> “If the promoter is left holding the costs, that would affect the revenue share between \[the artist\] and the promoter”

**Does providing a platform mean endorsing the artist?**  
Underlying all of this is a question the live industry has increasingly had to confront: does giving an artist a stage mean endorsing what they say?

In the past 18 months, festivals around the globe have grappled with this reality. Hungary’s Sziget firmed up on its “ [freedom of speech](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2025/08/the-pain-is-real-on-both-sides/) ” platform after the government banned Kneecap from entering and performing. France’s Eurockéennes de Belfort [denounced](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2025/07/eurockeennes-boss-hits-out-as-rappers-set-banned/) a decision to ban a controversial rapper from performing. Denmark’s Syd For Solen said it was a “ [privilege](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2025/08/syd-for-solen-art-should-be-allowed-to-drive-us-forward/) ” to give artists a platform to discuss humanitarian issues.

Superstruct Entertainment also recently introduced an [Artistic Code](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/09/superstruct-reveals-artistic-code-for-festival-portfolio/) that explicitly addresses the tension, a year after the brand came under intense scrutiny surrounding its [parent company KKR](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2025/05/superstruct-festivals-respond-to-kkr-boycott-calls/). The code outlines “no censorship of artists simply because their views are controversial”, while also setting boundaries around discrimination, safety, and incitement.

But how can the industry establish rules protecting artistic freedoms without leaving promoters and venues without recourse when boundaries are crossed? For some sources, the live industry should learn from the ordeal that contractual agreements around responsibility need to be clearer.

In the end, the biggest takeaway is how risk is allocated on multi-million-dollar tours like *Loop*. The result is likely to be more complicated, drawn-out touring agreements, but ones that might lead to fewer surprises when the tour hits the road.
