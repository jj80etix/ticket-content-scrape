# Serbian live sector ‘under threat’ amid ongoing protests

## Summary

Serbian live-music industry figures say the sector is "under threat" as artists who publicly support ongoing student protests report being blacklisted from publicly-funded festivals and venues, with bookings cancelled and grant funding stalled. The unrest traces back to a November 2024 railway-canopy collapse in Novi Sad that killed 16 people; EXIT Festival already relocated out of Serbia last year citing government pressure and withdrawal of over €1.5 million in public co-funding.

### Key Points
- Multiple Serbian acts (Goblini, Ritam Nereda, rapper Marčelo) report festival cancellations and an inability to book new shows tied to protest support.
- EXIT Festival left Novi Sad in 2025, citing "fierce government pressure" and loss of over €1.5M in public funding/sponsorship.
- Belgrade Beer Fest's 2026 edition remains in doubt, with minimal lineup announced and no public updates since June.
- One publicly-funded festival (Arsenal Festival) denies maintaining any blacklist.

## Serbian live sector ‘under threat’ amid ongoing protests

Artists supportive of protests allege being "blacklisted" as festivals fear losing public funding, while events at the heart of boycotts face uncertain futures

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/smush-webp/2026/08/Marko-Edge-SOLOMUN-19-scaled.jpg.webp)

EXIT Festival recently left Serbia citing political pressure © Marko Edge

Live music industry figures in Serbia have said the country’s sector is “under threat” as they claim artists known to support ongoing student protests are increasingly “blacklisted” from festivals and venues receiving government funding.

It comes as the latest chapter of the saga around protests that have been ongoing since the November 2024 collapse of a railway station canopy in the city of Novi Sad, which killed 16 people. The protests have since spread nationwide.

In the initial fallout from the protests, EXIT Festival announced last year it would be [leaving its home in Novi Sad](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2025/08/exit-launches-global-appeal-to-secure-future/) in favour of a travelling version of the event, citing “fierce government pressure and complete withdrawal of over €1.5 million in public co-funding and government-controlled sponsorships”.

And in recent days, sources told *IQ* festivals and venues that are wholly or partly publicly funded are reluctant to book acts known to be supportive of the protests.

“Since the student protests began in Serbia, artists, festivals, and cultural organisations that have publicly supported the movement have increasingly faced political pressure. Musicians have had concerts cancelled, venues have been pressured to withdraw bookings, and many performers have effectively been blacklisted because of their public stance against the government,” one source said.

They added their concern that “many cultural grant competitions have either not been launched at all or have been significantly delayed”.

A member of punk band Goblini, alleged to *IQ* there is a “coordinated and simultaneous approach across Serbia against my band”, which they said “clearly shows that everything has been well organised and that decisions have been made in a centralised way”.

“It didn’t come as a surprise that we gave our full support to \[the protests\], and this was reflected through issuing one of our songs dedicated to students. This song was the trigger for the widespread cancellation of our concerts in Serbia,” they said.

Goblini first had a festival performance cancelled at the Dani Piva Zrenjanin in July 2025, with the band member saying organisers told the group that “they were not interested in politically engaged artist’’.

> “places where we used to have concerts regularly tell us that there is ‘no available slot’ for us”

The musician added the timing of this decision aroused suspicion: “For us this was quite a surprise. We already played twice at that festival. Organisers were well aware that Goblini are not a ‘political’ band since we do not support any political party.”

“The week after that we found out that we would not perform at Zaječarska Gitarijada Festival too,” they said. The festival is owned and managed by the city of Zaječar.

“This time, the organiser publicly stated that we were not part of their lineup despite the fact that they announced us in their press conference held in Belgrade two months earlier,” the band member said, adding: “We have ended up in a situation where it is almost impossible to book new concerts, as in 2026 we only played two concerts in Serbia. The majority of our concerts in 2026 were scheduled and performed abroad.”

In recent weeks, the 2026 edition of Belgrade Beer Fest, one of Serbia’s biggest events, scheduled for later this month, has come under some doubt, with only one performance announced so far, that of Judas Priest set for 28 August, and no public communications by the event since early June.

This comes after last year’s event was affected by boycotts by some fans and artists, encouraged by the Belgrade Remains movement. In response to this, the festival said following its 2025 edition: “We are aware of the current political and social situation, which has brought many tensions and divisions. Unfortunately, many performers today are facing serious pressure and threats, and we fully understand and respect the decisions of those who, for these reasons, have cancelled their appearance at this year’s festival.”

Meanwhile, Serbian rapper and writer Marčelo, real name Marko Šelić, told *IQ* that since the protests began, “places where we used to have concerts regularly tell us that there is ‘no available slot’ for us”.

“After two decades of a career, the band and I are back to square one: in terms of what we have to work with, we have nothing but talent and our instruments, just like everyone else who is on the regime’s blacklist. On top of all that, we all receive threats from regime supporters,” he said, adding: “The regime’s tabloids target us literally every day.”

“All these summer festivals, which are largely financed from city budgets, never invite us, nor do libraries when it comes to literary events. Festivals that are funded by sponsors and ticket sales don’t invite us either, because they fear retaliation from the authorities,” he said.

Other acts and events who have claimed publicly that they have lost bookings due to their stance on the protest include punk band Ritam Nereda and Nojz Festival, which cancelled its upcoming edition last month, saying: “We’ll see each other in happier times — times in which censorship of art is unacceptable”.

*IQ* contacted several Serbian festivals for a response to the claims by artists and managers that bookings of acts supportive of the protests are avoided due to a fear of losing sponsorships, either directly from public funding or private sponsors.

> “artists, festivals, and cultural organisations that have publicly supported the movement have increasingly faced political pressure”

One of the country’s biggest events, Arsenal Festival, which receives 15-16% of its funding from the city of Kragujevac, said it “has never created any blacklists”. The festival’s co-owner and current head of the Serbian Water Polo Association, Nemanja Marijan, said the event can boast “a very broad spectrum of performers who have appeared, holding diverse political views, both local and global, with which I personally may or may not agree, but that is strictly my personal business”.

Several other Serbian festival, including the Dani Piva Zrenjanin, Belgrade Beer Fest, and Zaječarska Gitarijada, did not respond to requests for comment.

The situation in Serbia is not the only recent instance of freedom of expression becoming a live issue in the touring and booking space. Last year, organisers of Hungary’s Sziget Festival said that “cancel culture and cultural boycotts are not the solution” after the Hungarian government banned Irish rap group Kneecap from the country.

Speaking to *IQ* about developments in Serbia, a representative for EXIT Festival said: “The world needs to know that, in the heart of Europe, a systematic elimination of the free-thinking cultural scene of an entire country is taking place.”

“Organisers, promoters, bands, and artists who have supported the students are being blacklisted by the regime, and since the government controls almost all venues and has control over all cultural funding programs, the opportunities available for performances are becoming extremely limited. This situation has been going on for about a year and a half and has become extremely dramatic,” they added.
