# Creating connection: Seven takeaways from Spain’s GXG

## Summary

IQ recapped seven takeaways from GXG (Global Gathering of the Experience Economy) in Madrid, where 350+ touring-entertainment executives discussed feasibility modeling, scalable show design, the gap between audience awareness and purchase intent, and alternative revenue (F&B, early access) as ticket sales grow harder to drive in volume. Speakers also flagged a lack of institutional capital in the experience-economy space and argued human connection, not technology, is ultimately the product.

### Key Points
- Brilliant Work's David Schulman: most projects fail in financial feasibility modeling before the creative stage ever matters.
- Wishbone Entertainment's George Wood: designs shows with 10-20% market-specific flex to scale productions like the Peaky Blinders Experience internationally.
- Multiple panelists distinguished brand "awareness" from ticket-buying "appetite," citing conversion (not engagement) as the real bottleneck.
- Wishbone's Amy Farrant: F&B and pre/post-show add-ons are being used to offset pressure on ticket sales, which panelists said are "never...harder to sell in volume."
- EVOQ Ventures' Neil Chakravarti: institutional capital remains largely absent from experience-economy deals.
- GXG returns to Madrid Oct. 4-5, 2027.

## Creating connection: Seven takeaways from Spain’s GXG

The Global Gathering of the Experience Economy brought together over 350 executives from the touring entertainment industry

![GXG 2026](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/GXG-2026.png)

GXG 2026

GXG – the successor to the longstanding [Experience Economy Meeting](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/07/fever-to-reimagine-madrids-experience-economy-meeting/) – brought together over 350 top-tier executives from across the touring entertainment industry in Madrid for two days of networking and panel discussions.

The conference arrives as the experience economy becomes an increasingly large part of the live entertainment sphere, with wider touring, squeezed ticket prices, and heightened consumer expectations all core parts of conversations shaping the space [today](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/09/what-does-the-future-of-the-experience-economy-look-like/).

Here, *IQ* unpacks seven of the key takeaways from the discussions that took place:

**Fix it in the spreadsheet, not the build**  
“Most projects don’t fail because of the creative. There are many great creative ideas. Most projects fail in the build somewhere,” said Brilliant Work’s David Schulman.

His team runs a full feasibility model, including break-even ticket numbers, before spending anything. “The cheapest place to fix a project is when it’s still a spreadsheet.”

**Design for scale from day one**  
“One of the first questions you have to ask is, do I envisage this show going anywhere else?” said George Wood, CEO of Wishbone Entertainment, which is behind the Peaky Blinders Experience in London.

He told GXG he builds in 10-20% flex per market: “80% of it is the same, and we present it as if it is bespoke to this market.”

Marlene Toussaint of Family Entertainment Live, whose Mattel-licensed shows tour 32 countries, says the key is knowing what can’t change: “We need to understand really well what the show’s DNA is.”

> “They’re looking to understand how they’re going to feel when they’re in that room”

**Brand awareness isn’t demand**  
The Path Entertainment Group CEO David Hutchinson says it took him a long time to learn the difference between awareness and appetite. The real question, he says, is “how likely is someone to buy a ticket for that brand in that format.”

Olivier Goulet of Supply and Demand agrees that the gap is usually conversion: “there’s engagement, but there’s no conversion.”

**Sell the feeling, not the tech**  
Fever’s Mariano Otero said audiences don’t care about specs.

“They’re not looking to understand whether it’s 5K projection mapping. They’re looking to understand how they’re going to feel when they’re in that room.” Fever’s tests with video previews of sensory experiences lifted conversion.

Wishbone’s Amy Farrant added a warning: “You’re being aware of technology when it breaks.”

**F&B can be key to financial success**  
Wishbone’s Peaky Blinders Experience starts in the bar, a deliberate move to take pressure off ticketing, because “it’s never been harder to sell tickets in volume as we were used to,” Wood shared.

Toussaint sells two-hour-early pre-show access with the drivers, plus post-visit photo sales, so spend is spread across the customer journey.

> “We need to demonstrate sustained, repeatable successes”

**Capital is the missing piece**  
“Institutional capital is not really present in this space,” said EVOQ Ventures’ Neil Chakravarti, noting that most funds are too big for deals of this size.

Yet he praised the industry for getting this far on little: “The industry doesn’t have enough capital to be conservative with.”

Schulman says the way to unlock is: “We need to demonstrate sustained, repeatable successes.”

**Human connection is the product**  
Tom Lionetti-Maguire of Little Lion Entertainment said the recipe for success was simple: “Get people together, make it fun.”

Tomas Carrasco admitted that a tech-heavy Chilean experience missed the mark when visitors just wanted to play with their kids.

Farrant summed up the goal: “creating moments of connection for people that they’ll remember for hopefully months and years to come”.
