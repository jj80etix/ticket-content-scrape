# VIP offerings becoming vital to long-term touring strategies

## Summary

VIP-experience supplier Sound Rink, which has sold 600,000 VIP tickets across roughly 300 tours annually, says VIP add-ons and premium merchandise are becoming a financial lifeline for small- and mid-tier touring artists as margins tighten, driven by Gen Z fans who are increasingly willing to pay for tangible, shareable experiences.

### Key Points
- Sound Rink: 600,000 VIP tickets sold, ~300 tours serviced annually.
- Founded 2012 to help touring acts "come home with money" via VIP revenue.
- Gen Z cited (per Live Nation/Luminate data referenced) as highest-spending, least price-sensitive live-music demographic.
- Trend cited: growing demand for physical/tangible memorabilia (e.g., enamel pins, challenge coins) alongside standard meet-and-greets.

## Transcript

The next generation of fans is increasingly looking for physical representations of their favourite acts, says full-service supplier Sound Rink

![Sound Rink's Cody DeLong](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Sound-Rinks-Cody-DeLong.png)

Sound Rink's Cody DeLong

As touring margins get thinner, acts offering VIP add-ons can offer small- and mid-tier musicians a lifeline in securing their long-term touring careers, with the next generation of fans increasingly looking for physical representations of their favourite artists.

Behind the scenes sits Sound Rink, a full-service supplier that has already sold 600,000 VIP tickets and services roughly 300 global tours annually. Drawing on previous live industry experience, founder/CEO Cody DeLong set up the enterprise with partners Jason Mageau and Scott Lee in 2012 to “make sure bands actually come home with money,” he tells *IQ*.

“For bands, VIP has helped pay for a lot of things on the road. It’s helped artists be able to afford to put on bigger live shows on bigger stages, and I think that as an asset, it’s twofold. It helps fund the tour and increase the budget, but it also helps bring in your superfans and help keep them superfans,” DeLong says.

“Everybody’s talking about the superfan, but how do you really take care of them?” he adds.

The next generation of fans are heavily invested in music fandom, with a 2025 Live Nation study finding [fandom](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/04/music-fandom-is-central-to-gen-zs-identity/) is a core element of Gen Z’s identity.

To the digital-savvy generation, live experiences, curated offerings, and tangible merchandise are of notable importance. Now the highest-spending demographic for live, Gen Z is also becoming less [price-sensitive](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/07/gen-zs-price-sensitivity-declining-despite-concert-costs-rising/), per *Luminate*, indicating that these audiences are increasingly willing to pay a premium for experiences they deem worthwhile.

That translates into a recipe for success for VIP suppliers like Sound Rink, who work directly with management and agencies to help bands “maximise their profit potential and bring in additional revenue,” DeLong says.

Speaking of Gen Z, Rinks says: “The people want that experience, but they also want to be able to share it with their friends. That really helps the band; it’s almost like the fan is paying for the experience, but the band is then also getting free marketing.”

> “Regardless of the size of rooms you’re playing, I think it can help”

Beyond the standard meet & greet or photo opportunity, Rinks points out a shift back toward physical memorabilia, a tactile representation of fans’ memories and enduring fandom.

“This past year, we’ve done more than we’ve ever done before in premium items, and we’ve been getting highly creative with these artists on what to include,” he says.

For Breaking Benjamin, this took the shape of an exclusive enamel pin set and challenge coin, and for Dance Gavin Dance, the team worked on a fully bespoke Monopoly board game tailored around the band’s history, which Rinks calls “the most intricate merch piece I’ve ever worked on.”

But the buy-in for fans doesn’t have to be at that level, with DeLong pointing out rising talent can step onto the VIP ladder with a more contained offer.

“Younger bands doing VIP, they try to keep the price at a minimum or not too much, so they’ll include items that may not be as premium or include just one really premium item to help capture those fans,” he says, adding: “Regardless of the size of rooms you’re playing, I think it can help you if you have a fan base that will buy it.”

But to do so, standing out in a busy market of fans with stretched budgets is of the utmost importance. In the VIP vein, originality is becoming a key asset, which DeLong expects to become increasingly necessary in the future.

“You’re going to see more and more bands continuing to offer VIPs, and I think you’re going to see a lot more premium-type items that are included in the packages and rely more heavily on trying to distinguish from other artists,” DeLong says.

Ultimately, the value of including and curating VIP packages early in one’s touring career is one with a long-term payoff.

“In the end, it just helps create these fans that love and talk about your band, to hopefully create more fans and buy more VIP, and ultimately sell more tickets for your concerts,” he says.
