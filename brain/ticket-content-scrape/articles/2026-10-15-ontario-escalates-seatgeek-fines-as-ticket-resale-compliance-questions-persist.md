# Ontario Escalates SeatGeek Fines as Ticket Resale Compliance Questions Persist

## Summary

Ontario hit SeatGeek Canada with another C$22,500 in administrative penalties (Oct. 8) for resale-price-cap and original-price-disclosure violations under its ticket resale regulations, bringing SeatGeek's cumulative fines to C$47,500 as the province applies its escalating second-offense penalty tier. SeatGeek maintains the underlying compliance problem — resellers often can't access or verify the original face value, especially for season tickets, information it says is controlled by primary providers like Ticketmaster — remains unresolved; StubHub Canada faces a separate C$20,000 in penalties and is appealing.

### Highlights
- Oct. 8 penalties: C$15,000 for exceeding the resale price cap, C$7,500 for failing to disclose original purchase price; cumulative SeatGeek fines now C$47,500 since enforcement began.
- Under Ontario Reg. 175/26, penalties escalate per repeat violation (e.g., major violations: $10K → $15K → $25K); October amounts match the second-offense tier.
- Ontario's rules (effective April 2026) cap resale at the original total price paid (plus permitted resale fees/taxes) and require proof of original price — but primary sellers remain free to use dynamic pricing.
- SeatGeek argues primary platforms like Ticketmaster control the transaction records resellers need to prove compliance; Ticketmaster disputes this, saying buyers/sellers have receipts available.

### Industry Problem
Resale-price-cap laws tied to "original purchase price" create a verification burden that independent secondary platforms say they structurally cannot meet without cooperation from the primary ticketing provider — a dynamic that critics say entrenches primary platforms' competitive position even as it's framed as consumer protection.

### Proposed Solution
The dispute signals a potential role for primary platforms (including Etix) in standardizing original-price disclosure/receipts in a way resale platforms and regulators can rely on; worth monitoring Ontario's enforcement pattern and whether other jurisdictions adopt similar price-cap-plus-disclosure regimes, since that could reshape secondary-market compliance expectations industry-wide.

## SeatGeek Previously Challenged Ontario’s Compliance Requirements

The latest penalties arrive amid an ongoing dispute between Ontario regulators and independent resale marketplaces over how the province’s ticket resale restrictions should be implemented.

Ontario’s rules, which took effect in April, generally prohibit tickets from being resold for more than the total amount originally paid to the primary seller, including the original transaction’s fees and taxes. Permitted secondary-market service fees and applicable taxes can still be added to the resale transaction.

The legislation also requires resale platforms to disclose the original purchase price and obtain proof of that amount from sellers before facilitating transactions.

However, independent marketplaces have argued that complying with these requirements can be difficult when the original transaction occurred through a competing primary ticketing platform.

When [TicketNews reported SeatGeek’s initial penalties in September](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/09/ontario-seatgeek-ticket-resale-fine/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), Joe Freeman, the company’s vice president of government affairs, said the underlying problems remained unresolved.

“The core challenge hasn’t changed,” Freeman told TicketNews. “Many sellers, including season ticket holders, don’t have access to the original face value of their tickets.”

Freeman argued that original purchase information is often controlled by the primary ticket issuer rather than the resale marketplace. He specifically criticized Ticketmaster, saying the company’s control over transaction information made compliance more difficult for competing platforms.

Ticketmaster has disputed that characterization, arguing that primary ticket purchasers receive receipts documenting their purchases and that sellers can provide that information to independent resale platforms.

The disagreement highlights a distinction between obtaining documentation from a ticket holder and independently verifying information generated by another company’s ticketing system.

Ontario’s [public consumer guidance](https://www.ontario.ca/page/buying-tickets-events-ontario?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) requires resale marketplaces to receive proof of the original purchase price but does not specify what documentation qualifies as acceptable proof or describe an independent authentication standard.

The Ticket Sales Act expressly authorizes the provincial government to establish regulations defining what constitutes proof, but the public guidance does not resolve questions about how marketplaces should handle potentially incomplete documentation, altered receipts or season-ticket packages with uncertain individual-game values.

The October penalties are not specifically for failing to obtain proof. Nevertheless, the broader verification problem remains relevant to a marketplace’s obligation to accurately disclose the original purchase price.

## Ontario Defends Enforcement as StubHub Also Faces Penalties

Ontario maintains that its resale restrictions are necessary to protect consumers from excessive ticket markups and ensure that tickets remain affordable.

In comments provided to The Canadian Press on Thursday, Giulia Paikin, a spokesperson for Ontario Minister of Public and Business Service Delivery and Procurement Stephen Crawford, reiterated the province’s enforcement position.

“We will not hesitate to hold bad actors who break the rules accountable,” Paikin said.

However, the ministry did not provide specific information about the ticket transactions underlying the latest penalties. SeatGeek also did not immediately respond to the publication’s questions about the events involved or whether it plans to appeal.

SeatGeek is not the only major resale marketplace facing financial penalties under Ontario’s legislation.

In September, [StubHub Canada received C$20,000 in administrative penalties](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/09/stubhub-ontario-ticket-resale-fine-appeal/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) for alleged violations involving resale prices and the requirement to obtain proof of original purchase costs.

Unlike SeatGeek’s response to its August penalties, StubHub announced that it intended to appeal its assessments.

Both marketplaces have questioned how they can reliably determine original purchase prices when they do not necessarily control or have direct access to the records maintained by primary ticketing providers.

The restrictions also create different pricing rules for the primary and secondary markets. Primary sellers remain free to use variable or dynamic pricing, while consumers who subsequently resell their tickets are generally prohibited from charging more than their own original purchase price, excluding permitted resale fees and taxes.

Supporters of the legislation view that restriction as protection against excessive resale markups. Critics argue that it can limit consumers’ ability to recover losses across multiple tickets, particularly for season-ticket holders, while potentially strengthening the competitive position of primary ticketing companies that already control the underlying transaction information.

For SeatGeek, the latest penalties demonstrate the growing financial consequences of Ontario’s enforcement framework.

What remains unresolved in the public record is precisely which transactions prompted the October assessments, when those transactions occurred and whether the company intends to contest the new penalties.

The province’s escalating enforcement is now clear. Whether its approach ultimately resolves the compliance difficulties raised by independent marketplaces, rather than simply increasing the penalties associated with them, remains an open question.
