# ‘Special in so many ways’: Atlas Festival on turning challenge into opportunity

## Summary

Ukraine's Atlas Festival drew 95,000 fans to its 2026 edition at Kyiv's Blockbuster Mall despite a second consecutive air-raid evacuation, and raised roughly three-quarters of its 150 million UAH (€292M) fundraising goal across 87 acts. Programming director Vlad Yaremchuk tells IQ the festival has effectively outgrown its site but can't relocate because the venue's shelter capacity is what makes rapid evacuation possible, so the team is focusing on experience quality over scale. International booking remains difficult under wartime conditions, making this year's headline slot for Australian duo breathe. — who arrived hours after a 50-missile strike on Kyiv — one of the festival's most resonant moments.

### Key Points
- 95,000 attendees across 87 acts, down slightly from 2025 but still ahead of the 2024 comeback edition.
- Second consecutive year an air-raid alert forced a full crowd evacuation; organizers say the response was faster and better organized than 2025's.
- Fundraising target: 150 million UAH (€292 million); the weekend reached roughly 75% of that goal.
- Site (Blockbuster Mall, Kyiv) retained specifically for its shelter/evacuation capability despite the festival having outgrown it.
- Headliners breathe. (Australia) played their first-ever Ukraine shows, arriving after a 50-missile strike landed as close as 900m from their hotel; they are producing a documentary on the visit, due in three weeks.
- Pohoda Festival founder Michal Kascak performed with his band VBPS a week after his own festival in Slovakia, continuing a cross-festival relationship dating to 2022.

## ‘Special in so many ways’: Atlas Festival on turning challenge into opportunity

The Ukrainian festival tells IQ how outgrowing its current site and persistent difficulty booking internationally are forging some of the festival's most profound moments

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Atlas-Festival-2026-2-1.png)

© Atlas Festival

Ukraine’s Atlas Festival has celebrated continued success that has seen it “outgrow” its current site, as it hosted 95,000 fans and increased its fundraising goals for its 2026 edition this month, despite air raid alerts forcing a crowd evacuation for the second year running.

A lineup of 87 acts meant the festival’s fundraising goal grew even more ambitious this year as it targeted 150 million UAH (€292 million). Fundraising from the weekend itself was cause for celebration for organisers, as it brought the festival three quarters of the way towards this target, but the realities of the war were never far away.

“We had our second ever evacuation this year and we were happy with how it went,” Yaremchuk says. “It was even quicker and more organised as we took last year’s lessons to heart and did our best to do even better.”

Despite welcoming the growth of the festival since it returned to Ukraine in 2024, he explains a second consecutive event disrupted by an emergency evacuation shows Atlas is, at least for the time being, tied to its current site at Kyiv’s Blockbuster Mall due to the stringent safety standards organisers keep at heart.

“Even though we feel like we’ve already outgrown the venue where we are doing the festival during the war, it is not possible to move anywhere as it offers the best shelter, which enables us to evacuate the whole festival in mere minutes, which we’ve done twice successfully,” Yaremchuk says.

Any such site change would take “immense coordination, teamwork, and resilience”, Yaremchuk says, while expressing pride that the festival team has honed its operational protocols around such security threats.

“It’s great to see that we are improving, even though I wish we never had to,” he says.

But rather than a limitation, Atlas is looking at ways that a long-term commitment to the site can be a chance to broaden its offerings. The programming director says: “We also work hard to make people’s time at the festival as smooth and as comfortable as possible. We can’t improve in size or quantity, so we put our efforts into quality.”

> “we feel like we’ve already outgrown the venue where we are doing the festival during the war,”

While the 95,000 visitors to this year’s festival, Yaremchuk concedes, is “sadly a bit less than last year”, it is still “way ahead” of the 2024 comeback edition. And the realities of the sort of vigilance to security threats that are the new normal for the festival partly explain why Atlas’ lineup continues mostly to consist of local Ukrainian artists, as “booking international acts is still difficult,” Yaremchuk says.

Against this backdrop, though, he explains there are still artists who see coming to Ukraine “as an important mission and are ready to entrust their safety and wellbeing to us”, something the festival respects “immensely” and treats with the “utmost responsibility”.

Nevertheless, a restricted pool from which to book acts means organisers have to find novel ways to keep a feeling of freshness. And this has had the effect of Atlas becoming something of a locus for rising Ukrainian acts to announce themselves on a big stage for the first time.

“It is quite challenging to do a line-up of 80-plus acts every year while mostly being limited to local talent. We only have so many headliners and it’s not like new headliners pop-up every year amidst the war, though there is no absence of certain acts breaking through the ranks,” he says. “So we try our best to make the performances special, to switch things around, to introduce new young talent for whom our stage is the biggest yet and try to offer a lot more than just artist performances.”

> “We can’t improve in size or quantity, so we put our efforts into quality”

Since its comeback in 2024, however, Yaremchuk reflects that successive lineups have been punctuated by enough international acts to engender a sense of international support, even while the event is dealing with some constrictions on its programming possibilities.

“In 2024, when we came back, it was Within Temptation. Last year it was The Rasmus,” he recalls, adding that this year that spot was filled by Australian soul duo breathe., whom Yaremchuk says “travelled all the way from Sydney (that’s over 15000km) which took more than two days.”

“They covered their distance to play their first ever performances in Ukraine where they have their second biggest audience worldwide despite never having played there before. It was truly special,” he said of the artist. Among the “many” highlights, Yaremchuk calls breathe.’s performance “special in so many ways”, as the symbolic value of international acts at the festival is as profound as at any point in the last four years.

> “It’s great to see that we are improving, even though I wish we never had to”

“Not only was it a masterfully done show, but to see how much it meant to the fans who came to see them was priceless. They also showed an intro video of how excited, yet nervous they were as they prepared to depart from Sydney. In the middle of the performance they showed another video with messages from their dear friends across the world saying words of support to the Ukrainians,” he said.

Speaking to *IQ,* breathe. said that playing Atlas Festival was “one of the most profound experiences of our lives”.

“We arrived at the festival after a night of 50 ballistic missiles landing in Kyiv as close as 900m from our hotel. At the time, it was Kyiv’s largest attack since the full-scale invasion started more than four and half years ago. Less than 12 hours later, there were 30,000 people coming together to celebrate music, community and life,” the group said.

“ As soon as we started the room, the number of people playing went from zero to 5,000 in about 15 minutes. That contrast is something we’ll never forget,” they added. The band is now producing a documentary film about its visit to Ukraine that will be released in three weeks’ time.

This year, Atlas also welcomed Michal Kascak, the founder of Pohoda Festival, whom Yaremchuk calls “one of the dearest friends of our festival and of Ukraine”. Kascak played with his band VBPS only a week after wrapping up his own Pohoda Festival in Trenčín, Slovakia.

“It was very special as we had always dreamt of visiting each other’s festivals,” Yaremchuk says. “I came to Pohoda Festival in 2022 because of the war and he finally came to Atlas Festival this year — truly a guest of honour. As always, he also brought ambulances to the frontline as a part of his journey alongside amazing Slovak journalists and activists.”

As it outgrows its current site, Yaremchuk is framing not being able to grow in scale as an invitation to grow in quality, telling *IQ* this has crystallised the team’s focus for future years.

“Coming to play in Ukraine has its challenges, but we are ready to do the impossible for those who want to try,” he says. “My hope is that for 2027 we can attract more great international acts to come and have an experience of a lifetime with us.”
