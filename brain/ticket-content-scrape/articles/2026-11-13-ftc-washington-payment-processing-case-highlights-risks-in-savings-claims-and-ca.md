# FTC, Washington Payment-Processing Case Highlights Risks in Savings Claims and Cancellation Practices

## Summary

The FTC and Washington state secured a proposed $32 million judgment (mostly suspended) against payment processor Riverside Payments over allegations it misrepresented potential savings and made cancellation difficult for small-business customers, with over 300 Washington businesses eligible for relief. The law-firm summary draws broader compliance lessons about substantiating savings claims, aligning sales representations with contract terms, and ensuring cancellation is as easy as sign-up — none of which are specific to the ticketing industry.

### Key Points
- Proposed $32M judgment against Riverside Payments; all but $600,000 suspended due to inability to pay.
- 300+ Washington small businesses eligible for relief; 60-day fee-free rescission window ordered.
- FTC alleges inflated savings claims (40-60%) that omitted material fees.
- Order would require clear disclosure, express consent, and an easy cancellation mechanism for negative-option features.
- Case brought jointly under the FTC Act, Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, and Washington's Consumer Protection Act.

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and Washington state secured a proposed $32 million judgment against Vancouver, WA-based payment processor Riverside Payments Inc., an affiliate, and their owners [over allegations that they deceived small businesses about potential savings, contract terms, and their ability to cancel services](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/11/ftc-washington-take-action-against-payment-processor-deceiving-small-businesses-about-costs). All but $600,000 of the judgment would be suspended due to Riverside’s inability to pay the full amount.

[Under the proposed order](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/Riverside-Order.pdf), the defendants would fund $600,000 in merchant relief and give customers a 60-day period to rescind their payment-processing agreements without early termination fees or other penalties. [More than 300 Washington small businesses are eligible for relief](https://www.atg.wa.gov/news/news-releases/wa-ftc-reach-600k-settlement-payment-processing-company-deceptive-practices).

The allegations offer three broader lessons for businesses.

### What Should Businesses Consider When Making Savings Claims?

According to the complaint, Riverside Payments Inc. used quote sheets comparing what prospective customers paid their existing payment processors with what they purportedly could save by switching to Riverside. Regulators allege that Riverside at times claimed businesses could save 40% to 60%.

Yet, according to the FTC and the state of Washington, Riverside’s comparisons omitted charges customers could incur, including activation fees, batch fees, and fees associated with data-security compliance or inactive processing. Businesses that switched to Riverside typically ended up paying more than they had paid their previous providers, according to the complaint.

The allegations illustrate a risk that extends beyond payment processing. Businesses making quantified savings or comparative-pricing claims should be prepared to support not just the advertised percentage, but also the methodology used to calculate it. Fees or other costs that materially affect the comparison should be accounted for when evaluating whether a savings claim can be substantiated.

### When Sales Representations and Contract Terms Don’t Align

The allegations underscore a broader compliance point: material contract terms should align with the representations customers receive during the sales process, and businesses should consider when and how those terms are disclosed.

Businesses should review the sales process and written agreements together. Representations about savings, cancellation, and risk should be consistent with material contract terms, including termination fees, renewal provisions, and related contractual obligations.

### What Does the Case Say About Cancellation Practices?

The FTC and state of Washington allege that customers trying to cancel Riverside Payments Inc.’s services encountered unanswered requests and promised callbacks that often never came. The complaint describes customers being caught in a “complicated loop of deflection and fruitless phone calls.”

The proposed order addresses more than representations about cancellation. It would require the defendants to clearly and conspicuously disclose material terms of transactions involving a negative-option feature, obtain express informed consent before charging customers, maintain records verifying that consent, and provide a simple mechanism for cancellation. Under the proposed order, the cancellation mechanism generally must be at least as easy to use as the mechanism the customer used to consent to the negative-option feature.

The practical lesson is that compliance does not stop once renewal terms have been disclosed. Businesses using automatically renewing agreements should treat disclosure, consent, billing, and cancellation as parts of the same process and assess whether the experience customers encounter in practice matches the terms they were promised.

### The Broader Takeaway

The Riverside matter also illustrates how the same alleged sales practices can create overlapping federal and state enforcement exposure. The FTC alleges violations of the FTC Act and Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, and the state of Washington alleges violations of the Washington Consumer Protection Act.

For businesses making quantified savings claims or using automatically renewing agreements, the case underscores the value of reviewing the customer experience from beginning to end. That review should extend from the methodology behind advertising claims and representations made by sales personnel through contract formation, billing, and cancellation.
