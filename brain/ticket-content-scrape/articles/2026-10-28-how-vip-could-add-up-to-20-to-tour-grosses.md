# How VIP could add ‘up to 20%’ to tour grosses

## Summary

IQ reports that VIP packages can now add up to 20% to top arena/stadium tour grosses, with VIP agency leaders (SUPER's Mike Savas, SeatUnique's Robin Sherry, Ten Technologies Group) describing VIP/premium revenue as a foundational line in promoters' earnings planning rather than an add-on. Sources cite a $4.5bn addressable "super-fan monetization" market (Goldman Sachs) and data showing loyalty is driven by purchase frequency, not higher per-order spend, with Ticketmaster/AXS/Shopify behavioral data showing per-order spend barely changes between a fan's 1st ($443) and 10th ($485) purchase.

### Highlights
- VIP/premium can add 10–20% to grosses for hot arena/stadium tours (up to $200k/night on a $1M sellout, per SUPER's Savas); 5–10% for sub-triple-A acts.
- SeatUnique's Sherry: "20% of the seats probably generating around 50% of the revenue" — comparable to airline premium-cabin economics.
- Please & Thank You's cross-platform (Ticketmaster, AXS, Shopify) data: per-order VIP spend is flat from 1st to 10th purchase ($443 → $485); "loyalty runs through frequency, not price."
- Ten Technologies Group's 2M members average 3 ticketed purchases/year at ~£350 average order value; ticket sales up 135% YoY after its AEG Presents partnership.
- Promoters/managers must balance VIP vs. platinum allocation since promoters are cut into platinum revenue but often not VIP revenue when rights sit with an outside agency.

### Industry Problem
Promoters and venues lack a clean, platform-native way to plan, price and allocate VIP/platinum inventory alongside standard ticketing, and the data suggests the bigger lever for revenue and retention is repeat-purchase frequency rather than per-transaction upsell — a gap between how ticketing platforms price and how fan loyalty actually behaves.

### Proposed Solution
The piece doesn't describe a specific platform product beyond third-party VIP agencies' own tools; for Etix, the signal is to evaluate building VIP/platinum inventory management and frequency-based loyalty/retention features directly into the primary ticketing platform rather than ceding that revenue and fan-data layer to outside hospitality agencies.

## Transcript

Companies in the space say recent years have seen VIP revenue become a central pillar of tour cost and earnings planning for promoters and agents

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/IQ-and-Annual-Banners-26-VIP.png)

**VIP offerings on the top arena and stadium tours can now add up to 20% to a tour’s gross and represents “the largest growth opportunity for fans and managers”, according to leaders in the sector.**

In conversation with *IQ,* VIP agencies say t he aftermath of the pandemic has illuminated the untapped potential of the sub-sector, and that recent years have seen tour organisers start to use VIP offerings as a foundational part of their earnings and cost calculations.

This includes Mike Savas, CEO of VIP experiential management agency SUPER, who suggests soaring costs and shrinking margins after the pandemic have highlighted VIP as a critical ancillary revenue stream for artist teams, with Goldman Sachs estimating that the addressable market for super-fan monetisation is at $4.5bn.

“For arena and stadium tours that are hotter than that, it’s 10–20%. So a million-dollar sellout arena act that’s very hot could be bringing in as much as $200,000 a night,” Savas says.

Robin Sherry, CEO of SeatUnique, goes a step further, saying: “We’re heading in a direction where we start to look at 20% of the seats probably generating around 50% of the revenue.” Seat Unique Group is a premium hospitality platform that has partnerships with the likes of Co-op Live and KMJ Entertainment.

Even below the triple A level, Savas suggests that VIP can add between 5% and 10% to the gross touring revenue for the average arena and stadium-level acts.

“What we’re specialists in is stacking that revenue: add in travel, and you find another 1–3%; add in a brand deal, and it’s another 1 or 2%. So we can get artists an additional 5% above VIP when it’s stacked properly. I would argue it’s the most undervalued and largest growth opportunity for fans and managers.”

“No one wanted to offer an expensive package to their fans; they thought it was an injustice,” continues Savas, who is thought to be the world’s first-ever global touring VIP coordinator – for AEG Presents in 2008.

> “I would argue it’s the most undervalued and largest growth opportunity for fans and managers”

Another early pioneer of VIP services, Eddie Meehan from Please & Thank You (P&TY), had a similar experience. “When I started doing this, I couldn’t get anybody to do it. I spent half a decade talking people into it,” he tells *IQ.*

It wasn’t until the early to mid-2010s that the industry was forced to embrace VIP, largely as a means to combat scalpers, who were selling the best tickets at inflated prices to the disadvantage of everyone else.

“The scalpers actually helped us because we’re able to price our tickets in the same range but pack them with a ton of value; all for the same price fans would otherwise just be getting ripped off for,” explains Meehan, whose company works with 302 artists across 34 countries.

Ten Technologies Group (TTG), which recently partnered with AEG Presents, has also seen ticket sales grow more than 135% year on year. Music and sport are the top two categories requested by its membership, which is predominantly high-net-worth individuals.

“I think that reflects how difficult it is to get tickets for top-tier music,” says TTG’s global director of lifestyle proposition, Lucy Chillingworth. “When a member joins, we ask what events and artists interest them \[…\] Then we can reach out directly to them when an on-sale is coming.”

“The problem was that fans struggled to know where to buy from, and rights holders have struggled traditionally with online distribution,” explains Sherry. “It used to be called corporate hospitality, and it was sold by telesales or by sending PDFs, and it was very cumbersome. We wanted to bring all those packages online and make them frictionless, safe, and secure to buy.”

> In my experience, most of the promoters will use VIP pricing as the baseline

**Cost calculations  
**Promoters and agents are also increasingly using VIP revenue as a baseline for their earnings projections for shows and tours, rather than tacking VIP packages on top of standard ticket revenue.

“If you think about first-class business and premium economy, that’s probably about 35% of the seats available on the plane, but it accounts for about 68% of the revenue,” explains Seat Unique’s Sherry for a comparison.

“In my experience, most of the promoters will use VIP pricing as the baseline, and then very intelligently price platinum just below VIP because the promoter is cut in on the platinum side,” agrees Savas.

“They get their fee on that, and the ticketing platform gets their fee on that. When it comes to VIP, if the rights are given to an outside agency like mine, the promoters do not get a piece of that. So the promoters prefer selling platinum, and it’s on the manager to insist that VIP and platinum are balanced, because if it’s not balanced, you’re going to have imperfect location holds for those seats.”

While some promoters’ in-house VIP operation standardises prices for VIP globally, specialists warn that they must be adjusted on a market-by-market basis. “There’s a baseline: what did it cost in the US?” explains Savas. “Then adjust that for local currency and then adjust based on face value. It’s definitely not one-size-fits-all.”

> “we’re starting to look at 20% of the seats probably generating around 50% of the revenue”

However, P&TY believes that pricing strategy is of secondary importance to the retention strategy. Using behavioural data from Ticketmaster, AXS, and Shopify, P&TY mapped the complete lifecycle of a VIP fan. Across all platforms, per-order spend barely changes from the 1st purchase ($443) to the 10th ($485), the company found.

“The repeat buyer isn’t spending dramatically more per transaction – they’re spending more often. Loyalty runs through frequency, not price,” the company’s white paper states.

Repeat buyers are critical to Ten Technologies, which says that, on average, its 2 million registered members use Ten’s ticketing service three times a year. The average order value is around £350, with an average of 2.5 tickets per order.

“If we hook someone in with a good-quality ticket, they keep coming back,” says Chillingworth.
