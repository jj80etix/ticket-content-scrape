# DOL Finalizes Pharmacy Benefit Manager Fee Disclosure Rule: What PBMs Must Disclose, and Should Be Ready To Defend

## Summary

The U.S. Department of Labor finalized a rule (effective for plan years starting July 1, 2027) requiring pharmacy benefit managers serving large self-insured health plans to disclose detailed compensation data — including rebates, spread pricing, and recoupments — and to submit to employer audits. PBMs that miss disclosure deadlines risk exposing their employer clients to ERISA fiduciary-breach liability.

### Key Points
- Applies to PBMs serving self-insured plans with 100+ employees/participants; covers compensation of $1,000+ annually.
- Requires initial disclosures (90 days before renewal), biannual/quarterly ongoing reports, and employer audit rights.
- Estimates must be backed by documented methodology; employers choose and the PBM pays for audit costs.
- Rule takes effect 60 days after Federal Register publication; applies to plan years beginning July 1, 2027 or later.

Pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs) that serve large self-insured health plans will have to give employers a detailed account of how they are paid under a rule the U.S. Department of Labor (DOL) finalized on October 29, 2026. Employers will also have the right to audit that information.

PBMs have time to prepare but should not wait. A PBM that wants to rely on the 90-day advance-notice benchmark for renewals will need its disclosures ready well before the rule applies.

Under the Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA), an employer’s plan can only contract with a PBM if the PBM complies with the new rule requiring certain disclosures about their compensation arrangements with drug manufacturers and pharmacies. The disclosures are also a likely source of evidence in future fiduciary-breach suits, so a PBM should expect its numbers to be read closely.

### Who Is Covered

The detailed requirements apply to PBMs contracting with large, self-insured group health plans. Generally, that means plans sponsored by employers with at least 100 employees, and certain other self-insured plans with at least 100 participants. Fully insured plans and group health plans sponsored by entities in the drug supply chain — including drug manufacturers, distributors, wholesalers, PBMs, rebate aggregators, and affiliated pharmacies — are excluded from these detailed requirements, but their service providers still have disclosure obligations under ERISA.

The requirements cover PBMs and PBM-affiliated advisers that reasonably expect at least $1,000 in annual compensation related to their work for the employer’s plan.

### What PBMs Must Deliver

The disclosures must cover manufacturer rebates, spread compensation (the amount by which the plan’s price for a drug exceeds the PBM’s reimbursement to the pharmacy), and pharmacy recoupments, such as copay claw-backs, along with manufacturer incentives that influence coverage and the net price of drugs by pharmacy channel after rebates, fees, and other remuneration. The disclosures also cover inflation protection or price protection agreements, including the amounts expected to be retained by the PBM or related parties and the amounts expected to be passed through to the plan.

The rule has four key obligations:

- Initial disclosures: PBMs must give written disclosures reasonably in advance of entering into, extending, or renewing a contract. For renewals and extensions, 90 days in advance is deemed sufficient.
- Ongoing disclosures: PBMs must report compensation they received or reasonably expect to receive within 30 days after each six-month period — or each quarter, if the employer requests quarterly reporting.
- Audit rights: Employers may audit their PBM at least annually.
- Format: Compensation generally must be stated as a monetary amount. Estimates are allowed when actual amounts are not reasonably ascertainable and must be based on good-faith assumptions.

### Where PBMs Face the Most Risk

**Estimates and methodology**. When a monetary amount is not ascertainable, the PBM must disclose its pricing methodology and an objective way to verify it. Assumptions without a sound explanation are where disputes are most likely to start.

**Audits.** During an audit, the PBM must produce records, claims, and transaction-level data, and contracts with retail pharmacies, drug manufacturers, and rebate aggregators, subject to reasonable confidentiality agreements. The plan has the sole right to choose the auditor. The plan pays the auditor, while the PBM bears the cost of producing the information, data, and materials needed for the audit.

**Client reaction**. Some employers will see spread compensation and net drug prices by pharmacy channel for the first time, and may use them to push for contract changes.

### What Happens When a PBM Falls Short

If a PBM fails to meet the disclosure or audit requirements, its employer client could face liability under ERISA’s existing prohibited-transaction rules. The rule protects responsible plan fiduciaries who did not know of the failure and reasonably believed the requirements were met. To keep that protection, the fiduciary must request correction, notify the DOL if the failure is not fixed within 90 days, and document whether continuing the contract is prudent.

### Key Dates and Next Steps

The rule is effective 60 days after publication in the *Federal Register* and applies to plan years beginning on or after July 1, 2027.

PBMs should use the lead time to:

- Compare what they disclose today with what the rule requires;
- Build systems to deliver initial and ongoing disclosures on time, including quarterly reports;
- Document the methods and assumptions behind any estimates;
- Set up an audit-response process for transaction-level data and third-party contracts;
- Update contract templates and renewal timelines to fit the 90-day benchmark; and
- Identify affiliated brokers, consultants, and other advisers, and confirm who is responsible for their disclosures.
