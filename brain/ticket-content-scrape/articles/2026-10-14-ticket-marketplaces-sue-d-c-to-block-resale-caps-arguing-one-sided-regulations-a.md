# Ticket Marketplaces Sue D.C. to Block Resale Caps, Arguing One-Sided Regulations are Unconstitutional

## Summary

The Ticket Policy Forum, SeatGeek, Vivid Seats, Gametime and TickPick sued Washington, D.C. on October 14 to block its RESALE Amendment Act, arguing the law's resale price cap (110% of original price), 10% marketplace fee cap, and original-price disclosure mandate are unconstitutional. The suit raises dormant Commerce Clause, Takings Clause, First Amendment, Section 230, and Fourth Amendment claims, and asks the court to invalidate the entire law — not just the price provisions — before it takes effect January 1, 2027. The law notably leaves primary-market pricing and fees entirely uncapped, prompting plaintiffs to argue it protects primary sellers (including Live Nation/Ticketmaster) at secondary marketplaces' expense; the law's backers, including NIVA, dispute that framing and point to resale listings averaging far above face value.

### Key Points
- RESALE Act (D.C. Law 26-181, effective Jan. 1, 2027): resale capped at 110% of original price, marketplace fees capped at 10% of original price, plus all-in pricing, refund-protection, anti-bot, and speculative-listing rules.
- Plaintiffs seek a preliminary injunction ruling by December 10, and ask the court to strike the law in its entirety (not severably), including its unrelated consumer-protection provisions.
- Key constitutional arguments: D.C. regulating out-of-district transactions (dormant Commerce Clause/Home Rule Act), fee caps below cost-of-service (Takings/due process), and compelled disclosure of unverifiable original prices (First Amendment).
- Primary-market pricing and fees are untouched by the law — fee caps apply only to secondary marketplaces, a point plaintiffs use to argue the law entrenches primary sellers' pricing power.
- I.M.P. (a NIVA-aligned promoter) reports $13.9M in advertised D.C.-venue resale listings across 111,000+ tickets in September, 92% above face value — though these are asking prices, not confirmed completed sales.

### Highlights
- Law takes effect January 1, 2027; court decision on preliminary injunction sought by December 10, 2026 — a live near-term deadline.
- Resale price cap set at 110% of original purchase price; marketplace fee cap at an additional 10% of original price.
- Suit targets the law's entire structure, including provisions (all-in pricing, anti-bot, speculative-listing bans) beyond the price caps themselves.

### Industry Problem
This is a direct test of the resale price-cap model (modeled on similar bills spreading to other jurisdictions) that leaves primary-market pricing and fees completely unregulated, while capping secondary-market fees in ways plaintiffs say don't cover compliance/verification costs (fraud guarantees, payment processing, original-price verification across platforms that don't share transaction data).

### Proposed Solution
No product fix is proposed by either side in this piece — it's litigation, not technology. For a primary platform like Etix, the signal to monitor is whether courts validate location-based resale regulation and uncapped primary pricing as the default model; if the RESALE Act or similar laws survive, that reinforces the structural advantage of being a primary-market platform with lower incremental regulatory exposure than a secondary marketplace, and strengthens the case for in-house resale/transfer tooling that can meet transparency requirements (original-price retention, all-in pricing) by design rather than after the fact.

The Ticket Policy Forum and four major ticket marketplaces filed a federal lawsuit Wednesday seeking to block enforcement of Washington, D.C.’s new ticket resale law, arguing that its restrictions on resale prices and marketplace fees are unconstitutional and threaten competition in the live entertainment ticketing industry.

The [40-page complaint](https://ticketpolicyforum.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/TPF-1014-Complaint-.pdf?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), filed October 14 in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia, names the Ticket Policy Forum (TPF), SeatGeek, Vivid Seats, Gametime and TickPick as plaintiffs.

The companies are challenging the District’s Restricting Egregious Scalping Against Live Entertainment (RESALE) Amendment Act, which limits covered ticket resale prices to 10% above the original purchase price and caps additional marketplace service fees at another 10%.

The plaintiffs have also filed a [motion for a preliminary injunction](https://ticketpolicyforum.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/TPF-Prelimary-Injuction-Brief.pdf?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), asking the court to prevent enforcement against them and TPF’s members before the restrictions become applicable January 1, 2027.

It also places one of the country’s most closely watched ticket resale laws at the center of a legal dispute with implications for similar legislation being pursued elsewhere.

## Resale Price Caps Face Constitutional Challenge

The RESALE Act became law September 25 as D.C. Law 26-181, following approval by the District Council, mayoral review and the required congressional review period.

Under the [enacted legislation](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/laws/26-181?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), a concert, theater or other covered live entertainment ticket generally cannot be resold for more than 110% of its original purchase price, including the fees and taxes paid in the initial transaction.

A secondary marketplace may charge an additional service fee of up to 10% of that same original total.

For example, a ticket initially purchased for $100, including fees and taxes, could be resold for a maximum of $110, with the marketplace permitted to charge an additional $10. The buyer’s total would therefore be capped at $120.

The restrictions do not apply to covered sporting events or movie screenings. More significantly for the ticketing industry, the law does not impose equivalent price or fee caps on initial ticket sales. Primary ticket sellers remain free to establish initial prices, employ demand-based pricing and charge fees without being subject to the resale-specific limits.

The law nevertheless includes several provisions applying more broadly to ticketing transactions, including all-in pricing, refund protections, restrictions on speculative ticket listings, ticket-transfer disclosures, anti-bot requirements and a prohibition on certain forms of surveillance-based pricing.

It also establishes licensing requirements for higher-volume resellers and a $25,000 surety bond requirement for sellers listing at least 50 tickets annually.

The lawsuit seeks to invalidate the law in its entirety, rather than simply removing the resale price and fee restrictions.

## Regulating Transactions Outside D.C.

One of the central legal arguments concerns the dormant Commerce Clause, a constitutional principle limiting state and local governments’ ability to interfere with interstate commerce.

The plaintiffs argue that D.C. is attempting to regulate transactions between buyers, sellers and marketplaces located entirely outside the District, based solely on where the underlying concert or event takes place. According to the complaint, fewer than 1% of the plaintiffs’ covered ticket transactions during the previous year involved both a seller and buyer located in D.C.

The marketplaces argue that the District consequently has no authority to dictate the terms of those transactions.

The lawsuit also invokes the D.C. Home Rule Act, which restricts the District Council’s legislative authority to matters applicable within the District.

The plaintiffs contend that imposing D.C.-specific pricing restrictions on transactions conducted elsewhere exceeds those limits.

Whether the location of the underlying event provides a sufficient connection for the District to regulate those transactions will be one of the issues for the court to determine.

The complaint separately argues that the legislation was designed partly to protect local economic interests from out-of-state competition, citing statements by councilmembers about redirecting ticketing revenue toward D.C. venues and businesses.

That allegation builds on the legislative history of the RESALE Act, which TicketNews has followed closely.

As [TicketNews reported in July](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/07/washington-d-c-passes-industry-backed-price-cap-bill-stripping-primary-oversight-in-last-minute-amendment/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), the Council’s Public Works and Operations Committee initially removed the fixed resale price cap in favor of broader regulatory authority that could have addressed pricing and fees across both primary and secondary markets. The Council subsequently restored the fixed resale cap and eliminated the remaining authority to regulate primary-market ticketing fees.

The lawsuit points to that sequence as evidence of lawmakers imposing economic restrictions on one segment of the ticketing industry while protecting another.

## Fee Caps and Original Ticket Prices Raise Additional Questions

The complaint’s second major constitutional argument concerns whether the District can impose marketplace service-fee limits without accounting for the costs of providing those services.

The plaintiffs argue that the 10% fee cap violates due process because it could require them to facilitate certain transactions at a loss.

Their filing offers the example of a ticket originally costing $40. Under the law, a marketplace could collect no more than $4 in service fees, regardless of the costs associated with processing payment, verifying tickets, supporting customers and providing buyer guarantees.

The companies contend that those costs can exceed the permitted fee, particularly on lower-priced tickets.

They argue that governments cannot impose confiscatory rates that prevent businesses from recovering their costs or earning a reasonable return.

A separate challenge addresses the requirement that secondary marketplaces disclose the original purchase price of every ticket they list.

The plaintiffs argue that they generally do not have access to reliable information about what the initial purchaser paid, particularly when tickets were purchased through another platform.

The complaint highlights several practical complications, including dynamically priced tickets, bundled purchases, complimentary tickets and digital tickets that do not carry original purchase-price information.

Although marketplaces could ask sellers to provide the original price, they argue that seller-supplied figures cannot necessarily be independently verified.

That creates two problems under the legislation: determining whether a listing complies with the resale price cap and publishing accurate original-price information for consumers.

The companies claim the disclosure requirement violates the First Amendment by compelling them to publish information about transactions in which they did not participate and whose accuracy they cannot establish.

The lawsuit also questions why lawmakers did not instead require primary ticket issuers, which possess the relevant transaction information, to transmit original purchase prices with the tickets themselves.

The practical problem extends beyond constitutional theory. The initial ticket price, controlled by an artist, promoter, venue or primary ticketing platform, becomes the legally mandated benchmark for what an independent marketplace may subsequently charge.

But the law does not require the entity establishing that benchmark to provide the information necessary for competing marketplaces to verify it.

## Lawsuit Seeks to Invalidate Entire RESALE Act

The legal challenge goes substantially further than the three principal constitutional arguments emphasized in the plaintiffs’ preliminary injunction memorandum.

The complaint contains eight counts, including additional claims involving the Fifth Amendment’s Takings Clause and equal-protection principles, federal protections under Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act, and Fourth Amendment restrictions on government access to private business records.

The Section 230 challenge argues that the District cannot impose certain forms of liability on online marketplaces for ticket listings and pricing information supplied by third-party sellers.

The Fourth Amendment claim challenges requirements that marketplaces retain and disclose transaction records to District regulators.

The plaintiffs also argue that provisions requiring exchanges to verify sellers’ licensing and bonding status create compliance obligations that may be impossible to satisfy because sellers can list tickets across multiple competing marketplaces.

Critically, the complaint asks the court to declare that the challenged provisions cannot be separated from the rest of the legislation, rendering the entire RESALE Act invalid.

That request could put consumer protections unrelated to resale price caps at issue, including measures addressing speculative ticketing, transparency and reseller accountability.

Whether any provisions would remain enforceable if the court struck down the price restrictions would require a separate legal determination.

## Dispute Highlights Primary and Secondary Market Divide

The lawsuit arrives amid a broader national fight over legislation intended to restrict ticket resale prices.

Supporters of the RESALE Act, including NIVA and the related “Fix the Tix” coalition, have argued that ticket markups undermine artist-established prices, disadvantage local venues and make live entertainment less affordable.

Councilmember Charles Allen, who introduced the legislation, described its passage as a victory for consumers and D.C.’s independent entertainment venues.

[NIVA](https://www.nivassoc.org/statements/2026/niva-and-fix-the-tix-applaud-the-dc-councils-final-passage-of-nation-leading-resale-ticketing-bill?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) [praised the law](https://www.nivassoc.org/statements/2026/niva-and-fix-the-tix-applaud-the-dc-councils-final-passage-of-nation-leading-resale-ticketing-bill?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) in July as a model for other jurisdictions seeking stronger ticket buyer protections.

The competing positions reflect a fundamental disagreement about how ticket prices are established and what government intervention will accomplish.

A resale cap can limit the prices charged for tickets that remain available through regulated marketplaces. The law also permits sellers to offer tickets for less than they originally paid.

Opponents argue that restricting marketplace fees and potential resale profits could reduce inventory available through those platforms, including lower-priced tickets, while encouraging some transactions to migrate to less-regulated channels.

They also contend that leaving primary ticket pricing unrestricted could strengthen the market position of companies controlling original ticket inventory and distribution.

That concern is particularly relevant in a market where Live Nation and Ticketmaster have faced extensive federal and state antitrust scrutiny.

The District itself has pursued antitrust claims against Live Nation and Ticketmaster, with D.C. Attorney General Brian Schwalb [praising a jury verdict earlier this year](https://oag.dc.gov/release/attorney-general-schwalb-issues-statement?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) finding that Live Nation had illegally monopolized parts of the live entertainment industry.

Nevertheless, the District’s RESALE Act leaves primary-market ticket prices and fees outside its direct price controls.

Brian Berry, executive director of the Ticket Policy Forum, said the legislation risks weakening independent ticketing businesses without addressing the broader pricing practices of the primary market.

“Under the guise of affordability, the District enacted a law that will drive ticket sales into the shadows,” Berry said in a statement announcing the lawsuit.

He argued that the restrictions could expose consumers to additional fraud while making it harder for independent marketplaces to compete.

The marketplaces’ predictions about reduced competition, declining ticket supply and increased fraud have not been established by a court, and the District has not yet presented its defense in the filings reviewed.

## Allen, NIVA President Defend Resale Restrictions Against Legal Challenge

In a statement shared with TicketNews Wednesday, Allen dismissed the lawsuit, accusing the marketplaces challenging the legislation of profiting from inflated ticket prices at the expense of local consumers.

“I introduced this law because companies like the ones behind this lawsuit are gouging DC residents using technology to scoop up as many live music tickets as they can and resell them at inflated prices – a scam that’s been extremely lucrative for them for years,” Allen said.

“Under my bill, that show is over. However, it’s not surprising to see them try for an encore to preserve these enormous profits and the status quo.”

Schaefer offered an even sharper response to TicketNews.

“Just because you, your stockholders and the parasitic scalpers you profit off of don’t like a law, it doesn’t mean it’s not valid,” Schaefer said. “The DC Council voted unanimously to protect its citizens and homegrown businesses from the predatory gouging by scalpers who siphon millions each year out of consumers’ pockets and the DC tax base.”

Schaefer has been a prominent figure in NIVA and the allied Fix the Tix coalition’s efforts to advance restrictions on ticket resale, including price caps. Her involvement in the RESALE Act extends to the legislation’s origins. As [Washingtonian reported in July](https://washingtonian.com/2026/07/15/dc-just-passed-a-bill-to-protect-you-from-ticket-scalpers), Schaefer originally approached Allen about the legislation. She has also been active in efforts to enact similar restrictions in Maryland, where she testified in support of legislation targeting ticket resale practices.

In March 2024, [TicketNews examined Schaefer’s testimony before Maryland lawmakers](https://www.ticketnews.com/2024/03/ticketnetwork-slams-lobbyist-misleading-resale-scapegoat-ploy/) in support of a price cap bill in the legislature, in which she cited extreme asking prices on independent resale marketplaces as evidence of the need for restrictions. That reporting compared the advertised asking prices with transaction data provided by TicketNetwork and dynamically priced primary tickets available through Ticketmaster, highlighting the distinction between extreme resale listings and the prices consumers actually pay.

Responding to the new lawsuit, Schaefer characterized the challenge as another effort by resale companies to protect their financial interests. She argued that ticket resale markups divert spending away from local venues, their employees and surrounding businesses, sharing figures regarding ticket resale listings associated with I.M.P. venues.

“Last month alone, I.M.P. tracked $13.9 million worth of listings on resale platforms for our venues, across more than 111,000 tickets; 92% of those tickets were listed above face value,” Schaefer said.

The figures describe tickets advertised for resale rather than completed transactions, meaning they do not establish how many tickets actually sold above their original purchase prices or how much consumers ultimately paid. It is also unclear if the figures include the same tickets listed across multiple marketplaces or how original purchase prices were determined for the comparisons.

Schaefer characterized the RESALE Act as a victory for local concertgoers and businesses, arguing that ticket resale companies had unsuccessfully fought the legislation before the D.C. Council and were now seeking to overturn it through litigation.

Both responses focused primarily on the economic and consumer-protection rationale for the legislation rather than the specific constitutional questions raised in the complaint. Neither statement directly addressed the plaintiffs’ concerns about regulating transactions outside D.C., whether the marketplace service-fee limits would permit companies to recover their costs, or how resale platforms could reliably verify original purchase prices without access to primary-market transaction data.

The District has not yet presented its legal response to the lawsuit.

## Court Decision Sought by December 10

The plaintiffs are seeking an expedited ruling on their preliminary injunction motion before the RESALE Act becomes applicable January 1. Their motion specifically requests a decision by December 10, providing time to pursue an appeal if the court rejects their application.

The immediate injunction request would prevent enforcement against the named plaintiffs and Ticket Policy Forum members while the broader litigation proceeds. TPF’s membership also includes StubHub and Events Ticket Center, although neither company is individually named as a plaintiff in the complaint.

If the court declines to block enforcement, covered marketplaces would face the prospect of implementing the new restrictions while continuing to litigate their constitutionality.
