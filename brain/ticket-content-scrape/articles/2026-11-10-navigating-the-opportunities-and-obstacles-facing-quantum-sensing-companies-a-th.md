### Part 1: The Quantum Sensing Landscape, Funding Options, and Market Applications

#### Background on Quantum Sensing Technologies

Quantum sensing technologies enable highly reliable positioning, navigation, and timing (PNT) systems and are well positioned to augment and, in some cases, ultimately replace traditional radar and GPS. In 2024, there were more than 240,000 reported incidents of GPS interference, a figure that has steadily risen in recent years and that presents significant national security concerns. These incidents can be mitigated by deploying quantum sensing systems that employ magnetic navigation frameworks that are more resistant to jamming and spoofing tactics than conventional GPS. Quantum sensing technologies are also well suited to operate in environments where GPS struggles to provide PNT information (*e.g.*, indoors, underground, and underwater), offering a clear advantage for operations in these settings and enabling continuous, resilient PNT in contested or GPS-denied environments.<sup>1</sup> Investors are becoming increasingly attracted to quantum sensing technologies specifically for their national security and dual-use applications and their ability to leverage existing photonics, semiconductor, and radio frequency (RF) hardware, thus lowering capital needs and time-to-market.<sup>2</sup>

Quantum sensing technologies also intersect with traditional radar systems and Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) applications. These subsets of quantum sensing, quantum radar and LiDAR, focus on measuring an object’s properties, such as speed and range, rather than on broader PNT capabilities and are being developed to exploit quantum entanglement and surpass the precision of conventional systems. Early prototypes have shown potential in defense, aerospace, autonomous systems, and industrial sensing.

For venture capital investors, these dynamics make quantum sensing a particularly attractive entry point into the broader quantum technology ecosystem, which has already experienced a surge in venture investment in recent years (up more than sixfold in 2025 from 2024).<sup>3</sup> Specific to quantum sensing, the combination of government-backed demand for resilient PNT and advanced sensing, near-term commercialization timelines, and strong potential for defensible intellectual property supports a thesis that quantum sensing companies can scale revenue on timelines consistent with venture-backed growth. Companies that successfully translate laboratory advances in quantum PNT, radar, and LiDAR into ruggedized, certifiable products are positioned to win large defense and industrial contracts, access nondilutive public funding, and build durable competitive moats in markets characterized by high technical complexity, regulatory barriers, and lengthy customer relationships. These are features that align well with venture-backed growth and long-term value creation. And the evidence is already there; in recent years, venture capital investment in quantum technologies has surged. Although much of the attention and investment has centered on quantum computing to date, investors are increasingly attracted to quantum sensing technologies because of their broad national security and dual-use applications and their comparatively faster, more incremental path to commercialization relative to quantum computing technologies.

#### Funding Options and Market Applications

Quantum sensing companies have multiple funding avenues, including the nondilutive [SBIR and STTR](https://www.sbir.gov/) programs,<sup>4</sup> which are a cornerstone source of early-stage, nondilutive capital for deep-tech and defense-focused ventures. Overseen by the Small Business Administration (SBA) and administered by various federal agencies, such as the Department of Defense/War (DoD/W) and Department of Energy, these programs support early-stage companies in the R&D phase and help bridge them to commercialization by funding high-risk technical work, generating government-validated milestones, and signaling technical credibility to private investors. The SBIR/STTR programs’ funding is structured in three phases, creating a staged on-ramp from concept validation to commercial deployment:

1. Phase I – Proof of Concept: Intended to establish technical merit, feasibility, and commercial potential of proposed federal R&D and technology, and to assess the small business’s performance before Phase II and verify readiness for further support. Phase I awards typically do not exceed $150,000 <sup>5</sup>; however, some agencies such as the National Institutes of Health (NIH) may issue Phase I awards over $300,000.<sup>6</sup> Phase I awards have short durations of typically six to 12 months.
2. Phase II – Technology Development: Aimed at continuing the Phase I R&D. Funding depends on Phase I results and the project’s scientific/technical merit and commercial potential. Generally, only Phase I awardees are eligible for Phase II; however, under the “Direct to Phase II” authority available in certain SBIR/STTR solicitations, a project that has already completed the equivalent of Phase I R&D (without prior SBIR/STTR funding) may be allowed to apply directly for Phase II funding, subject to the specific agency’s rules and requirements.<sup>7</sup> Phase II awards can be up to $2.1 million and have a two-year period of performance. In practice, Phase II can support systems engineering, field testing, and integration work that moves quantum sensing hardware from lab setups toward deployable systems, and it often coincides with or helps catalyze early venture rounds and strategic partnerships with defense primes or major industrial customers.
3. Phase III – Commercialization: Used to pursue commercialization of Phase I/II outcomes. Note, the SBIR/STTR program does not fund Phase III. Instead, agencies may offer follow-on, non-SBIR/STTR R&D or production contracts for products, processes, or services intended for U.S. government use. Phase III can take the form of multiyear procurement, pilot deployments, or follow-on development contracts, providing meaningful revenue visibility and a strong validation signal for private capital.

In addition to SBIR/STTR opportunities, the White House has made quantum innovation a policy priority, which may spur development and new government funding opportunities. Notably, the White House issued Executive Order 14413 on June 22, 2026. Among other items, the order tasked the DoD/W with identifying at least three next-generation quantum sensor projects to prioritize for fielding sensors by September 30, 2028, and mandated that various agencies develop a five-year plan for advancing quantum sensing and networking.<sup>8</sup> The next day, the Defense Innovation Unit announced plans to invest up to $200 million over the next year to accelerate the deployment of quantum sensing and timing technologies for intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance missions across the U.S. military.<sup>9</sup>

While public funding will continue to be significant in the quantum sensing sector, the private markets should not be overlooked. With a growing number of funds dedicated to quantum technologies and quantum sensing specifically, private investment into quantum sensing companies is pouring in. Compare the ~$220 million reported by PitchBook as invested into quantum sensing deals in 2025 to the ~$732 million invested already in 2026.<sup>10</sup> Regardless of where funds originate, the quantum sensing market is expected to continue growing: McKinsey & Company’s research predicts the global quantum market will grow to $60 to $100 billion by 2035, which nearly doubled its 2025 prediction.<sup>11</sup>

#### Broad-Based Commercial and Defense Applications

Quantum sensing technologies span broad-based commercial and national security applications. In the defense context, quantum sensors can enable stealth aircraft detection, missile detection, submarine detection, and underground facility mapping, while quantum-enhanced radar and LiDAR systems can improve target discrimination and performance in low-visibility conditions. In the commercial context, quantum sensing can advance biotech, life sciences, health care, infrastructure, mineral exploration, and PNT capabilities for logistics, transportation, and autonomous systems. These diverse applications present various business and legal implications, which are addressed in the remaining installments of this series.

We expect quantum sensing to continue attracting significant investment in coming years, and we encourage readers to reach out to the authors for a more detailed discussion of the topics addressed in this series.
