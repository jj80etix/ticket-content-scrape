*Breaking into the music industry is still no easy feat, even in a digital age where one catchy TikTok tune could get you in the door or even catapult you to stardom. But it was especially difficult in a social media-less world, with artists needing luck as well as some grit and grind to go out and introduce themselves any way they could, whether it was stapling flyers on telephone poles, convincing the local café manager to give you a gig or setting up a deal with your local record store to carry your album.*

*Some of those strategies may seem archaic these days, but not to MARIS. The 27-year-old DIY singer from Missoula, Montana, is still doing things the old-fashioned way, burning her music on CDs and doing the work at small venues to build her audience.*

*But listen to “Body Is On Fire,” “Give Me A Sign” or “The Fight,” and her approach makes sense. MARIS is somewhat of an old soul, exploring ’80s pop with a punk rock attitude that somehow feels fresh and in line with modern stars like Chappell Roan and Olivia Rodrigo.*

*Or maybe fans are charmed by her Bowie-esque face paint and Freddie Mercury-style performance onstage. MARIS isn’t paying homage; she’s creating an authentic persona so audacious that it emboldens her audience and makes them feel comfortable dancing the night away.*

*Without any backing from a major label, the Gen Z singer embarked on a fall headline tour that includes stops at The Altar at The Masquerade in Atlanta, Boston’s Brighton Music Hall, Encore at Uptown in Kansas City and Café Du Nord in San Francisco. She’s already sold out The Evening Muse in Charlotte, The Atlantis, MilkBoy Philadelphia, New York’s Mercury Lounge, Schubas Tavern in Chicago and The Echo in Los Angeles.*

*As she hit the road for September’s Sommo Festival in Prince Edward Island, MARIS took time to chat with* Pollstar *about her unique trajectory, her work creating queer-friendly spaces and her first EP,* MOSH★PIT*.*

***Pollstar*: How are you feeling going into the \[Sommo Festival\]?**  
**MARIS**: Festivals are always kind of funny. I can be a little bit of a perfectionist. So, they can be anxiety-inducing because you get one shot to do a show, whereas touring is if you fucked up on one show, you have the next night to fix it and make it better. But I’m excited just to play some of this new music. I’ve never been to this part of the world, and it’s just absolutely gorgeous out here.

**I get it. People forget that when you’re playing a festival and not a headliner, you’re playing to a crowd that’s likely there to see another act, and you have to win them over.**  
In a funny way, it feels like we’re opening for an artist. So, I’m just going to take it like that and think, “How do I get everybody riled up, feeling excited and feeling good right before the Twenty One Pilots set?”

**It can’t be difficult to get people riled up with your music. It’s catchy as hell. Congratulations on the EP, by the way. How do you feel finally getting that out to the world?**  
It’s exciting. This music is a really massive part of a period of my life where I was growing a lot, emotionally and mentally, and trying to figure out what I want my impact to be on the world and how I want to exist in the space that I’m in as an artist and also as a person. This music is really special to me because I feel like it documented a lot of that transitional time in my life and helped me come to a lot of conclusions and understand what I was going through a lot better. I love the music. I’m really proud of it. So, I hope that it’s received well, obviously, but at the same time, I’m just excited that I am going to get to play it every single night for a month and a half. I just love it.

**I heard about your journey, which is a bit unorthodox these days, especially for someone from Missoula your age. Is it true that you burned your music onto CDs and sold it at a record store?**  
Yes. I didn’t really know exactly how to get into the music industry because my mom is a real estate agent and my dad is a hunter and fisherman, and he has a radio show where they talk about what’s in season. I really had no idea what the music industry was or how to even get started, so I was determined to make an album by myself on GarageBand. I think I was 15 or 16 when I started that. Rockin Rudy’s is a record store in my hometown that’s always been a staple in my life, and Bruce, the owner, is a really wonderful guy. I took my CDs there that I had burned, and they sold them for me.

It was really formative. It’s kind of funny how everything feels so full circle because 10 years later, I’m burning CDs again to sell on tour. I’m doing vinyl for the first time, which is really exciting, but hand-burned CDs are just always going to be something special. There’s something charming about it.

**You’re based in Los Angeles now, which feels like a natural move given how you started your career. It’s really interesting how your trajectory is typical for artists before your time. Do you see yourself as an old soul?**  
I moved to New York in 2017 and then to Los Angeles in 2021. Honestly, I think it was just a lack of connections and understanding of the business. I think I was operating off an old manual of how to be an artist and make music. From my understanding, it was you record songs and put them on a CD and you try to sell that CD. I’m grateful for that journey and for that ignorance in a lot of ways because it’s taught me so much and also created this really strong bond with the fans. I would put CDs up on my Squarespace and take a bunch of them to the post office and ship them from there, but I would also do little tiny paintings with the CDs. I called it tiny art, and there are people who come to the shows, and they’re like, “I still have the tiny art that I bought 10 years ago.”

Maybe I am an old soul. Maybe it’s old-fashioned, but it was all a happy accident to that the fact that I didn’t know anybody who had ever done music in the industry.

**What’s the music scene like in Missoula?**  
A lot of country, folk and Americana. But there’s a pretty strong punk scene in Missoula.

**Maybe that’s where you get your aesthetic. It’s very Bowie and glam rock. And it suits your music. The melody has a playfulness and attitude that can resemble ’80s rock and Queen’s later era.**  
I’m a massive Queen fan.

**Who isn’t?**  
I mean, seriously. Freddie Mercury is my icon. He’s a Virgo, and I’m a Virgo, so I feel like we’re aligned. I am always thinking, “What would Freddie do in these kind of moments?,” when I’m questioning my artistic instincts. I also ask myself if I should be less bold, and typically the answer is no because, with what we’re trying to do, Freddie would lean in all the way.

The star that I paint around my eye originated in high school. I would do plastic sticker stars going down because I was like, “I’m full of so many emotions. Let’s just pull it out of the stars!” I used to make really emo music. And then I moved to New York and started playing rock shows with a drummer, a bassist and a guitarist with no tracks. I would do this with eyeliner and then I would smear the eyeliner. At the end of the show, I’d be crazy and look insane. I get Bowie a lot with the star on the eye, which there’s definitely some inspiration that I’m pulling from him, but more so I think I was inspired by KISS.

When I started making more pop music that was more colorful and vibrant and working with incredibly talented producers who are able to incorporate all these sounds that are super saturated and huge, I was like, “I need to fix the star to feel more pop.” What I love about KISS is that they just get right to the chorus. A lot of KISS songs, you get a little bit of the verse, but then it gets us right to the hook. That ability to put on makeup and step into this persona that’s larger than life has really helped me develop my stage persona.

**I find it fascinating how artists can have that switch. I’ve seen some footage of your performances, and you really have a unique way of commanding the stage.**  
There’s this person inside of me, and I think I would be a real pain in the ass and a real asshole if I was that person all the time. The process of getting into makeup and understanding the responsibility of a performer—I think people forget being a performer is like a public service job. Your job is to make people feel good, to open your heart up and open your body up and your mind and allow people to see themselves in you. If I were just trying to do that as myself, I’m not sure I’d have the same impact because, ultimately, my goal as a performer is to be bold and outrageous enough that it allows people who don’t get to be that in their everyday life to come to the show and be bold and get to put on the makeup and be outrageous and dance, scream, sing, cry and make new friends. The makeup helps.

MARIS performs on stage at the Trafalgar Square Show during Pride in London 2026 at Trafalgar Square on July 4, 2026 in London, England. (Photo by Jeff Spicer/Getty Images)

**It seems like you really want to provide an intimate, safe setting for your fans, especially those who are part of the LGBTQ+ community, which has really embraced you.**  
We’re in such a divisive time right now where it’s, I think, a massive cultural shift in so many different ways. There need to be spaces where people can just be themselves without having to worry about hate crime or being marginalized. We’ve come a long way as a society, but I think right now there’s this feeling of concern, especially with the attack on trans people and the media frenzy that comes with the clickbait economy. It’s just kind of a necessity when you’re making music and performing music to create a space for people to be themselves, no matter who they are—gay, trans, cis, whatever. I really want to have a space in my shows where it’s like, as long as you are respectful, you will be respected.

**Going back to your trajectory and approach, what are the pros and cons of entering the industry without backing from a major label or company?**  
It’s definitely taught me so much to have to do things on my own. I’m not going to say that I was immune to the pipe dream move of moving to a major city, signing a major label deal, and having my dreams come true. The longer that I’ve been in the industry and the farther along I’ve gotten, the more I’ve had this moment of realization of like, “Oh my God; these major label people that, for a long time, I craved for their approval—they’re just people. And a lot of times they’re wrong. They can have bad opinions and not know how to develop some artists.”

And it’s a tale as old as time where you see somebody that’s independent, and they sign to a major label, and then all of a sudden it’s like, who is this person? They’re completely detached from the person that originally signed up. I have pretty strong opinions about my music and what I like and don’t like, so it’s hard to sway me in that way. But I’ve been lucky with my situation. I’m distributed through The Orchard and Blackhouse. Having that label team has been a massive upgrade in my life compared to burning your own CDs and your own zines. We can do these fun giveaways and other things, and I’m not the one who has to handle all the administrative stuff.

But really, it’s my managers and me navigating the industry without that major label push and without that backing. For a long time, I felt rejected, like I don’t have what it takes. But the longer I did it, the more I felt like I could make my own destiny. And the most important thing is the fans. You can have a label putting $1 billion into you, but if you don’t know how to connect with people on a real level that makes them want to come see you live and makes them want to be invested emotionally, and they don’t feel like they have a safe space where they can be vulnerable with you, then I don’t really know if all the money in the world could be worth it.

**Talking about that connection, what are you hoping to convey with your tour?**  
*MOSH★PIT* is an EP that’s all about this time in my life when I felt really disconnected from my body and myself and what I was doing; and then coming back to myself and finding that homecoming through dance and music and having a mosh pit everywhere. I dance by myself in my room pretty much every day, at least twice a day, and I’ve done it since I was a little kid. I would get overstimulated and start acting like a bitch, and my family would be like, “Go dance about.” We called it dance about because all I needed was like 20 minutes to go to my room and run around and jump and dance, and then I’d feel better.

I was also raised competitively, but not in a bad way. I really view competition as a positive thing because it breeds innovation. So when I’m going out, I’m thinking, “How am I going to get people to spend their hard-earned money on a ticket and spend the night with me? What am I going to offer them that’s going to be different than what everybody else is offering?” That is really inspiring and exciting to me because it’s such a competitive game right now with so many people doing incredible things live.

For us, it was integrating what makes me drawn to a show. I’ve been training and working to be able to sing my fucking face off every single night, but it’s also about having a sense of humor. My favorite shows are shows where I’m screaming and dancing and having a good time and laughing and crying. I really want to touch on all of the different emotions that I’m trying to get from music. … My fans are so fucking funny. They bring something funny to the table for me to riff off of, so we’re integrating a lot of cute little bits, leaving the room for improvisation. I want it to be a space where it’s sweaty, fun, packed and no humiliation allowed. Right now, people don’t want to dance because they’re worried that they’re going to be filmed and made fun of online. I want to completely eliminate that from my shows. As an artist and front person of my band, I feel the need to take charge in making sure that it’s a space where everybody can express themselves and be themselves without the risk of being humiliated in any way.
