# State AG Oversight of Health Care Transactions: Key Takeaways From NAAG

## Summary

A NAAG Eastern Region panel examined how state attorneys general monitor health care market transactions amid hospital closures and physician consolidation, highlighting transaction-notification requirements and post-closing compliance oversight. Panelists cited Rhode Island AG Peter Neronha's rejection of a turnaround plan tied to a safety-net hospital sale as an example of AGs using approval conditions to preserve services.

### Key Points
- NAAG Eastern Region panel discussed state AG oversight of health care transactions.
- Focus areas: hospital closures, physician consolidation, rural health funding, fraud.
- Transaction-notification laws help AGs flag concerns before deals close.
- Rhode Island AG Neronha rejected a turnaround plan for two safety-net hospitals under sale conditions.
- Panel moderated by PA AG's Antitrust Section with GAO's Leslie V. Gordon.

## Transcript

Health care market pressures and the evolving role of state enforcement were the focus of a recent National Association of Attorneys General (NAAG) Eastern Region meeting. Tracy Wertz, chief deputy attorney general (AG) of the Antitrust Section in the Pennsylvania AG’s Office, moderated the discussion with Leslie V. Gordon, director at the U.S. Government Accountability Office. Together, they addressed hospital closures, physician consolidation, rural health funding, and health care fraud, with particular attention to how state attorneys general can monitor transactions and respond to changing market conditions.

Gordon highlighted several financial and operational pressures facing hospitals, including declining patient volumes, aging facilities, management challenges, and the costs of operating independently. The speakers noted that these pressures can ultimately lead to closures with significant community effects, particularly when closures reduce access to emergency, behavioral health, obstetric, dialysis, or cancer care.

Against that backdrop, the panel discussed how transaction notification requirements and coordination with legislators, licensing agencies, and other regulators can help state attorneys general identify potential concerns earlier and respond more effectively.

Recent transactions illustrate how state AGs may also use approval conditions and negotiated agreements to preserve services, require investment, and oversee compliance after closing. In Rhode Island, AG Peter Neronha [recently rejected](https://riag.ri.gov/press-releases/attorney-general-neronha-requires-changes-strategic-plan-roger-williams-and-fatima) a five-year turnaround plan submitted under conditions governing the sale of two safety-net hospitals and directed the owners to revise it. Minnesota’s AG similarly [secured](https://www.ag.state.mn.us/Office/Communications/2026/08/28_Sanford-NorthMemorial.asp) a 10-year oversight agreement in the Sanford Health–North Memorial Health transaction that includes investment and service commitments, ongoing reporting, and regular meetings with the AG’s office.

Beyond hospital transactions, the panel examined several forms of physician consolidation, including acquisitions, affiliations, management services organizations, and clinically integrated networks. The discussion highlighted the potential effects on negotiating leverage and prices, as well as the difficulty of monitoring smaller or serial transactions that may fall below federal reporting thresholds.

The discussion also addressed the federal [Rural Health Transformation Program](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-27-108853), including restrictions on the use of program funds and anticipated federal oversight. Turning to health care fraud, Gordon outlined the risks facing Medicare and the tools used to identify suspicious billing, such as predictive analytics, provider risk scoring, peer comparisons, and payment suspensions.

### Why It Matters

The discussion underscores that state AG oversight of health care transactions increasingly extends beyond whether a deal closes. Depending on their authority, state AGs may use transaction reviews, approval conditions, negotiated agreements, reporting requirements, and enforcement actions to preserve services and monitor compliance long after closing. Health care organizations should therefore expect scrutiny of their operational and capital plans, understand every state and local regulatory touchpoint, and be prepared to implement and document compliance with ongoing commitments.
