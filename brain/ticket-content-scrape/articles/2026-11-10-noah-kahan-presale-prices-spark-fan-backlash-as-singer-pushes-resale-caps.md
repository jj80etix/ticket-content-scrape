# Noah Kahan Presale Prices Spark Fan Backlash as Singer Pushes Resale Caps

## Summary

Noah Kahan fans are pushing back against presale prices of $300–$700+ for his 2027 "The Last of the Bugs" stadium tour, reviving scrutiny of Kahan's prominent advocacy for resale price caps (he backed Massachusetts' proposed Great Divide Act, capping resale at 110% of original price) even as his own primary prices run high. The tour uses Ticketmaster's Face Value Exchange, which locks resale at the original price and can't be lowered even if demand softens — illustrating again that resale caps constrain secondary-market markups but do nothing about how high primary face value is set in the first place.

### Key Points
- Presale tickets ranged roughly $80–$727 depending on market/section; some fans reported $300–$583 for standard and early-entry options
- Kahan publicly backed Massachusetts' proposed Great Divide Act (110% resale cap, fee restrictions); provision was later stripped from one budget bill but remains under negotiation
- Tour uses Ticketmaster's Face Value Exchange — resale price is fixed at original purchase price and cannot be adjusted down, unlike CashorTrade (also authorized) which allows below-face resale
- Critics (including promoter Tommy Dorfman, a long-time Live Nation antitrust critic) argue fans are "priced out before resale even enters the picture"
- Echoes a near-identical controversy from Kahan's 2023 tour (Platinum dynamic pricing + transfer restrictions)
- State laws (NY, IL, CO, VA, UT, CT) still guarantee some transfer rights regardless of exchange restrictions

### Highlights
- Same artist is simultaneously a public face for resale-cap legislation and the beneficiary of high, unrestricted primary pricing — a direct real-world test of the "resale caps don't fix affordability" critique
- Face Value Exchange's inability to lower resale price can trap a fan who overpaid with no way to recover cost if demand is weaker than expected
- Recurrence of the same complaint pattern across two tours (2023, 2027) suggests this isn't a one-off incident but a structural pricing approach

### Industry Problem
Artist-backed resale price caps are popular as consumer protection, but when primary pricing remains unrestricted and demand-sensitive (even without formal dynamic pricing), caps can leave fans worse off — unable to recoup cost via resale while still facing high face-value prices at onsale.

### Proposed Solution
AI-context/monitoring material on the resale-cap debate's limits; for Etix, reinforces that patron-facing pricing transparency and flexible (not just capped) resale tools are more defensible than caps alone, and that legislative momentum (Great Divide Act, Texas SB 42, etc.) around resale caps should be tracked as it could extend beyond Massachusetts.

## Resale Restrictions Face Scrutiny Amid High Primary Prices

Kahan’s ticket pricing controversy carries particular significance because of his prominent role in supporting legislation restricting ticket resale.

The Vermont-born artist has advocated for resale reforms in his home state and Massachusetts, arguing that restrictions on secondary sellers can protect fans from excessive markups and speculative ticket listings.

In July, Kahan joined Massachusetts Gov. Maura Healey in supporting the proposed Great Divide Act, legislation named after his album that would generally cap concert ticket resale prices at 110% of the original ticket price. The proposal would also restrict resale marketplace fees and prohibit certain deceptive sales practices.

“I love my fans and want to protect them however I can,” Kahan said in a [statement released by Healey’s office](https://www.mass.gov/news/governor-healey-takes-action-to-cut-concert-sports-ticket-resale-and-fees?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content).

He added that artists could not address what he described as manipulation by secondary resellers without additional government intervention.

The proposal drew support from other New England musicians and industry organizations but also generated concerns about its potential effect on competition in the ticketing marketplace.

As [TicketNews reported in July](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/07/new-england-artists-offer-clout-to-industry-backed-push-for-ticket-resale-cap-in-massachusetts/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), the legislation would restrict what independent resellers could charge without imposing comparable limits on prices set in the primary ticket market.

Concert promoter Tommy Dorfman, chief executive of Juice Entertainment and a longtime critic of Live Nation, also questioned the pricing and market imbalance in a social media post directed at Massachusetts Gov. Maura Healey.

“Fans are being priced out before resale even enters the picture,” Dorfman wrote, pointing to Tuesday’s complaints about Kahan’s ticket prices.

Dorfman has spent more than a decade pursuing litigation against Live Nation, alleging that the company interfered with his plans to produce an electronic dance music festival at the Meadowlands, including by threatening his access to artists and Ticketmaster ticketing services. Live Nation has disputed those allegations. As [TicketNews previously reported](https://www.ticketnews.com/2025/07/tommy-dorfman-says-live-nation-makes-the-mafia-look-like-gentlemen-urges-doj-to-press-for-full-break-up/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), Dorfman has publicly advocated breaking up Live Nation and Ticketmaster and, more recently, submitted Tunney Act comments opposing the Justice Department’s proposed antitrust settlement with the companies.

A resale price ceiling can prevent ticket holders and professional resellers from charging large premiums above the original price. It does not, however, prevent artists, promoters and their ticketing partners from setting that original price at $300, $400 or more.

The economic consequences extend beyond the initial purchase. Primary ticket sellers can potentially adjust prices or release differently priced inventory as an event approaches, responding to demand and the number of seats remaining.

Ticket holders subject to resale restrictions do not necessarily have the same flexibility.

For the 2027 tour, Kahan is using Ticketmaster’s Face Value Exchange, which restricts resale to the original purchase price and generally prohibits transfers where legally permitted.

Under [Ticketmaster’s published exchange rules](https://help.ticketmaster.com/hc/en-us/articles/9781464415249-How-does-Ticketmaster-s-Face-Value-Exchange-work?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), the resale listing price is automatically fixed at the amount originally paid, including applicable fees and taxes. Sellers cannot increase or decrease that price through the exchange.

That means a fan who purchases an expensive original ticket may be unable to lower the asking price on Ticketmaster’s exchange if demand weakens or the fan simply wants to recover part of the purchase cost.

There are qualifications to those restrictions. [Ticketmaster’s listing for the June 18 MetLife show](https://www.ticketmaster.com/noah-kahan-the-last-of-the-east-rutherford-new-jersey-06-18-2027/event/0000655BA74A5861?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) also authorizes CashorTrade for eligible resale transactions. Unlike Ticketmaster’s fixed-price exchange, [CashorTrade permits tickets to be listed below their original face value](https://help.cashortrade.org/hc/en-us/articles/12917535906445-What-is-considered-Face-Value?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content).

Additionally, state laws in jurisdictions including New York, Illinois, Colorado, Virginia, Utah and Connecticut limit the ability of ticket issuers to prohibit transfers. Ticketmaster’s own event terms acknowledge those exceptions.

Nevertheless, the restrictions substantially narrow the ways some ticket holders can resell their purchases compared with an unrestricted secondary marketplace.

Supporters of face-value resale limits argue that they reduce opportunities for professional scalpers to extract substantial profits from high-demand events and can help keep tickets available at their original prices. The restrictions also reflect artists’ interest in discouraging speculative purchasing and excessive markups.

The opposing concern is that restricting competition in resale does nothing to address how expensive the original ticket may be. It can also give ticket issuers greater influence over the terms on which tickets circulate after their initial sale.

## A Recurring Pricing Controversy

This is not the first time Kahan’s ticket sales have produced criticism over the relationship between original prices and resale restrictions.

In September 2023, [TicketNews documented similar fan complaints](https://www.ticketnews.com/2023/09/noah-kahan-fans-cry-foul-over-platinum-prices-while-restricting-transfer/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) during the presale for his *We’ll All Be Here Forever Tour*. At that time, fans objected to Ticketmaster’s Platinum pricing system, which allowed prices to fluctuate based on demand, while ticket-transfer restrictions were also being imposed.

Reported prices during that presale ranged from approximately $150 to more than $500, prompting fans to question why original ticket prices could rise even as the artist sought to restrict resale markups.

The current screenshots establish that some 2027 tickets carry similarly substantial prices, although they do not establish whether dynamic or Platinum pricing was enabled for this particular presale.

The broader ticketing-policy debate has also continued.

In October, the Massachusetts House [removed Healey’s proposed resale cap from one supplemental budget measure](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/10/massachusetts-house-ticket-resale-cap-consumer-concerns/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), although similar restrictions remained under negotiation through a separate legislative process.

The episode highlights an unresolved question in the effort to regulate concert ticket prices: whether restrictions focused on resale can meaningfully improve affordability when the original ticket price remains unrestricted.

For Kahan’s fans, that question is no longer hypothetical. Some are being asked to spend several hundred dollars on tickets before any independent reseller becomes involved.

The tour’s Reserved by Spotify presale is scheduled for Wednesday, November 11, at 9 a.m. local time for eligible subscribers, followed by the general public sale on Thursday, November 12, at noon local time. Those subsequent sales may provide a clearer picture of ticket availability and whether primary-market prices change after the initial presale.
