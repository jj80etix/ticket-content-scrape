# Knicks Pause Sales After ‘Fan First’ Ticket Prices Soar Above $1,000

## Summary

The New York Knicks halted individual-game ticket sales after a pricing "mistake" pushed some primary-market prices above $1,000 — including in the Fan First presale — with the average opening-night asking price reaching roughly $2,500. MSG Sports will roll prices back to last season's levels plus an average 12% increase before restarting sales, and will refund affected buyers the difference.

### Key Points
- Get-in price for the Oct 20 season opener vs. Philadelphia hit $1,408 in the Chase Fan First presale, still $1,251 after the public onsale.
- Front Office Sports reported Ticketmaster's minimum listed price reached ~$1,000 with an average opening-night price around $2,500; Ticketmaster said it "does not set prices" and pointed to the Knicks.
- MSG Sports has not explained whether the error stemmed from dynamic-pricing configuration, incorrect starting prices, or another cause.
- Fan First program was originally marketed (2024) as protecting verified fans from inflated secondary-market prices; this is the second high-profile pricing controversy tied to the program (after a postponed 2026 NBA Finals presale).
- Prices will reset to last season's levels plus ~12% before individual-game sales resume.

### Highlights
- Dynamic/error-driven primary pricing (not resale) produced the highest reported prices, undercutting the Fan First program's "fans first, brokers last" positioning.
- MSG is issuing refunds for the price difference to affected buyers rather than honoring the erroneous charges.

### Industry Problem
Patrons face confusion and mistrust when primary-market dynamic pricing produces unexplained, broker-level prices under a program explicitly marketed as fan-protective — blurring the line between legitimate demand-based pricing and a pricing error from the buyer's perspective.

### Proposed Solution
No specific technical fix is described by MSG beyond a manual price reset and refunds. Etix should note this as a cautionary case for any dynamic-pricing feature: guardrails (price-change caps, review thresholds before extreme jumps) and clear buyer-facing communication about pricing mechanics reduce the risk of a comparable "mistake" narrative.

The New York Knicks have halted individual-game ticket sales after [acknowledging a pricing “mistake”](https://apnews.com/article/knicks-ticket-sale-prices-be25d546aed2d1737d310e6c991b4efe) that sent some primary-market prices above $1,000 — including during the team’s Fan First presale — and pushed the average asking price for the defending NBA champions’ season opener to roughly $2,500.

Madison Square Garden Sports said Thursday that it would roll prices back to last season’s levels, followed by an average 12% increase, before restarting sales. Customers who already purchased individual tickets at the higher prices will receive refunds for the difference.

“Our intention was never to overcharge our loyal fans,” MSG Sports said.

The reversal came after a three-stage single-game onsale that began Monday with Chase cardholders participating in the Knicks’ Fan First program, expanded Tuesday to other Fan First members and opened to the general public Wednesday. The New York Post reported before the sale that the inventory would be dynamically priced in response to heightened demand following the Knicks’ first NBA championship in 53 years.

Prices were quickly soaring, reaching into the thousands in some instances.

TicketData reported that the get-in price for the Oct. 20 opener against the Philadelphia 76ers reached as high as $1,408 when tickets first became available to Chase Fan First members Monday. The get-in was $1,363 after the general public sale began Wednesday and remained at $1,251 on Thursday. That was higher than the get-in price for any Knicks regular-season game during the entire 2025-26 season.

[Front Office Sports separately reported that the minimum Ticketmaster price](https://frontofficesports.com/article/knicks-ticket-prices-overcharged-refunds-ticketmaster/) for the opener reached roughly $1,000 while the average opening-night price climbed to around $2,500. Ticketmaster told the publication that it “does not set prices” and referred questions about the listings back to the Knicks. MSG Sports declined to provide additional details about how the mistake occurred, how many purchases were affected or the total value of refunds.

The Knicks have not publicly explained whether the error resulted from the configuration of the dynamic-pricing system, incorrect starting prices or another issue. The team has said only that there was a “mistake in our pricing.”

The episode is particularly notable because of how MSG Sports has marketed its Fan First program.

When the Knicks and New York Rangers promoted the program in 2024, MSG Sports said it was intended to give verified fans early access while helping them avoid elevated prices on secondary marketplaces. COO Jamaal Lesane said at the time that the program was designed to “put our fans first and brokers last.”

[TicketNews reported then that the initiative was already drawing criticism from fans](https://www.ticketnews.com/2024/09/rangers-knicks-fans-rip-obscene-fans-first-program-prices/) over primary prices, including complaints about $200-plus upper-level tickets. Several fans argued that the distinction between expensive broker inventory and expensive face-value inventory meant little when the team itself was setting prices beyond what they were willing to pay.

Two years later, that tension has become even more visible.

The Knicks’ Fan First Chase presale produced an opening-night get-in above $1,400 this week before MSG ultimately concluded that its individual-game pricing needed to be corrected.

The opening-night matchup is an unusually hot regular-season event. The Knicks will raise their 2026 championship banner before facing a Philadelphia team headlined by LeBron James, Joel Embiid, Tyrese Maxey and newly acquired Jaylen Brown. Even before the September onsale, secondary-market tickets for the game were already trading at premium levels.

The episode also follows an unusually expensive postseason at Madison Square Garden.

In May, a [planned Fan First NBA Finals presale was postponed](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/05/knicks-fans-left-waiting-after-fan-first-nba-finals-presale-is-postponed-without-clear-explanation/) without a clear public explanation as the Knicks prepared for their first Finals appearance since 1999. At the same time, reported primary-market prices for some Finals inventory included $1,725 for an upper-level seat, more than $2,100 in the 200 level and more than $3,300 for some lower-bowl inventory.

Those Finals prices illustrated the same distinction that has resurfaced this week: while spectacular secondary-market listings frequently attract attention, face value itself can climb dramatically when teams and rights-holders price scarce inventory according to demand.

For now, Knicks buyers have no corrected primary price against which to compare the resale market. MSG says sales will restart with prices based on last season’s levels plus an average 12% increase, while anyone who purchased affected individual tickets will receive the difference back.

Once those tickets return to Ticketmaster, buyers will get a clearer picture of whether opening-night resale prices remain above the Knicks’ corrected face value — or whether a secondary market currently sitting around $1,200 begins competing with the team on price.
