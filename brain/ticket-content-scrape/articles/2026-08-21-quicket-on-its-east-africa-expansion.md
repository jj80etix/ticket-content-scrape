# Quicket on its East Africa Expansion

## Summary

Ticketmaster-owned Quicket has launched in Kenya and Uganda, extending its East African presence after nearly a decade of regional partnerships, bringing local payment integrations, digital ticketing, and distribution across Spotify, Google, Meta, Bandsintown, and Apple Music. In interview, Quicket and Ticketmaster South Africa leadership frame the expansion around organizer/fan pain points that persist globally: fast and trustworthy checkout, verifying real buyers over just sales volume, and entry scanning that holds up under crowd surges.

### Key Points
- Ticketmaster-owned Quicket officially launches in Kenya and Uganda, its first East African market entries after ~10 years of partner work in the region.
- Expansion bundles local payment integrations, digital tickets, and distribution across Spotify, Google, Meta, Bandsintown, and Apple Music.
- Quicket execs frame persistent organizer pain points as: fast/trusted checkout, knowing who is actually buying (vs. just ticket-sold counts), and scanning/entry that scales to crowd surges.
- Kenya and Uganda picked as "young, digitally connected markets with real entertainment [demand]" — a template for further African expansion.

### Highlights
- Launch markets: Kenya and Uganda, following ~10 years of regional partner work.
- Product bundle: local payments + anti-fake digital tickets + high-throughput entry scanning + multi-platform distribution (Spotify, Google, Meta, Bandsintown, Apple Music).
- Named client pain points: buyer verification/fraud, checkout speed, and entry-scan throughput at scale.

### Industry Problem
Organizers in fast-growing but underserved markets face the same core issues as anywhere: confidence that a ticket is genuine, visibility into who is actually attending (not just units sold), and entry technology that doesn't buckle under a crowd surge — problems that also apply directly to Etix's client base in any market.

### Proposed Solution
Quicket's response bundles anti-fraud digital ticketing, real buyer-identity signal for organizers, and scan-throughput-optimized entry into one packaged, localized (payments) rollout — a template Etix could benchmark for its own client-pain messaging (fraud confidence, buyer visibility, entry-scan performance) even outside new-geography expansion.

## Transcript

As the ticketing platform extends its footprint to Kenya and Uganda, bosses tell IQ about the thinking behind the move and how the two markets "chose themselves"

Ticketmaster-owned Quicket has officially launched in Kenya and Uganda, extending its East African presence after nearly a decade of working with partners in the region.

The expansion brings local payment integrations, digital ticketing, and event distribution across platforms including Spotify, Google, Meta, Bandsintown, and Apple Music to two of East Africa’s key live entertainment markets.

Speaking with John Masembe, business operations director at Quicket, and Justin Van Wyk, managing director of Ticketmaster South Africa, IQ explores the launch, what they are seeing across East Africa’s live market and what the expansion means for Quicket’s ambitions across the continent.

**Having worked with partners in East Africa for about a decade now, are there any learnings about specific challenges that organisers and fans encounter around ticketing, payments, fraud and audience management?  
**John Masembe: Ten years in and the fundamentals remain the same. Fans have always wanted the quickest, safest way to buy tickets, which today means on their phone, and the confidence that the ticket they’re buying is real.

Organisers want to know who is actually buying rather than just how many tickets are sold; that’s the difference between hoping a night goes well and knowing it will. Then there’s entry, where scanning has to hold up when a few thousand people arrive in the same twenty minutes.

Quicket was built for exactly that; local payment options, digital tickets that are impossible to fake, and scanning that keeps the queue moving. That means organisers can spend their time on the show itself, and fans spend theirs inside it.

**Why were Kenya and Uganda selected as the first markets for this expansion, and how do you see the two markets developing differently from one another?  
**John Masembe: To be honest, they chose themselves. Both markets are young, digitally connected markets with real entertainment scenes and audiences who will turn out for a good night. Spend an evening in Nairobi or Kampala and it’s so easy to immediately see: Afro-fusion and Afro-House nights, festivals, homegrown artists with whole communities built around their music.

The two won’t grow in the same way, and we’re not expecting them to. But both have the audiences and the digital-first habits that live entertainment runs on. Our job is to make sure the ticketing stays one step ahead of what the fans expect.

**Describing East Africa as one of the continent’s most exciting live entertainment markets, what are you seeing in terms of demand from audiences, the growth of local events and the increasing number of international artists touring the region?  
**John Masembe: Pleasantly surprised to see demand is even higher than we expected. There are more locally produced events, more East African artists touring neighbouring countries, and promoters working across borders in a way they simply weren’t a few years ago. Nairobi and Kampala have also become stops that international artists actively want, rather than ones they need persuading about.

The encouraging part is that everything around the events is catching up too. Money is going into venues – purpose-built rooms like BK Arena in Kigali, and a wave of upgrades is underway ahead of AFCON 2027.

**Quicket’s distribution through Spotify, Google, Meta, Bandsintown, and Apple Music is an interesting part of the proposition. How important is the convergence of music discovery and ticket purchasing becoming for the live business in East Africa?**  
John Masembe: The link between discovery and purchase is critical because it’s already how fans behave. Africa has the youngest and fastest-growing population in the world, so you’ve got this huge generation of fans discovering, sharing, and buying through their phones. Today, barely anyone sets out to look for a tour date. They hear a track, fall for the artist, and then want to be in the room. The less distance between those two moments, the better.

That’s why we’re focused on making sure official tickets are where discovery is already happening – on Spotify, Google, Meta, Bandsintown and Apple Music. Organisers are now able to reach people exactly where they are – scrolling on their phones – making the path from discovery to live even shorter.

**How significant are local payment systems such as M-Pesa, MTN Mobile Money, and Airtel Money in making ticketing scale in these markets?  
**John Masembe: They’re essential. If you want ticketing to scale, you have to let fans pay in the ways they already use every day. Integrating M-Pesa, MTN Mobile Money, and Airtel Money makes buying a ticket easier and more accessible, especially for people who don’t rely on traditional banking or international cards.  
For organisers, it also means faster, more secure digital payments and less dependence on cash.

**You also mentioned WhatsApp-based ticketing and AI-powered discovery as developments in the pipeline. What problems are you hoping these will solve for African organisers and audiences?  
**John Masembe: The key is meeting fans where they already are. In East Africa, people are highly mobile-first and WhatsApp is already part of everyday life, so we want to make discovering and buying tickets feel just as easy.

WhatsApp-based ticketing could allow fans to find an event, ask questions, pay and receive their ticket all in one familiar place. AI-powered discovery can then help fans cut through the noise and discover more events they love.

**What does the East African expansion mean for Quicket’s ambitions across the rest of the continent? Are there additional markets you’re also currently looking at?  
**Justin Van Wyk: Kenya and Uganda are the start. There is already an enormous amount of live entertainment happening across Africa that deserves better ticketing behind it – for fans, promoters, venues and artists alike.

Quicket was already built as a pan-African platform. What we add is scale, the technology and the global relationships to move faster than either of us could on our own.

**What do you think needs to change in East Africa’s live entertainment infrastructure for the region to support more international touring and major events?  
**Justin Van Wyk: A lot of that change is already happening. Across East Africa and the wider continent, new arenas, and stadia are opening, being announced or under construction, while existing venues are being upgraded.

That infrastructure can be a real catalyst. Better venues create the conditions for promoters and the wider industry to invest in people, technology and supporting services, build economies of scale and grow event tourism. That makes the region even more attractive for major international tours while creating a stronger, more sustainable ecosystem for local events and artists too.

**What does success look like for Quicket in Kenya and Uganda over the next three years, in terms of organisers, events, audiences or market share?  
**Justin Van Wyk: Three years out, I’d like organisers in places like Nairobi, Kampala and other great spots picking digital ticketing without thinking twice about it, and when something big goes on sale, we’re the first call.

Market share matters, but trust is the harder thing to earn. If an organiser has run five events with us and never once had to worry about fans getting their tickets, we’ve done our job.
