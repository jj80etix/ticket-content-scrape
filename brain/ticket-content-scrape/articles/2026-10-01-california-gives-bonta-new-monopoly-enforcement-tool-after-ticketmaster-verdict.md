# California Gives Bonta New Monopoly Enforcement Tool After Ticketmaster Verdict

## Summary

California Gov. Gavin Newsom signed the COMPETE Act (AB 1776), giving Attorney General Rob Bonta a new state-law monopolization claim that doesn't require two-party conduct, with Bonta's office explicitly citing April's federal antitrust verdict against Live Nation/Ticketmaster as justification. The law takes effect Jan. 1, 2027, is enforceable only by the AG or district attorneys (no private right of action after lobbying stripped it), and requires proof of "substantial market power." It doesn't alter the pending federal Live Nation case but adds a parallel state enforcement track as Bonta continues criticizing the DOJ's settlement as a "sweetheart deal."

### Key Points
- AB 1776 (COMPETE Act) creates a standalone single-firm monopolization claim under California's Cartwright Act, effective Jan. 1, 2027.
- Bonta's announcement directly invokes the April jury verdict against Live Nation/Ticketmaster as the law's real-world reference case.
- Enforcement limited to the AG/DAs only; private lawsuits were stripped from the bill after business-group opposition.
- Exempts small CA businesses (≤100 employees, ≤$10M avg. annual receipts).
- Comes as California continues separately challenging the adequacy of DOJ's federal settlement in the Live Nation case.

### Highlights
- Signed by Newsom; effective Jan. 1, 2027.
- Requires proving "substantial market power" — not a strict-liability standard.
- Bonta has openly framed state enforcement as filling gaps left by the federal DOJ settlement.

### Industry Problem
State AGs are building independent, state-law antitrust tools specifically because they view federal remedies (the DOJ settlement) as inadequate against dominant primary-ticketing platforms — meaning a primary platform now faces compounding legal exposure across multiple state regimes, not just one federal case.

### Proposed Solution
No product response is applicable here (this is a legal/regulatory development, not a feature gap). For Etix, the posture is monitor: track how aggressively Bonta's office uses this tool and whether other states copy the single-firm monopolization model, since it reshapes the competitive/legal risk calculus for dominant platforms and could open further opportunities for independent primary ticketing providers.

## Transcript

![California Attorney General Rob Bonta](https://www.ticketnews.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/bonta-speaking.webp)

California Attorney General Rob Bonta

California Attorney General Rob Bonta will enter 2027 with a new state-law tool for pursuing monopolization cases after Gov. Gavin Newsom signed legislation expanding California’s Cartwright Act to reach anticompetitive conduct by a single dominant company.

The change has an immediate connection to the live-event business. In announcing the law Wednesday, Bonta’s office specifically pointed to the April antitrust verdict against Live Nation and Ticketmaster as an example of California’s recent enforcement efforts, months after a coalition of state attorneys general convinced a federal jury that Ticketmaster illegally maintained monopoly power in primary ticketing markets.

Assembly Bill 1776, known as the [COMPETE Act,](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260AB1776) makes it unlawful under California law to monopolize or monopsonize, attempt to do so, or unlawfully maintain monopoly or monopsony power. The legislation addresses a gap in the century-old Cartwright Act, which had generally required anticompetitive conduct involving two or more parties rather than providing a standalone state-law claim against a single company.

“The COMPETE Act will arm my office with a new, important tool to go after anticompetitive conduct by a single firm,” Bonta said in announcing the signing.

## New State Path for Monopoly Cases

Under the previous framework, Bonta’s office said California prosecutors challenging alleged unilateral monopoly conduct often had to rely on federal antitrust law and bring those claims in federal court.

AB 1776 creates a California cause of action instead.

A government plaintiff will still have to allege and ultimately prove that a defendant possesses “substantial market power,” using either direct or indirect evidence. The statute also exempts qualifying independently owned California small businesses with no more than 100 employees and average annual gross receipts of $10 million or less during the preceding three years.

The legislation expressly says federal antitrust interpretations may be instructive when California courts consider state claims but are not controlling, reflecting longstanding differences between the Cartwright Act and the federal Sherman Act. Courts considering the new claims are directed to use the analytical framework outlined by the California Supreme Court in *In re Cipro Cases I & II* and to interpret California antitrust law in a manner that promotes competition.

Newsom accompanied his signature with a note cautioning against treating market power by itself as proof of illegal conduct. He said “substantial market power” should be considered a “necessary — but not sufficient — condition” for liability and warned against drawing legitimate superior products or business practices into the definition of anticompetitive behavior.

The governor said he expects Bonta, district attorneys and courts to apply the statute against clear wrongdoing without creating unnecessary uncertainty for legitimate businesses, while acknowledging that lawmakers may eventually need to further clarify the standards courts develop under the new law.

Those qualifications followed months of debate over how broadly California should depart from existing federal monopoly law.

Earlier versions of AB 1776 were substantially broader and contemplated private enforcement, while also using different standards for evaluating potentially anticompetitive conduct. The private right of action became a major point of opposition for the California Chamber of Commerce and other business interests, which argued it could expose companies to excessive litigation. That provision was ultimately stripped from the bill, while lawmakers added the “substantial market power” requirement and other limitations. Some organizations that originally supported the broader proposal subsequently criticized those changes as weakening the legislation.

## Ticketmaster Verdict Gives Law Immediate Industry Context

Bonta’s decision to highlight Live Nation and Ticketmaster in announcing the COMPETE Act places the law squarely within a broader state-versus-federal antitrust debate already playing out in the ticketing business.

In April, a [Manhattan federal jury found for the state plaintiffs on the antitrust claims submitted to it,](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/04/live-nation-and-ticketmaster-operate-as-an-illegal-monopoly-jury-finds-in-landmark-antitrust-trial/) including findings that Ticketmaster unlawfully maintained monopoly power in primary ticketing markets and that Live Nation unlawfully maintained monopoly power involving large amphitheaters. Jurors also found an unlawful tying arrangement involving concert-promotion services and access to Live Nation amphitheaters.

That [verdict remains under post-trial challenge](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/08/judge-probes-evidence-behind-live-nation-monopoly-verdict-leaves-ruling-pending/). Live Nation and Ticketmaster have asked U.S. District Judge Arun Subramanian to overturn portions of the decision or order a new trial, arguing that the states failed to establish the elements necessary to support the jury’s conclusions. Subramanian scrutinized those arguments during a July hearing but had not ruled on the companies’ request as of TicketNews’ most recent coverage.

AB 1776 does not change the law governing that pending federal case. Instead, its significance for ticketing lies in what California public enforcers will be able to do in future investigations and lawsuits without depending entirely on a federal Sherman Act monopolization claim.

That distinction is particularly relevant because Bonta has openly emphasized the role of state enforcement following the Justice Department’s decision to settle its portion of the Live Nation case during trial. In July, he [described DOJ’s proposed agreement as a “slap on the wrist sweetheart deal](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/07/california-ag-bonta-calls-dojs-live-nation-settlement-a-slap-on-the-wrist-sweetheart-deal/),” while California and other states continued litigating and later secured the jury verdict.

California also remains among the states challenging the adequacy of the federal settlement, while the state plaintiffs separately pursue remedies following their trial victory and Live Nation continues seeking to overturn the verdict.

Beginning Jan. 1, that broader enforcement strategy will include a California-law monopolization claim that did not previously exist. How much it ultimately differs from federal monopoly law will depend on the cases Bonta and local prosecutors choose to bring — and how California courts define the statute’s new “substantial market power” standard.
