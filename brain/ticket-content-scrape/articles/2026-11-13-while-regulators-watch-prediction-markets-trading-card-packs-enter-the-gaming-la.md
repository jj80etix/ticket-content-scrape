# While Regulators Watch Prediction Markets, Trading-Card ‘Packs’ Enter the Gaming-Law Spotlight

## Summary

A legal-industry piece flags that online trading-card "packs," livestream card "breaks," and "repacks" are drawing regulatory scrutiny similar to prediction markets, as regulators weigh whether these randomized-value retail products cross into unlicensed gambling. The products vary by model — some deliver a randomly assigned physical card, others let buyers resell instantly back to the platform — complicating a single legal classification.

### Key Points
- Three product models examined: digital packs revealing a physical card, livestream "breaks," and "repacks" of previously opened cards.
- Legal arguments both for (genuine merchandise delivered, disclosed odds, secondary market) and against treating these as ordinary retail sales are outlined.
- Positioned as the "next" category regulators may scrutinize after prediction markets.

Prediction markets may be attracting most of the gaming industry’s attention, but another fast-growing product category is beginning to raise familiar regulatory questions: online trading-card “packs,” breaks, and repacks.

The products vary considerably. Some platforms sell a digital pack that reveals a randomly assigned physical trading card. The customer can take delivery or, in some models, immediately sell the card back to the platform at its assessed market value. Livestream “breaks” take a different approach: multiple customers purchase teams, players, or other interests in a box of manufacturer-sealed cards, which a seller opens on camera and distributes according to predetermined rules. Repacks add another variation, with previously opened cards curated and resealed into new mystery products, often around the possibility of receiving a valuable “chase” card.

There are credible arguments that these products are lawful retail sales. Every customer may receive genuine physical merchandise; the possible outcomes and value ranges may be disclosed before purchase; and the cards may have an established secondary market. Unlike a conventional wager, the transaction can be characterized as the purchase of a collectible whose identity and market value are unknown at the time of sale, not a stake placed on an external event.

But product labels are not dispositive. Regulators and plaintiffs may focus instead on the economic substance of the transaction. The familiar lottery elements are readily alleged: the customer pays **consideration**, **chance** determines the card or allocation received, and the possibility of obtaining merchandise worth substantially more than the purchase price supplies the alleged **prize**. That theory becomes more plausible when marketing emphasizes “hits” or chase cards; customers are randomly assigned teams or players; the seller controls the contents of a repack; or the platform allows the customer to immediately convert the revealed card back into cash or wallet credit.

The product’s presentation matters as well. A transaction involving shipment of an ordinary manufacturer-sealed pack looks different from an animated reveal followed by an integrated buyback offer. A customer-selected team in a livestream break may present a different risk than a team assigned by a wheel or randomizer. And a repack assembled by an intermediary creates different questions from a factory-sealed product because the intermediary selects the cards, establishes the potential outcomes, and may possess information unavailable to purchasers.

California presents an unusually specific statutory issue. California Penal Code § 319.3 treats certain sports-trading-card “grab bag” games as lotteries. The statute covers paid, chance-based schemes involving sealed packages containing sports cards removed from their original manufacturer packaging, where purchasers understand that designated prizes appear in some, but not all, packages. That language may not reach an ordinary break involving original manufacturer-sealed products, but it could apply more directly to sports-card repacks marketed around identified chase cards. The statute excludes otherwise lawful sweepstakes and chance-based distributions, but that savings clause does not independently legalize a transaction that is unlawful under California’s other lottery or gambling laws.

These questions are now being tested in California. Consumers reportedly have filed dozens of arbitration demands alleging that randomized card breaks and repack breaks violate California lottery and gambling laws, the federal RICO statute, and state consumer-protection laws. Separate California litigation alleges that online marketplaces have facilitated unlicensed box-break lotteries and other randomized card products. The defendants dispute the allegations, and no court or arbitrator has ruled that the challenged products constitute unlawful gambling. Still, the proceedings have moved the legal risk from a hypothetical concern to an active dispute and may provide an early indication of how courts, arbitrators, and regulators will approach these emerging products.

The emerging debate follows a pattern seen with sweepstakes casinos and “DFS 2.0” pick’em contests. Each product developed around a colorable distinction from regulated gambling: sweepstakes casinos relied on promotional currencies and free entry, while pick’em operators argued that their contests qualified as fantasy sports or games of skill. But as the products grew and began to resemble familiar regulated offerings, legislatures, gaming agencies, attorneys general, and private plaintiffs looked past the labels and scrutinized how the products actually operated. Recent sweepstakes-casino measures have increasingly reached not only operators but also payment processors, financial institutions, platform providers, and other supporting businesses.

Trading-card platforms, advertisers, marketplaces, and payment providers therefore should not treat “packs” as a single product category. Risk should be calibrated to the specific mechanics. Relevant considerations include whether the product contains original or repackaged cards; whether customers choose or are randomly assigned their allocation; whether every customer receives meaningful physical merchandise; how floors, ceilings, odds, and chase cards are disclosed; whether casino-style animations or terminology are used; and whether the platform offers immediate buybacks, wallet credits, or repeat-play functionality. The jurisdictions in which each format is offered also matter, particularly where state statutes expressly address trading-card grab bags or apply an expansive definition of lottery or gambling.

Contracts and diligence can reflect those distinctions. Platforms should consider product-specific legal opinions, jurisdictional exclusions, substantiated valuation practices, transparent checklists and odds, fulfillment controls, complaint monitoring, and prompt notice of material changes. Advertisers and payment processors may similarly want representations addressing applicable gambling and consumer-protection laws, audit and information rights, tailored indemnification, and the ability to suspend higher-risk formats as litigation or enforcement develops.

None of this means every card pack, break, or repack is gambling. There are substantial factual and legal distinctions among the products, and the pending California claims remain unproven. But the history of sweepstakes casinos and pick’em contests suggests that a product can move quickly from innovation to an area of regulatory focus once its scale, presentation, and economics begin to resemble gaming. While much of the industry watches prediction markets, trading-card packs may be the next product category requiring a serious gaming-law analysis.
