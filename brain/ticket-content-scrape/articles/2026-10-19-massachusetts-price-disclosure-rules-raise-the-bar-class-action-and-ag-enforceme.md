# Massachusetts’ Price Disclosure Rules Raise the Bar: Class Action and AG Enforcement Risks in a Post-Click-to-Cancel World

## Summary

A client alert from Troutman Pepper Locke surveys the rapidly expanding state-level "junk fee" enforcement landscape, centered on Massachusetts' new Unfair and Deceptive Fees regulation (effective Sept. 2, 2025), which imposes stricter "clear and conspicuous" total-price and negative-option disclosure standards than the state's older retail-advertising rule. It uses pending drip-pricing class actions against the San Francisco Giants and Boston Red Sox -- both alleging mandatory ticket fees were hidden until final checkout -- as a case study for how regulators and plaintiffs will apply the new standard, after the Eighth Circuit's vacatur of the FTC's federal "Click to Cancel" rule left states as the primary enforcers.

### Key Points
- Massachusetts' Section 38.04 requires total price (incl. mandatory fees) to be shown at least as prominently as any other price, bars contradicting disclosures elsewhere on the page, and requires disclosures to be "unavoidable" online, multilingual where applicable, and simultaneous across visual/audio media.
- Section 38.05 imposes strict negative-option/trial-offer disclosure and cancellation-notice requirements (5-30 days advance notice, cancellation as easy as sign-up).
- Giants case: plaintiff paid $29 for $10 tickets after a $5.50 "convenience" fee and $3.50 "order" fee appeared only at final checkout; Giants are compelling arbitration and disputing the Honest Pricing Act's retroactivity and CLRA coverage; hearing was Oct. 1.
- Red Sox case: alleges a 5-minute checkout countdown timer combined with late-disclosed $0.50-$8.50 per-ticket and $7 order fees; Red Sox are compelling arbitration under MLB's Terms of Use.
- The Eighth Circuit's vacatur of the FTC's federal Negative Option ("Click to Cancel") Rule leaves states (MA, CA, MN) as primary enforcers; the FTC's separate 2025 Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees (16 C.F.R. §464) remains in effect and explicitly covers live-event tickets.
- AG enforcement requires no named plaintiff, class, or arbitration-clause analysis -- a materially lower bar than private litigation.

### Industry Problem
Ticket sellers face a fragmenting state-by-state patchwork of "all-in" pricing and fee-disclosure rules (MA, CA, MN, and more to come) that are stricter than older advertising standards and are now being tested directly against drip-pricing checkout flows (hidden per-ticket/order fees, countdown timers) at sports and live-entertainment sellers -- creating both AG enforcement and consumer class-action exposure, with ticketing-fee display already a proven litigation target.

### Proposed Solution
Businesses are advised to audit price displays, fee descriptions, and cancellation flows as a connected checkout experience across every state where they sell. For Etix, this is a concrete argument to front-load total price (incl. all mandatory fees) at first display rather than at final checkout, avoid countdown-timer-style pressure tactics, and treat state junk-fee compliance as a proactive differentiator versus the pricing practices at issue in the Giants/Red Sox litigation.

### I. Introduction: The Rise of State-Level Junk Fee Enforcement

The national conversation about so-called junk fees — charges hidden until the final stage of a consumer transaction — has accelerated dramatically over the past three years. Federal regulatory efforts have stalled or been struck down. Most notably, on July 8, 2025, just days before a scheduled compliance deadline, the Eighth Circuit vacated the Federal Trade Commission’s (FTC) sweeping amendment to its Negative Option Rule on procedural grounds.[\[1\]](#_ftn1)

With federal rulemaking constrained, states have become the primary regulators. California’s Honest Pricing Act took effect July 1, 2024; Minnesota’s comparable all-in pricing requirements followed January 1, 2025; and the Massachusetts attorney general’s (AG) Unfair and Deceptive Fees regulation took effect September 2, 2025. Meanwhile, consumer plaintiffs have filed putative class actions against Major League Baseball (MLB) clubs, including the San Francisco Giants and the Boston Red Sox, bringing junk fee litigation to the sports and live entertainment industries.

This article surveys the regulatory and enforcement landscape: the substantive requirements of the new Massachusetts regulation,[\[2\]](#_ftn2) with particular focus on its disclosure standards and their relationship to the AG’s longstanding retail advertising standard [\[3\]](#_ftn3); the posture of the Giants and Red Sox cases; the significance of the Eighth Circuit’s “Click to Cancel” Rule decision; and forward-looking enforcement and litigation risks for businesses likely to be scrutinized by regulators and state AGs.

### II. Massachusetts’ New Junk Fee Framework

The Massachusetts regulation establishes enforceable standards against unfair and deceptive fee practices in connection with the advertising, marketing, solicitation, and sale of products to Massachusetts consumers. Violations constitute unfair or deceptive acts or practices under the Massachusetts Consumer Protection Act [\[4\]](#_ftn4) meaning they are privately actionable and may also be pursued by the Massachusetts AG.

Section 38.04 bars sellers from misrepresenting or failing to disclose “clearly and conspicuously” the total price — the maximum amount a consumer must pay, including mandatory fees and charges other than government and shipping charges — at the initial and subsequent price presentations. Sellers must disclose the nature, purpose, and amount of all fees; identify optional or waivable fees and explain how to avoid them; and disclose the final transaction amount before sale. No pricing information may appear more prominently than the total price, except that the final transaction amount takes precedence at the final presentation.[\[5\]](#_ftn5)

Section 38.05 addresses recurring billing and trial offers. For any trial offer, sellers must “clearly and conspicuously” disclose in writing, before the consumer accepts, the financial obligations that may follow, the identity of all products to which those obligations attach, the cancellation mechanism, and the specific calendar dates by which cancellation must occur to avoid charges. For any product with a negative option feature — automatic renewals, continuity plans, and free-to-pay conversions —sellers must disclose in writing that recurring charges will apply, together with a “simple mechanism” for cancellation that must be at least as easy to use as the method by which the consumer initiated the subscription. For subscriptions exceeding 31 days, sellers must provide advance written notice, no more than 30 and no fewer than five calendar days before the cancellation deadline, disclosing the upcoming charge, the cancellation mechanism, and the relevant dates. Notice must be delivered through a medium substantially similar to that used to initiate the subscription, or through a commonly used medium affirmatively chosen by the consumer as their preferred contact method.

### III. The New Disclosure Standard

The existing Massachusetts retail advertising standard defines “clear and conspicuous” disclosure as a representation of sufficient size, color, contrast, or audibility to be readily noticed and understood by a reasonable person.[\[6\]](#_ftn6) It sets medium-specific floors: at least eight-point type, or bold six-point modifying type, for print; adequate volume and speed without distracting sounds for audio; and contrasting color with at least three seconds of screen time for video. This approach has long served as a baseline for multiple AG regulations.

By contrast, the new “clearly and conspicuously” definition imposes mandatory cross-medium and contextual requirements that are either broader than, or have no counterpart in, the earlier retail advertising regulation:

- **Simultaneous dual-medium disclosure:** In any communication using both visual and audible means, *e.g.,* a television advertisement, the disclosure must appear simultaneously in both the visual and audible portions, even if the underlying representation requiring the disclosure appears in only one medium.
- **“Unavoidable” online disclosures:** For any communication through an interactive electronic medium — such as a website or mobile application — the disclosure must be “unavoidable.” The older regulation offered only a “non-mandatory guideline” that disclosures be “accessible” before purchase completion.
- **Multilingual requirement:** Disclosures must appear in each language in which the primary representation requiring them appears.
- **Targeted audience provision:** Where a sales practice targets a specific audience, *e.g.,* children, older adults, the “ordinary consumers” comprehension standard must be satisfied for a general audience *and* the targeted group.
- **Anti-contradiction:** Disclosures must not be “contradicted or mitigated by, or inconsistent with, anything else in the communication.” This standard reaches any inconsistency anywhere in the communication and is thus broader than the pre-existing prohibition on asterisked footnotes that contradict the primary claim, which is limited to specific, set-apart disclosures.

A business whose fee disclosures comfortably cleared the older “clear and conspicuous” threshold by displaying fees in adequately sized type accessible via a hyperlink before checkout may violate the new requirement to “clearly and conspicuously” disclose total price and negative option features under the new regulation if those disclosures are not unavoidable, appear in only one medium in a dual-medium communication, or are undermined by surrounding visual elements that divert consumer attention.

### IV. The Giants and Red Sox Litigation: A Case Study in Drip Pricing Theories

The pending MLB junk fee class actions illustrate how plaintiffs are applying regulatory concepts to private litigation.

The complaint in the San Francisco Giants case alleges that the Giants violated California’s Consumers Legal Remedies Act (CLRA), Unfair Competition Law (UCL), and False Advertising Law (FAL) by advertising tickets at a “starting at” price and then disclosing mandatory service and processing fees at the final checkout page.[\[7\]](#_ftn7)

The plaintiff alleges that in March 2024, shortly before the California law took effect, he selected two $10 tickets but paid $29 after a $5.50 “convenience” fee and $3.50 “order” processing fee appeared at checkout. The Giants moved to compel individual arbitration and to dismiss, relying on their Ticket Terms and Conditions’ arbitration clause and class action waiver. They also argue that the Honest Pricing Act does not apply retroactively and baseball tickets are not “goods or services” under the CLRA. Argument is scheduled for October 1.

The Red Sox case involves analogous claims under the Massachusetts Consumer Protection Act and Rhode Island’s Deceptive Trade Practices Act.[\[8\]](#_ftn8) According to the putative class plaintiffs’ complaint, the Red Sox ticket online purchase screen displayed prices without mandatory fees during seat selection. Then, at the final checkout page, the website allegedly displayed “Per-Ticket Fees” (ranging from $0.50 to $8.50) and “Order Fees” (typically $7.00 per order regardless of ticket price or quantity) accompanied by a countdown timer of just five minutes within which consumers were required to enter payment information, review multiple sets of terms and conditions, and finalize their purchase. The Red Sox have moved to compel arbitration under Major League Baseball’s Terms of Use and alternatively to dismiss. A hearing has not been scheduled.

### V. The Eighth Circuit’s Negative Option Rule Vacatur and Its Implications

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Custom Communications, Inc. v. Federal Trade Commission* temporarily removed what would have been the most comprehensive federal framework for automatic renewals and negative option billing. The court did not reach whether the substantive requirements exceeded the FTC’s authority or were arbitrary and capricious. It held that the agency’s failure to conduct the preliminary regulatory analysis required by 15 U.S.C. § 57b-3(a)(1)(A) for a rule with projected annual effects exceeding $100 million required vacatur.[\[9\]](#_ftn9)

Consequently, the “separate consent” requirement under the FTC’s “Click to Cancel” Rule — which would have required that consent to automatic-renewal terms be obtained separately from any other portion of the transaction — did not take effect, providing meaningful compliance relief.

For companies doing business in Massachusetts, however, the vacatur changes little. California’s automatic renewal law, similarly, was amended (effective July 1, 2025) to impose tighter affirmative consent requirements and more rigorous cancellation procedures.

Indeed, the patchwork compliance challenge for national businesses continues with the potentially pending enactment of the District of Columbia’s Enhancing Consumer Protection Procedures Act of 2025. Without a single federal standard, companies must account for differing state rules on price presentation, renewal consent, notices, cancellation, and enforcement.

### VI. Forward-Looking Enforcement and Litigation Risk

Several elements of the Giants and Red Sox cases are addressed by the new Massachusetts regulation and signal where enforcement and class action activity may focus. Regulators and plaintiffs may examine whether the first displayed price includes every mandatory charge; optional fees are accurately identified with workable avoidance instructions; disclosures remain prominent across interfaces; and countdown timers or other design features impede a consumer’s understanding of the total cost. Subscription programs present additional risk when enrollment is easier than cancellation or reminder notices omit required information. Notably, an AG enforcement action for alleged violations typically requires no named plaintiff, no class representative, no arbitration agreement analysis, and no individualized proof of consumer reliance or injury.

### VII. Conclusion

The Giants and Red Sox class actions show that plaintiffs’ counsel are already litigating drip pricing theories, while the Massachusetts regulation gives the AG and private litigants more detailed standards for testing consumer-facing practices. Businesses operating in Massachusetts, California, Minnesota, and other jurisdictions with drip pricing restrictions should review advertising, price displays, fee descriptions, subscription enrollment, renewal notices, and cancellation procedures as a connected consumer experience. A documented, cross-functional audit can help reduce enforcement exposure and prepare businesses for additional state requirements.
