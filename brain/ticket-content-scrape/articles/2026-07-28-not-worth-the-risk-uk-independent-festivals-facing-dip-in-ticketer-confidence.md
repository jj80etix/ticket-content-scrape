# ‘Not worth the risk’: UK independent festivals facing dip in ticketer confidence

## Summary

UK ticketing companies are growing wary of working with independent festivals as promoters increasingly demand advance payments against ticket revenue to fund production costs, leaving ticketers exposed to refund liability when events cancel — a dynamic that boiled over in the See Tickets/Heritage Live cancellation dispute this year.

### Key Points
- More than 25 UK independent festivals have canceled in 2026 so far, tracking toward 2025's 43, per the Association of Independent Festivals (AIF).
- Ticketers are effectively becoming "the banks of the independent festival sector," fronting advances that aren't always recoverable.
- See Tickets said advances drawn by Heritage Live for three canceled festivals were not returned, leaving it to cover fan refunds itself.
- Ticket agents also absorb £10-£15 per chargeback regardless of merchant coverage; larger promoter-backed ticketers negotiate lower fees.
- AIF is lobbying for a Grassroots Music Festival Tax Relief to ease the financial pressure driving these advance demands.

### Highlights
- 25+ UK independent-festival cancellations in 2026 to date; See Tickets left covering refunds after a promoter's advance wasn't returned.
- AIF is pushing a Grassroots Music Festival Tax Relief as a structural fix.

### Industry Problem
Independent festivals, squeezed by post-pandemic/post-Brexit costs and thin margins, increasingly demand ticketing-company advances to fund production — pushing financial and refund risk onto ticket agents, who have no reliable way to recover advances when an event collapses, and who separately absorb chargeback costs that fall disproportionately on smaller/independent ticketers.

### Proposed Solution
The article surfaces no vendor fix beyond AIF's tax-relief advocacy; for a primary platform, this is a concrete signal to evaluate advance and cancellation-insurance terms and chargeback-fee structures before extending credit to financially fragile independent-festival clients, and to watch whether similar advance-recovery disputes spread to other promoters.

## ‘Not worth the risk’: UK independent festivals facing dip in ticketer confidence

Recent festival cancellations have left independent ticket firms wary of increasingly early drawdowns and refund liabilities

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/smush-webp/2026/07/fESTIVAL-1-scaled.jpg.webp)

Independent ticketing companies are becoming “increasingly cautious” about working with festivals that rely heavily on advance ticket revenue, UK sources tell *IQ,* amid growing concern that ticketing platforms are being left to shoulder more financial risk.

Sources in the ticketing sector saythat though there is a desire to work with independent festivals, increasingly, in the words of one ticketer, “the reward is not worth the risk”.

In conversation with *IQ,* sources emphasised they do not blame independent festivals and wish to support them, but lamented that supply chain conditions have forced independent festivals to be run with tighter margins and increased risk, in turn putting pressure on ticket firms.

This comes in a year that has seen the cancellation of more than 25 independent festivals in the UK, as recorded by the Association of Independent Festivals (AIF). This puts 2026 approximately on track to match the 43 cancellations seen in 2025, but considerably below the 78 recorded in 2024, according to AIF figures.

Ticketers’ growing concerns are largely around advance payments that promoters typically draw to fund the run-up to events, which comes out of the ticket proceeds collected by ticket agents. When approached by *IQ,* AIF CEO John Rostron affirmed that “many ticketing companies are noticing an increased demand from events to get more advances from their ticket sales”, adding: “Indeed, ticket companies have pretty much become the banks of the independent festival sector.”

These advances usually occur in periodic lump sums, though it is not uncommon for some contracts to incorporate weekly withdrawals of ticket proceeds from ticket agents by promoters.

One recent example is the case of [three Heritage Live festivals](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/07/heritage-live-cancels-three-uk-festival-events-citing-spiralling-costs/), which kicked off a [row between promoters and ticketers](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/07/row-erupts-over-heritage-live-cancellation-following-refund-offers/) over who owed fans refunds. In that case, See Tickets, who did not speak to  *IQ* for this story, claimed that advance payments drawn by promoters to fund the event had not been returned, leaving the company out of pocket when issuing refunds.

> “the ideal situation is looking for a promoter that doesn’t want an advance”

However, Rostron said that “meeting demands for supply chain payment terms is part of the challenge for independents”. He added that in the view of the AIF, it is “reasonable that for most established festivals they should be making deposits prior to the show with balance payments either made during the event or on the first day post-event when the festival has much needed income from onsite spend to distribute, as well as final release ticket income”.

However, ticketing sources say festivals are increasingly paying off the bulk of their fees after the event has taken place, leading to worries among ticket agents that promoters could be paying off costs from a previous year’s event with their advances.

Rostron conceded that for some festivals legacy debt “remains an issue”, particularly events which survived through the pandemic. “Generally, demanding money earlier from those struggling makes things worse, not better, as festivals take loans on high interest rates, which piles on pressure and is money wasted leaving the festival ecosystem,” he said.

One ticketer said that the ideal situation for companies in the sector is “looking for a promoter that doesn’t want an advance”, though they added that this is “very rare”, while raising concerns that Heritage Live’s case could happen again in future. One source said Heritage Live’s cancellation exploited a “loophole” and has shown other events “how to get away with it”.

In the case of festival cancellations, ticket companies also typically pay between £10-£15 per chargeback through their card merchants*,* meaning that even when merchants cover the face value of the purchase, ticket agents still lose out. Meanwhile, *IQ* understands that ticketers backed by large promoting business can typically negotiate lower chargeback fees due to their greater cashflow, which sees large players impacted far less by chargebacks.

Cancellation insurance has also become more difficult to secure in the years since the pandemic, sources say, although most policies will only reimburse ticket agents for cancellations caused by weather or other factors out of organisers’ control.

> “it’s in the interest of the ticketing firms to work with festivals and the industry to push back on supply chain terms”

Responding to ticketers’ concerns, AIF’s Rostron said: “Rising costs, coupled with tight margins, make promoting a festival incredibly costly and risky. For independent festivals, which make up the majority of events in the UK, there’s then the added pressure of trying to finance the show, something the major corporations or private equity-backed events don’t have to sweat over”.

“It’s probably in the interest of the ticketing firms to work with festivals and the industry to push back on supply chain terms that were understandably onerous post-pandemic and post-Brexit when the whole sector had been hit, but now need to ease if the industry wants the independent festival sector to thrive,” Rostron said, adding that “some of the \[supply chain\] terms we hear of are wild and some basic best practice would be of huge help to the sector”.

The AIF CEO told *IQ: “* Whilst the industry can take action here to support the independents, AIF is also advocating for a Grassroots Music Festival Tax Relief. A creative relief on festivals below 20,000 capacity won’t make running a festival any less risky, but it will create some of the financial gap that mitigates closures and postponements and also helps gives festivals breathing space.

“We want to try and remove some of that jeopardy, support established festivals and help the many new micro-festivals who have been starting up in recent years to thrive and survive.”
