# Latam’s cultural export fuels La Sordera’s cross-border growth

## Summary

IQ profiles La Sordera, a promoter founded in 2016 to serve Venezuelan expat audiences in Chile, which has grown into a multinational operation producing 500+ shows across Europe and Latin America and now advises artists, labels and management teams on cross-border logistics, venue routing and tax. Its principals say scaling faster than their in-house finance/tax knowledge has been the company's biggest operating challenge, particularly differing withholding-tax treatment between Latin American and European markets. The piece ties the growth to the broader crossover of Spanish-language music into Europe (Bad Bunny, Shakira).

### Highlights
- La Sordera now operates offices in Chile, Peru, Argentina, Spain, the US, Colombia, Venezuela, the UK, France and Portugal.
- Self-described gap: business/finance/tax knowledge growth has lagged the company's own growth rate ("we're self-taught in business and finance").
- Cross-border withholding-tax treatment cited as a recurring friction point between Latin American artists and European markets.
- Growth linked to mainstream crossover of Latin acts (Bad Bunny, Shakira) driving European demand for the wider Latin catalog.

### Industry Problem
Mid-size international promoters scaling across regions faster than their internal tax/compliance/market-entry expertise face real operational friction — a peer-company pain point distinct from patron-facing issues.

### Proposed Solution
No product fix is proposed in the piece; the company addresses it today via local offices and outside business-management help. Worth monitoring as a peer-services gap — a primary platform with existing multi-market operations could in principle package that expertise (tax handling, entity setup) for touring/promoter partners.

## Latam’s cultural export fuels La Sordera’s cross-border growth

Its principals unpack the promoter's evolving business strategy, from servicing Venezuelan expats a decade ago to European expansion today

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/©-La-Sordera.png)

© La Sordera

When La Sordera began promoting shows for Venezuelan expats in Chile a decade ago, its business was rooted in connecting communities.

That purpose remains today, its principals tell *IQ,* though the operation has scaled to produce and promote more than 500 shows across Europe and Latin America, building a multinational business that helps artists, management, and touring teams navigate the complex trade between the two regions.

The company’s evolution mirrors the broader rise of Latin music over the past 10 years, as demand for Spanish-language artists has rapidly accelerated in Europe and beyond, making the business of exporting and importing talent a complex operation.

La Sordera was founded in 2016 as Venezuela was undergoing political and economic challenges that led to an exodus from the country. The company’s origin stemmed from connecting Venezuelan expats through cultural ties. At home, the Venezuelan government had cracked down on international talent, the team says, contributing to the rise of domestic acts and stand-up comedians.

“We had about 10 years of only listening to our own music, and being deprived of international talent live,” marketing director Adriana Jimenez tells *IQ*. “So in other markets, Venezuelans got to feel this euphoric sensation of going to watch a Venezuelan artist with other Venezuelans. It was a nostalgic, solidarity moment with those shows. Everybody cried.”

That shared experience was not one confined to Chile. La Sordera quickly expanded to promote shows, like that of legendary Venezuelan artist Canserbero, and serve audiences across the region, and then further afield, initially connecting Latin communities with the continent’s festivals and venues by exporting Venezuelan talent like Rawayana to Spanish festivals.

But moving artists between Latin America and Europe is not as straightforward as a shared language may suggest. “Even though we talk the same language, it’s not the same culture,” Jimenez says.

> “The marketing strategies have to be quite different because the market in Europe is so big”

On a cultural level, La Sordera’s general director Francisco José Méndez says the difference is due in part to market structure. While Latin audiences in Europe can constitute a broad, diverse consumer base, audiences within individual South American markets are typically more concentrated.

“When you go to Chile, you only develop for the Chileans, or maybe some expat communities, but the size of those is lower than in Europe,” Méndez says. “Plus, the marketing strategies have to be quite different because the market in Europe is so big.”

That complexity has helped push La Sordera beyond traditional promotion. Deploying its learned knowledge, the company developed into an end-to-end provider and consultant for agents, labels, and management teams, helping them navigate cross-border logistics, local taxes, venue routing, and market-entry strategies in key European and Latam venues.

Méndez points to differences in withholding tax as one of the key differences between the markets.

“Sometimes, the Latin artists don’t understand having to pay withholding tax or the benefits, because it’s not very similar in South America. One of the things that has benefited us is having offices in Chile, Peru, Argentina, Spain, and the US, to help provide business management services, so we can advise and help save money on taxes,” he says.

In addition, the company also has offices in Colombia, Venezuela, the UK, France, and Portugal. That network gives La Sordera a local presence across an increasingly connected network of markets, but Méndez says its rapid growth has come with its own set of operational challenges.

> “Because of Bad Bunny and Shakira, people are a bit more curious about other Latin artists”

“La Sordera has grown so fast that for us, it’s been hard to keep up our knowledge about management, finance, and taxes. We’ve been successful, but not without mistakes,” he says. “We’re self-taught in business and finance, and the challenge is like we’re growing so fast that our knowledge is having to grow as fast as the rest of the company.”

That learning curve is unfolding against a backdrop where Latin and Spanish-language talent is increasingly in demand. La Sordera sits at a unique intersection of that growth, with its business model built around moving artists and audiences between two culturally interconnected markets.

Spain was a natural entry point for the company’s European expansion, but does not represent the final destination for the company’s growth on the continent.

“Because of Bad Bunny and Shakira bringing Spanish music to the masses, people are a bit more curious about what other Latin artists there are,” says Jimenez. And in Europe, the expat communities are “ambassadors for their culture, music, and artists with a global identity,” she says.

That kind of cultural exchange has also become part of a wider conversation around Latin American identity in Europe. Ahead of her 12-night [Madrid residency](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/09/shakira-launches-madrid-residency-with-cultural-takeover/), Shakira penned an op-ed with a similar message, framing the Spanish capital as a city where Latin culture and immigration are ‘celebrated’.

> “How are we going to take this moment to work together?”

“The most powerful response to fear is to exist with all the strength, all the joy, and all the dignity in the world. And for that celebration, I chose Madrid,” she wrote in Spanish newspaper [*El País*](https://elpais.com/gente/2026-08-31/en-madrid-somos-todos-latinos.html). “Because something happens here that the world deserves to see: Latin American immigration isn’t tolerated; it’s celebrated. Spain has embraced it, and that embrace has made it younger, more vibrant, more creative.”

For La Sordera, that momentum is becoming a broader business opportunity. The company is actively expanding into markets including Berlin and Paris, with plans to export more Venezuelan and Latin talent while adapting to new trends and the rapid growth of its own business.

Ten years on, this year is described as a turning point for the outfit, which has upcoming shows with Venezuelan comedian Professor Briceño, Uruguayan artist Cardellino, Spanish singer Chiara Oliver, and Venezuelan duo LAGOS.

“2026 has been a very transformative year as society in general. One of the parts that I love about La Sordera is that we’ve also had a lot of transformations throughout these 10 years, but I feel that 2026 was a year where we’ve transformed our way of thinking,” Jiminez says. “Now, with everything happening, I feel we know how to take what we’re learning and put it on paper.”

The next chapter, she says, depends on how the wider live music business responds to the growing movement around Latin music.

“Our expansion is based on what people are doing, between the Venezuelans and the Latin Americans and now the Spaniards. We listen to the society we’re in, and in each challenge we’ve faced, we just take the wave and ride it. My fear now is how the rest of the industry is going to ride this wave – how are we going to take this moment to work together?” she says.
