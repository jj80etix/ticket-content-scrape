# A Permanent Injunction Isn’t the End: State AGs Are Policing Compliance With Court Orders Through Separate Contempt Actions

## Summary

A legal explainer on how state attorneys general are increasingly pursuing standalone civil-contempt actions to enforce compliance with permanent injunctions years after a case closes, illustrated by the FTC and seven states' Oct. 13, 2026 motion against pharmaceutical executive Martin Shkreli for allegedly violating his lifetime pharmaceutical-industry ban tied to the Daraprim price-gouging case.

### Key Points
- FTC + 7 states filed an Oct. 13, 2026 motion to hold Martin Shkreli in civil contempt for violating a 2022 lifetime pharmaceutical-industry ban.
- The underlying case concerned Vyera Pharmaceuticals' steep price increase on the drug Daraprim in 2015 (from $17.50 to $750 per tablet).
- Explains the legal standard for civil contempt (clear order, clear noncompliance, no diligent compliance effort) and the remedies sought (divestiture, disgorgement, escalating sanctions).
- Broader takeaway: state AGs are treating old permanent injunctions as ongoing obligations, enforced via fresh contempt motions rather than new lawsuits.

This item concerns pharmaceutical-industry antitrust enforcement and has no ticketing-industry content, so no research-question tags apply.

A final court order does not necessarily mean an end to the involvement of state attorneys general (AG) in an enforcement action. As a recent filing shows, state AGs are now treating failure to comply with post-judgment permanent injunctions as standalone contempt actions.

On October 13, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and seven states moved in the Southern District of New York for an order to show cause why Martin Shkreli, founder of the now defunct Vyera Pharmaceuticals LLC, should not be sanctioned and held in civil contempt for violating a February 4, 2022, permanent injunction that banned him from participating in the pharmaceutical industry for life.[\[1\]](#_ftn1) Plaintiffs allege that Shkreli has violated the ban “routinely” by continuing to have ownership interests in numerous pharmaceutical companies and by influencing the prices of company stocks through his public commentary on social media.[\[2\]](#_ftn2)

### Background: The Initial Action and Permanent Injunction

On January 27, 2020, the FTC and the New York AG sued Vyera Pharmaceuticals, its parent company Phoenixus AG, Martin Shkreli (founder of Vyera), and Kevin Mulleady (CEO of Vyera) for injunctive and other equitable relief. Six more states later joined. According to the complaint, after acquiring the lifesaving drug, Daraprim, in August 2015, the defendants raised its price from $17.50 to $750 per tablet.[\[3\]](#_ftn3) They then allegedly shielded that price from generic competition by restricting distribution so generic makers could not obtain the samples needed for FDA bioequivalence testing, and by locking up the two most viable suppliers of the drug’s active ingredient through exclusive agreements.[\[4\]](#_ftn4)

Vyera and Mulleady settled on the eve of trial. Shkreli proceeded with trial where Judge Denise Cote found him individually liable under Sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act and parallel state laws. The court found that Shkreli’s conduct delayed the entry of generic competition by at least eighteen months and generated over $64 million in profits for him and his company.[\[5\]](#_ftn5)

On February 4, 2022, the court issued a permanent injunction that imposed on Shkreli a lifetime ban from directly or indirectly participating in the pharmaceutical industry. It cited conduct that was “egregious, deliberate, repetitive, long-running, and ultimately dangerous.” [\[6\]](#_ftn6) The order also required Shkreli to pay $64.6 million in excess profits, to submit verified annual compliance reports for at least 10 years, and to make himself and his records available to regulators on five days’ notice.[\[7\]](#_ftn7) The order expressly provides that Shkreli’s public statements about a pharmaceutical company are deemed an action taken to influence or control that company if he “intended the statement to have that effect or if a reasonable person would conclude that the statement has that effect.” [\[8\]](#_ftn8) The Second Circuit affirmed the injunction in January 2024.[\[9\]](#_ftn9)

### What the Contempt Motion Alleges

On October 13, 2026, the FTC and state AGs from New York, California, Illinois, North Carolina, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and Virginia moved for an order to show cause why Shkreli should not be held in civil contempt and be sanctioned for violating the court’s permanent injunction that bars him “for life from directly or indirectly participating in any manner in the pharmaceutical industry.” [\[10\]](#_ftn10) Plaintiffs allege that Shkreli specifically violated the provisions prohibiting him from “acquiring or holding an Ownership Interest in a Pharmaceutical Company” and “taking any action to directly or indirectly influence or control the management or business of any Pharmaceutical Company.” [\[11\]](#_ftn11)

Under Second Circuit precedent, a court may hold a party in civil contempt for failing to comply with an order if: (1) the order is clear and unambiguous, (2) proof of noncompliance is clear and convincing, and (3) the party has not diligently attempted to comply in a reasonable manner.[\[12\]](#_ftn12) The FTC and state AGs lean on the Second Circuit’s affirmance that the injunction is sufficiently “specific and definite” to satisfy the first element.[\[13\]](#_ftn13) Relying largely on Shkreli’s own YouTube livestreams and social media posts, they argue that the second and third elements are met because Shkreli possessed ownership interests in more than 60 pharmaceutical companies, and that Shkreli intended his public commentary on social media and livestream videos to move prices, noting that one company’s stock fell 17% right after he announced his short.[\[14\]](#_ftn14)

In addition to asking that Shkreli be held in civil contempt and sanctioned, the FTC and state AGs have also asked for (1) divestiture of all pharmaceutical holdings; (2) a fine equal to trading profits since February 4, 2022, with no offset for losses, plus prejudgment interest; (3) a sworn accounting and financial statement, verified by an auditor at Shkreli’s expense; (4) escalating consequences for continued or future violations, including additional fines and incarceration; and (5) an order that Shkreli not destroy any potential evidence relating to his past, present, or future violations of the permanent injunction.[\[15\]](#_ftn15)

### Why This Matters

***Continuous monitoring*.** The motion comes more than four years after the permanent injunction, showing that an individual’s obligations under a court order do not lapse when the legal proceeding ends. States and government entities may regularly monitor for conduct that violates permanent injunctions they have pursued and won. Statements from state AGs reflect this posture. New York AG Letitia James stated that “\[a\] court-ordered lifetime ban isn’t a suggestion,” and that her office is “going back to court to put a stop to \[Shkreli’s\] latest illegal behavior.” [\[16\]](#_ftn16) Pennsylvania AG Dave Sunday echoed that message, stating, “\[a\] court-ordered industry ban is not a suggestion, it is a legal obligation” and that plaintiffs will “ensure Shkreli faces consequences” if he has violated the terms of the injunction.[\[17\]](#_ftn17)

***Public-source detection*.** The alleged violations were found largely through public conduct, namely livestreams and social media. According to James, Shkreli “regularly livestreams his trading activity, posts publicly on social media about his trades and analysis, and brags about profits he’s made from trading pharmaceutical stocks.” [\[18\]](#_ftn18) Regulators may be tuning into social media and public internet commentary to discover noncompliance, rather than relying solely on periodic compliance reports and monitorships.

***Mechanism for equitable relief*.** Under the theory that a violator should not profit from wrongful conduct, the proposed sanctions include disgorgement of profits without offset for losses from violative trades, prejudgment interest, and fines. In the initial action, the FTC did not have statutory authority to obtain monetary relief such as restitution and disgorgement; however, plaintiffs argue, citing Fourth and Eleventh Circuit precedent, that this limitation does not apply to contempt actions seeking sanctions for violating an existing injunction.[\[19\]](#_ftn19) If this argument is accepted, contempt actions may be a tool used by federal and state regulators to recover money they may not have had authority to recoup in the original enforcement action.

***Potential future action*.** The “do not destroy” provision in the contempt filing serves as a warning that further action may be brought by the FTC and the states after court ordered obligations are imposed.

The contempt filing is a reminder that compliance with a court order is a continuing obligation, and regulators can and do police it. Parties subject to permanent injunctions, consent orders, and similar judgments should periodically review the scope of their obligations, including restrictions on indirect conduct and public statements, and should assume that public activity may be reviewed by regulators for compliance.
