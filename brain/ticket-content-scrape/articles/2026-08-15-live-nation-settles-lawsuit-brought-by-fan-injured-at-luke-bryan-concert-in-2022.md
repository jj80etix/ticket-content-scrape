# Live Nation Settles Lawsuit Brought by Fan Injured at Luke Bryan Concert in 2022

## Summary

Live Nation has settled a negligence lawsuit brought by Gary Stanhope, a concertgoer who suffered a traumatic brain injury during a fight at a 2022 Luke Bryan show at Hartford's Xfinity Theatre, ending a case that had been headed to trial. Financial terms weren't disclosed and the settlement carries no admission of liability, but it comes after a federal judge denied Live Nation's motion for summary judgment in May, finding a jury could reasonably conclude security was inadequate despite the promoter's staffing of 116 security personnel and 16 police officers for the show.

### Key Points
- Stanhope was injured intervening in a fight near "the rail" at the venue; a witness said the initial confrontation went on 2-3 minutes without security present before he was struck and knocked to the concrete.
- Live Nation argued the assault was an unforeseeable third-party criminal act and that its staffing met NFPA crowd-management standards; it also noted Stanhope had been drinking.
- Judge Janet C. Hall ruled the dispute wasn't just about headcount but whether security was appropriately deployed and responsive to the developing altercation, letting the negligence claim proceed toward trial.
- The parties settled last month and moved to dismiss with prejudice this week; Bryan was not a defendant.

Live Nation has settled a negligence lawsuit brought by a concertgoer who suffered a traumatic brain injury during a fight at a 2022 Luke Bryan concert in Hartford, Connecticut, ending a case that had been headed toward a jury trial over the promoter’s handling of venue security.

Gary Stanhope sued Live Nation Worldwide over injuries he suffered during Bryan’s July 30, 2022 performance at Xfinity Theatre. The concert was part of Bryan’s Live Nation-promoted Raised Up Right Tour.

The parties reached a settlement during proceedings last month and moved this week to dismiss the lawsuit with prejudice, according to court records and reporting from Billboard and Consequence. Financial terms were not disclosed, and the settlement does not represent an admission of liability by Live Nation.

Stanhope alleged that he was seriously injured after intervening in an altercation between a man and woman near an area of the amphitheater known as “the rail,” between the lower general-admission area and covered pavilion seating.

According to testimony summarized by the federal court, a witness said the confrontation involving the couple continued for roughly two to three minutes without security present. After Stanhope approached the altercation, another man struck him and knocked him onto the concrete. Stanhope alleged that the attack resulted in a traumatic brain injury and other lasting medical problems.

The lawsuit accused Live Nation of failing to provide adequate security and crowd management despite allegedly knowing of previous fights and other problems at the Hartford venue.

Live Nation disputed that characterization and pointed to extensive preparations for the concert. Court records show the promoter hired 116 security personnel and retained 16 uniformed Hartford Police Department officers for the show. Patrons were screened at the entrances, security personnel were briefed before the concert and Live Nation said its staffing complied with a National Fire Protection Association standard calling for one crowd manager for every 250 patrons.

The company also argued that Stanhope’s injuries resulted from the criminal actions of a third party rather than negligence by the venue operator. Live Nation characterized the assault as essentially unforeseeable and noted that Stanhope had been drinking before and during the concert and could not remember much of the incident himself.

But those arguments were not enough to end the case before trial.

In May, U.S. District Judge Janet C. Hall denied Live Nation’s motion for summary judgment, finding enough conflicting evidence about the security response for a jury to decide whether the promoter had breached its duty to protect patrons.

Hall pointed to testimony concerning previous fights at the venue, an “aggressive and rowdy crowd,” and the absence of security during the minutes in which the initial confrontation was unfolding. One witness testified there was no security present while the man involved in the original confrontation was throwing the woman around, although police were seen speaking with the alleged assailant within several minutes after Stanhope was knocked to the ground.

“A reasonable juror could find that it was foreseeable that Mr. Stanhope would be injured under these circumstances,” Hall concluded in rejecting Live Nation’s argument that the assault constituted superseding criminal conduct that relieved the company of potential liability.

Hall also rejected the idea that the sheer number of security personnel resolved the negligence question. The dispute, in other words, was not simply whether Live Nation had hired enough guards, but whether security was appropriately deployed and responded adequately to a confrontation developing inside the venue.

That distinction could have made the case particularly relevant for concert and venue operators had it reached trial. Live Nation demonstrated substantial security planning and staffing, but the court found that evidence of a delayed or inadequate response to a developing altercation was still enough to potentially support a negligence verdict.

The settlement prevents a jury from resolving those competing claims and establishes no finding that Live Nation was responsible for Stanhope’s injuries.

Bryan was not accused of wrongdoing and was not a defendant in the case. The litigation was directed at Live Nation in its capacity as operator of Xfinity Theatre and focused on the company’s responsibility for patron security at the concert.
