# Olivia Rodrigo Live At The Blue Room At Third Man Records (Exclusive)

## Summary

Olivia Rodrigo played an exclusive, invite-only show at Jack White's Blue Room inside Third Man Records in Nashville, with White personally introducing her to a crowd of fewer than 200 for a set drawing from Guts and covering the White Stripes' "Dead Leaves and the Dirty Ground."

### Key Points
- The invite-only performance took place at Third Man Records' Blue Room, Nashville.
- Jack White personally introduced Rodrigo to the audience.
- The setlist included "Ballad of a Homeschooled Girl" and a White Stripes cover.
- Attended by industry figures including T-Bone Burnett and NPR's Ann Powers; recorded live to acetate.

*Olivia Rodrigo had an exclusive, invite-only performance at the Blue Room at Third Man Records in Nashville. And of course,* Pollstar *was there to capture the superstar in her element.*

*During the special event, Jack White hit the stage at his bar to introduce Olivia Rodrigo to the crowd. The star opened her set with the* Guts *fan favorite “Ballad of a Homeschooled Girl” and later covered “Dead Leaves And The Dirty Ground” by the White Stripes.*

*Here’s a review by* Pollstar *Senior Contributing Editor Holly Gleason, followed by photos captured at the event.*  
  
Olivia Rodrigo may be a product of the child star industrial complex, but since vexing certain male critical types with her explosion across the pop horizon with “Driver’s License,” she’s made a staggering case for punk’s transcendent glory. Taking the stage at Third Man’s Blue Room in Nashville Sunday night, she defined the thrill of garage rock, the punk-pop collision and the reality that angst is just as angsty even when wrapped in lush vocals and classic songwriting forms.

For many of the tweens and teens, as well as an industry heavy throng that included uber-producer T-Bone Burnett, Americana Music Association head Jed Hilly and NPR’s chief pop critic Ann Powers, the intimacy offered the raw Rodrigo – with an all-girl band who offered their own propulsive thrum with equal vigor – unadorned and ready to offer her youthful malaise, bad decisions and high drama with the joy that comes from bucket list moments.

After Third Man Co-Founder Ben Swank had explained that the process of cutting an acetate live – and pointing to the monitors where people could watch the recording be captured, Jack White, in a snappy black suit, welcomed the crowd of less than 200, announcing “We’re going to have an outstanding artist come on stage here in a second…”

Rodrigo came onstage to the expected shrieks and cheers. Tucking her long hair behind her ear, the band went straight to the feedback loop “Ballad of a Homeschooled Girl.” With a towering rhythm section of drummer Jordi Radnoti and bassist Kendall Wind, the propulsive force not only delivered that hardcore pummel, but there was a melodicism to the attack that injected a rounder musicality.

Indeed, that musicality is Rodrigo’s super-power. Like Blondie and the Go-Gos, she’s not afraid to make it pretty; understanding that the thrust and the rage will deliver the punk authenticity. My Chemical Romance and Smashing Pumpkins baroque touches fall across her songs without being self-conscious, but there’s also bits of Avril LaVigne’s feral approach, especially where Rodrigo shreds the most intense vocal patches.

As a singer, her upper register is mostly used as an instrument of profound sadness. The poster queen for the dysthymic generation, swallowing their meds and finding ennui, desolation and desperation that’s hard to claw out from. But in the middle range, in the hushed delivery, in the big sweeps vocally, Rodrigo is an agent of insurrective refusal. She tells her own bad thoughts, her own petty notions, and unrepentantly throws it all into the songs.

“The Cure” is a quiet storm delivered over a strummed acoustic guitar. “My head is full of poison, my heart is full of doubt,” she tells a suitor she has no interest in, as the four backing singers echo her thoughts like so many voices in her head. So much more than the sunny melody suggests, it’s that sort of subterfuge that makes Rodrigo – sans all production tricks – such a potent voice for the romantically disaffected.

In some ways, it’s easy to dismiss a thin girl in a pink and black mini-kilt, a black baby t-shirt that says “New York Confuses Me,” kohl liner and matte black boots laced almost to the knees. One more Hot Topic kid with Goth ambitions, right? But do so at your peril because these kids are smart and have reason to feel all the things.

Whether “Maggots for Brains” with its pep rally rhythms and Arianna Powell’s thrummy solo on a black Les Paul, or the staccato punch-up of “Bad Idea, Right?,” where Rodrigo’s spoken piece is more river of words than rap, the power of lean garage rock throbs like a knee into a coffee table. Present, pounding and a reminder of how much living feels, garage rock is where you find it.

At the halfway point – time to change the acetate to cut the second half of the set – Rodrigo finally spoke to the crowd. Explaining her debate about sharing the fact that when she was 15, she had an online account “Third Fan Records,” she acknowledged picking which White Stripes cover she wanted to do was a lot. Her choice of “Dead Leaves and Dirty Ground” was spot-on: blunt force staccato sections, stark industrial thrash and a vivid emotional and visual realm.

In a world of always wanting more – dazzle, production, tricks, drama – Olivia Rodrigo brought something far more compelling to the Blue Room. Deconstructing age-old tropes for right now, she stood in both juxtaposition and alignment with the generations of punks, rockers, mods and fashionistas who came before. With pure pop as a through line, she romped through the gears of MTV’s waves of sui generis – from synth (“Serena Joy”) to the Lillith Fair folk meets riot grrrl back and forth rage (“All-American Bitch”) or the Tori Amos-Fiona Apple into Taylor-esque confessional ear candy (“Stupid Song”) – with a fluidity that avoided whiplash or potholes.

Plying innocence as a tourniquet, the set-closing “Déjà Vu” delivered the been-there, done-that reality with ether-light vocals over a child’s piano part. The real talk to a former amour hits in the second verse with the crazy synth embellishments, the dagger details and the set-up for the all-group taunt, chanting “I know you get déjà vu.”

And then it was over. Mic drop. Nothing else needed to be said.
