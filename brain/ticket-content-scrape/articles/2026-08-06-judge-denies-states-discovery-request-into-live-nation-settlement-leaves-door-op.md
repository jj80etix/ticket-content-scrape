# Judge Denies States’ Discovery Request Into Live Nation Settlement, Leaves Door Open

## Summary

U.S. District Judge Arun Subramanian denied, without prejudice, a request by state attorneys general for discovery into the Justice Department's antitrust settlement with Live Nation Entertainment, ruling in a one-page order (filed August 4, 2026) that no specific discovery requests had been presented for review. The judge left the door open, directing the states to submit "narrow and targeted" requests to DOJ and Live Nation and to negotiate in good faith before returning to court if unresolved. The ruling is a procedural setback for the states but not a rejection of their underlying position, and comes as the court separately weighs Live Nation/Ticketmaster's post-trial motions to overturn parts of the states' jury verdict.

### Key Points
- Judge Arun Subramanian denied the states' discovery request without prejudice on August 4, 2026, citing lack of specific requests to evaluate.
- States must now submit "narrow and targeted" document/testimony requests directly to DOJ and Live Nation and attempt voluntary resolution before returning to court.
- The dispute sits within the court's Tunney Act review of the DOJ-Live Nation settlement, which would resolve federal antitrust claims while leaving intact a separate jury verdict won by the states.
- States have questioned whether the settlement provides meaningful relief, how its open-ticketing provisions were developed, and whether outside influence shaped the negotiations.
- Both Live Nation and DOJ had jointly opposed the states' discovery bid in filings the prior week; the judge affirmed discovery is legally available "within reasonable bounds" under Tunney Act review.
- Separately, Live Nation/Ticketmaster's post-trial motions to overturn parts of the jury verdict were argued July 31, 2026, with no ruling yet issued.

### Highlights
- Order filed August 4, 2026, by Judge Arun Subramanian (Tunney Act review of DOJ v. Live Nation settlement).
- States retain a path to discovery if they narrow their requests — not a final rejection.
- Open questions flagged by states: adequacy of relief, development of open-ticketing provisions, possible outside influence on settlement talks.
- Related but separate: post-trial motions on the states' jury verdict argued July 31, 2026, ruling pending.

### Industry Problem
This is a live-litigation checkpoint in the DOJ v. Live Nation/Ticketmaster antitrust case, the central legal proceeding shaping how much structural or behavioral relief (e.g., open-ticketing requirements) will be imposed on the dominant primary-ticketing incumbent — directly informing what competitive openings independent platforms like Etix may see.

### Proposed Solution
No solution is proposed in the article itself; the appropriate posture for Etix is to monitor the Tunney Act proceeding and the parallel post-trial motions closely, since the settlement's final terms (especially any open-ticketing/interoperability provisions) could materially reshape competitive dynamics — the trigger to escalate from monitoring to strategic planning would be a ruling on either the discovery dispute's merits or the post-trial motions.

A federal judge has denied a request by state attorneys general seeking discovery into the Justice Department’s antitrust settlement with Live Nation Entertainment, but expressly left the states a path to return with narrower demands for documents or testimony.

U.S. District Judge Arun Subramanian denied the request without prejudice in a one-page order filed August 4, finding that the court had not been given specific discovery requests to evaluate.

“The Court has no actual requests for testimony or documents to review, and so the precise scope of what the states are asking for is unclear,” Subramanian wrote.

The judge directed the states to prepare “narrow and targeted” requests, send them to the Justice Department and Live Nation, and meet with the parties to determine whether some or all of the requested discovery can be provided voluntarily.

Subramanian said the Justice Department and Live Nation should consider the states’ requests in good faith, adding that the court believes discovery in the Tunney Act proceeding is legally available when kept “within reasonable bounds.”

“The states should make sure their requests are narrow and targeted, and the United States and Live Nation should entertain these requests in good faith,” the judge wrote.

The states had sought discovery concerning the circumstances and substance of the Justice Department’s settlement, which would resolve the federal government’s claims against Live Nation while leaving intact a separate jury verdict obtained by the states.

Among other issues, the states have questioned whether the proposed settlement provides meaningful relief, how its open-ticketing provisions were developed and whether outside influence played a role in the negotiations.

Live Nation and the Justice Department opposed the broader discovery effort, arguing that the states had not established a sufficient basis for expanding the court’s review into the settlement negotiations.

Subramanian’s order does not authorize any particular request or require either party to immediately produce information. It does, however, reject the idea that settlement-related discovery is categorically unavailable and invites the states to return with a more defined application.

The discovery dispute is part of the court’s review of the Justice Department settlement under the Tunney Act, which requires federal courts to determine whether proposed antitrust consent judgments are in the public interest.

It is separate from Live Nation and Ticketmaster’s pending post-trial motions seeking to overturn portions of the states’ jury verdict. Subramanian heard arguments on those motions July 31 but has not yet issued a ruling.
