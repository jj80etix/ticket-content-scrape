# Resale Marketplace Leaders Examine AI Trends, Evolution of How Consumers Find and Buy Tickets at WTC

## Summary

Executives from TicketNetwork, Gametime, TickPick, AXS, Automatiq, and SeatGeek used the Marketplace Panel at the 2026 World Ticket Conference in New Orleans to describe how AI purchasing agents, open distribution, and regulatory pressure are reshaping resale ticketing. Panelists expect AI agents to increasingly handle discovery and comparison on consumers' behalf, pushing marketplaces to compete on fulfillment reliability, inventory accuracy, and integrations rather than brand or storefront traffic, while cautioning that AI-driven purchases still represent under 1% of volume today. The panel also flagged fast-moving state/provincial resale legislation (price caps, marketplace commissions, transfer restrictions) as one of the industry's biggest threats, and pointed to World Cup fulfillment failures as a preview of the operational and reputational stakes.

### Key Points
- Panelists: TicketNetwork SVP Kevin Lemke, Gametime CEO Brad Griffith, TickPick co-CEO Brett Goldberg, AXS Sr. Director of Resale Brock Moore, Automatiq CPO Drew Gainor, SeatGeek's Jonny Kalfus; moderated by Select-A-Ticket's Tom Patania.
- Lemke predicts ticketing in five years will be "clearly dominated by purchasing agents from the consumer side," alongside leaner seller operations.
- One panelist estimated AI-agent-driven marketplace business at under 1% of volume today, though growing rapidly.
- Gainor predicts marketplaces could become an API/infrastructure layer serving AI agents, travel platforms, media companies and rights holders rather than direct-traffic storefronts.
- Panelists cited the recently completed World Cup as a high-visibility stress test of fulfillment, transfer restrictions, and seller reliability.
- Griffith acknowledged the resale sector "may not have done enough" to head off government intervention amid rising state/provincial resale legislation (price caps, marketplace commissions, transfer restrictions, seller verification).

### Highlights
- AI-agent share of marketplace transactions is currently estimated at under 1% but "growing rapidly," per an unnamed panelist.
- Lemke's five-year prediction: consumer-side purchasing agents will dominate, paired with larger-but-leaner ticket-selling operations.
- Panel identified concrete resale-trust signals AI agents will weight: verified listings, instant delivery, accurate seat data, and disclosed benefits (seat numbers, club access, F&B credits, parking).
- State/provincial legislative activity is accelerating on resale price caps, marketplace commissions, transfer restrictions, and seller verification, with panelists calling the pace "surprising."

### Industry Problem
Resale marketplaces face a dual pressure: AI agents are poised to strip away brand/storefront advantages and force competition purely on fulfillment reliability and inventory accuracy (Q14), while inconsistent, fast-moving state and provincial resale rules — often written without a clear view of how ticket distribution actually works, including who holds original-price data — threaten to impose unenforceable requirements on the secondary market (Q11, Q6). Both pressures are compounded by weak cross-party data sharing between rights holders, ticketing platforms, and resale marketplaces, which makes it hard to verify inventory, original prices, or transfer rights.

### Proposed Solution
Panelists see the path forward as deeper primary/secondary integration — rights holders and resale platforms sharing data to validate tickets and speed delivery — plus marketplaces repositioning as API/infrastructure layers optimized for agent-readable trust signals (fulfillment history, verified listings, delivery speed) rather than consumer-facing UX alone. For an independent primary platform like Etix, this argues for building structured, agent-consumable inventory/fulfillment APIs and verified-listing data now (to be well-positioned as AI purchasing agents pick distribution partners) and monitoring state-by-state resale legislation closely, since a primary platform controlling original-price data could become a key player in enabling (or resisting) price-cap enforcement schemes.

**Executives from TicketNetwork, Gametime, TickPick, AXS, Automatiq and SeatGeek said artificial intelligence will change ticket discovery and distribution, while placing greater importance on fulfillment, integrations and consumer trust.**

The next major ticket marketplace may not look like a marketplace at all.

Instead of visiting several ticket websites, comparing listings and completing a transaction through a familiar storefront, consumers may increasingly tell an artificial intelligence agent what event they want to attend, where they prefer to sit and how much they are willing to spend.

The agent would handle much of the remaining work, potentially comparing inventory across multiple sources before directing the transaction to whichever marketplace can offer the best combination of price, availability and dependable fulfillment.

That emerging model dominated the Marketplace Panel at the 2026 World Ticket Conference in New Orleans, where executives from TicketNetwork, Gametime, TickPick, AXS, Automatiq and SeatGeek discussed how artificial intelligence, open distribution, regulatory pressure and changing rights-holder strategies are reshaping the ticket business.

“I think the industry is remarkably resilient,” TicketNetwork Senior Vice President of Strategy and Planning Kevin Lemke said. “It’s clearly under pressure from a number of fronts. There’s just a ton of change coming at everybody, but I’m optimistic, and I think we’re going to adapt well.”

The panelists broadly agreed that artificial intelligence will reduce the amount of time consumers spend manually searching for tickets, although they differed on how quickly AI agents will begin completing purchases themselves.

Rather than opening numerous browser tabs and comparing hundreds of listings, a buyer could eventually give an agent a relatively simple instruction: Find the best available seats within a stated budget, in a preferred section and with sufficient confidence that the tickets will arrive on time.

Moore said he expects AI to eliminate much of the work required to reach a purchasing decision, even if consumers do not immediately turn over the entire transaction to an autonomous agent.

Gainor similarly argued that the ticket discovery layer is being rebuilt while the transactional infrastructure is likely to remain with established marketplaces. Under that model, AI may help consumers decide what to purchase, but marketplaces will still be responsible for the inventory, payment, delivery and customer-service functions behind the transaction.

That could change how marketplaces compete.

A marketplace optimized for conventional search engines has historically attempted to attract consumers to its own website or application. An AI agent may instead evaluate ticket sources according to pricing, inventory accuracy, fulfillment history, customer support and dispute resolution.

Traditional brand recognition would remain valuable, panelists said, but an agent may place less weight on logos, application downloads or advertising prominence than on measurable evidence that a marketplace consistently completes orders.

Lemke predicted that ticketing five years from now will be “clearly dominated by purchasing agents from the consumer side,” alongside larger but leaner ticket-selling operations.

He also identified artificial intelligence as one of the largest opportunities available to professional sellers, allowing businesses to automate repetitive work, make faster decisions and “do more with less.”

Griffith said Gametime has already seen a significant increase in product-development velocity through the use of AI tools. Goldberg offered a similar example from TickPick, saying the company was able to rebuild its application on a timeline that would previously have required a substantially longer development process.

The immediate consumer impact may still be limited, however. When an audience member asked how much marketplace business was already arriving directly through AI platforms, one panelist described the current share as less than 1%, although growing rapidly.

## Marketplaces May Move Behind the Consumer Interface

Artificial intelligence does not necessarily mean fewer ticket transactions for marketplaces. It could instead mean that marketplaces increasingly operate behind other companies’ interfaces.

Gainor predicted that the industry could eventually have fewer large consumer-facing marketplace brands but substantially more places where consumers can purchase tickets.

In that scenario, a marketplace becomes an infrastructure and API layer supplying inventory to AI agents, travel platforms, media companies, rights holders and other distribution partners. Marketplaces would compete for integrations and transaction volume rather than solely for direct consumer traffic.

The panel described that shift as part of the broader convergence of primary and secondary ticketing.

Teams, venues and other rights holders are increasingly willing to distribute inventory through multiple channels, particularly when they can retain control of the underlying ticket, collect customer data and ensure that the eventual buyer receives a reliable experience.

Panelists repeatedly referred to open distribution arrangements that allow rights holders to make inventory available through additional marketplaces without abandoning their existing primary ticketing relationships.

That approach also recognizes the amount marketplaces spend finding potential buyers. A team may operate its own ticketing website, but an outside marketplace or distribution partner can reach consumers who would not otherwise visit the team’s direct channel.

For TicketNetwork, which has long supplied ticket inventory and technology across a broader network of retail and distribution partners, the movement toward marketplace infrastructure closely resembles a model the company has operated for years. AI agents could substantially expand the number and types of businesses capable of presenting that inventory to consumers.

The result may be a more distributed ticket market in which the consumer does not always know—or necessarily care—which company supplied the underlying listing.

As the marketplace itself becomes less visible, dependable fulfillment becomes more important.

Panelists said AI agents will need reliable information about whether inventory is authentic, whether a ticket is immediately available and whether the seller and marketplace have a strong record of completing orders.

The discussion repeatedly returned to the importance of verified listings, instant delivery, accurate seat information and clearer disclosures about amenities or benefits included with a ticket.

Kalfus said listings with more complete information generally perform better because consumers have greater confidence in what they are purchasing. That can include specific seat numbers, club access, food and beverage credits, parking or other premium benefits that sellers sometimes fail to disclose.

Marketplaces are also beginning to use seller-performance data to determine which listings receive greater visibility. A slightly cheaper ticket may not represent the best transaction when it comes from a seller with a history of late delivery or customer-service problems.

The recently completed World Cup provided an unusually visible test of those systems.

Panelists described the tournament as both a significant commercial opportunity and a difficult operational environment involving transfer restrictions, changing policies and sellers who could not always fulfill orders as originally expected.

Kalfus said SeatGeek made a deliberate decision to ensure that customers reached their events when fulfillment problems arose, even when replacing an order required the marketplace to accept a loss.

The reasoning extended beyond any individual transaction. A consumer who experiences a failed order may not distinguish among the marketplace, seller, rights holder and ticketing platform involved. The resulting complaint instead becomes another public example of a resale transaction going wrong.

Several speakers argued that marketplaces, professional sellers and rights holders therefore share responsibility for the consumer experience.

Greater direct integration between rights holders and resale platforms could help address that problem by allowing marketplaces to validate tickets and deliver them sooner. Panelists acknowledged, however, that accomplishing that goal requires greater cooperation between parties that have traditionally viewed one another with suspicion.

## Regulatory Pressure Remains an Industry Threat

Despite the panel’s optimism about technology and consumer demand, executives described legislation as one of the largest threats facing the resale industry.

States and provinces have considered or enacted rules involving resale-price caps, marketplace commissions, transfer restrictions and seller verification. Panelists said the speed and volume of those proposals have surprised parts of the industry.

Griffith acknowledged that the resale sector may not have done enough to prevent government intervention.

When consumers repeatedly encounter failed orders, unclear listings or other problems, he said, lawmakers are more likely to conclude that new rules are necessary.

Other speakers cautioned that legislation written without a detailed understanding of ticket distribution can create requirements that marketplaces cannot reasonably enforce. Price caps are particularly complicated when marketplaces do not have access to the original price paid by a seller or when different buyers paid different primary prices for comparable seats.

An audience member from Ontario raised that concern during the panel, arguing that governments imposing resale restrictions also need to explain how marketplaces are expected to identify and enforce the original ticket price.

The broader regulatory debate reinforced the panel’s emphasis on collaboration. Marketplaces argued that rights holders, ticketing platforms and sellers must share better information if regulators expect the industry to verify inventory, original prices and transfer rights.

For all the attention paid to artificial intelligence, the panelists did not describe a future in which technology eliminates marketplaces or professional sellers.

Instead, AI is likely to alter where those businesses operate within the transaction.

Consumers may interact with an agent rather than a marketplace homepage. Sellers may use automated systems to identify events, price inventory and manage fulfillment. Marketplaces may distribute their inventory through dozens or hundreds of outside interfaces.

The companies controlling reliable inventory, integrations and fulfillment infrastructure will nevertheless remain central to the transaction. But the pace of technological change means ticket businesses cannot assume that today’s operating model will remain intact for long.

“Pay very close attention to what’s going on with these AI tools, because things are changing so rapidly,” Lemke said. “What we know today is not going to be the same six months from now. You really have to stay aware.”
