# Louisiana Raises the Stakes in the Prediction Markets Fight

## Summary

Louisiana's attorney general filed a sweeping state-court action against roughly 30 entities tied to major prediction-market platforms, alleging their event contracts constitute unlicensed gambling under state law, and won an immediate ex parte temporary restraining order blocking in-state access pending a November 30 preliminary injunction hearing. Unlike prior single-platform state actions, Louisiana's approach targets the broader ecosystem at once.

### Key Points
- Suit names ~30 affiliated entities across the prediction-market industry, not a single platform.
- Court granted an ex parte TRO blocking Louisiana access immediately; merits hearing set for November 30.
- Alleges event contracts are unlicensed gambling under Louisiana law.

The legal battle over prediction markets has escalated again, but Louisiana’s latest move stands apart from the state enforcement actions that came before it.

On November 12, the Louisiana attorney general (AG) filed a sweeping state-court action challenging the operation of prediction markets in Louisiana. Rather than proceeding against a single platform, the state sued approximately 30 affiliated entities associated with many of the largest participants in the prediction-market ecosystem. Louisiana alleges that event contracts offered to persons in the state constitute unlicensed gambling under Louisiana law.

Within hours, the state court entered an *ex parte* temporary restraining order (TRO). The order temporarily prohibits the defendants from offering or facilitating event contracts for persons located in Louisiana without state authorization and requires them to restrict access from within the state. A preliminary injunction hearing is scheduled for November 30. The TRO is preliminary relief, not a determination of the merits.

### What Makes Louisiana Different?

The significance of the action lies as much in its strategy as in its underlying legal theory.

**First, Louisiana is targeting the industry collectively.** Other state challenges have generally focused on particular operators or products. Louisiana instead brought numerous major prediction-market businesses and affiliated entities into a single proceeding.

**Second, the state obtained relief almost immediately.** The court entered the TRO on the same day the lawsuit was filed and without first hearing from the defendants. That is particularly notable in an area where federal and state regulators have been litigating difficult jurisdictional questions across multiple courts.

**Third, the order is not limited to sports.** The challenged event contracts extend to subjects including sports, elections, and awards, and the order reaches activities such as operating, listing, accepting, clearing, selling, brokering, and market-making. It also requires geographic restrictions on Louisiana access.

That breadth matters. The prediction-market debate is often described as a fight over sports wagering. Louisiana’s action illustrates how the same legal theories could potentially reach a substantially broader universe of event contracts and the entities that facilitate their distribution.

### A New Front in the Federal-State Dispute

The Louisiana proceeding also sharpens the fundamental jurisdictional question facing the industry.

Prediction-market operators have generally argued that event contracts traded on federally regulated derivatives exchanges fall within the federal commodities framework and that the Commodity Exchange Act preempts contrary state gambling restrictions. States have taken the opposite view, maintaining that contracts functioning as wagers within their borders remain subject to traditional state gambling laws and licensing requirements. Those competing theories remain the subject of active litigation rather than settled law.

Louisiana may become an important testing ground because federal litigation was already underway before the state filed its case. Prediction-market operators had sued Louisiana officials in separate federal actions in October, asserting federal preemption, and at least one operator sought emergency federal relief as Louisiana commenced its state proceeding.

The immediate question is what happens after the TRO. But the broader development may be more important: Louisiana has demonstrated a potentially new model for state enforcement against prediction markets. Instead of pursuing individual platforms or particular sports products over time, a state can attempt to challenge much of the market at once and seek immediate restrictions while the larger federal preemption fight proceeds.
