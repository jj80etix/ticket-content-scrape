# Tufts Study Warns Massachusetts Ticket Resale Cap Could Strengthen Ticketmaster

## Summary

A Tufts University Center for State Policy Analysis study, funded by the independent-resale-marketplace coalition Ticket Policy Forum, warns that Massachusetts' proposed 110%-of-face resale price cap could hand Ticketmaster a structural compliance advantage over independent secondary platforms like StubHub and SeatGeek, since Ticketmaster's primary-ticketing business gives it direct access to original purchase prices that resale-only platforms lack. The report also estimates Gov. Healey's companion 10%-fee cap could cut resale-platform fee revenue by roughly two-thirds, given current combined buyer/seller fees of 25–35%.

### Highlights
- Report author Evan Horowitz: a resale price cap "could give Ticketmaster a compliance advantage, which could allow it to broaden its reach into the secondary market."
- Healey's "Great Divide Act" pairs a 110%-of-face resale cap with a 10% resale-fee cap; a Senate version has the price cap but not the fee cap. The House omitted the restrictions from its October budget — legislation remains unresolved.
- Current resale-platform combined fees run ~25–35% of ticket price; a strict 10% combined-fee reading could cut resale fee revenue by roughly two-thirds.
- The report flags that e-tickets often lack visible original prices and that purchase receipts can be digitally altered, complicating price-cap enforcement.

### Industry Problem
Price-cap and fee-cap legislation aimed at curbing resale markups, if enforced via original-purchase-price verification, could structurally favor platforms (like Ticketmaster) that control primary-sale data — a competitive asymmetry for any platform, primary or secondary, that doesn't hold that data natively.

### Proposed Solution
The study flags the problem rather than a solution; posture is Monitor (track how Massachusetts' legislature resolves the House/Senate gap and whether other states adopt similar resale caps) and Improve (as a primary platform, Etix already holds original-price data — worth assessing whether that data position is an asset if similar state-level resale regulation spreads).

A new Tufts University policy analysis warns that Massachusetts’ proposed ticket resale price cap could unintentionally strengthen Ticketmaster’s position in the secondary market, giving the company a competitive advantage over independent resale platforms that lack access to original ticket purchase prices.

The [10-page study from Tufts University’s Center for State Policy Analysis](https://cspa.tufts.edu/node/3031?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) examines competing proposals from Gov. Maura Healey and the Massachusetts Senate to restrict concert ticket resale prices to 110% of their original value. The analysis raises concerns about the potential impact on competition, ticket availability, marketplace fees and fraud, while questioning whether the restrictions would deliver their intended benefits to consumers.

Authored by Evan Horowitz, executive director of the center, the November report draws on existing academic research and experiences in other jurisdictions. It does not offer a specific forecast of how ticket prices or marketplace shares would change in Massachusetts, but identifies several economic and enforcement challenges that lawmakers would need to address.

The study was funded through a research grant from the Ticket Policy Forum, a coalition representing independent ticket resale marketplaces that have a commercial interest in the outcome of the legislation. Tufts disclosed the funding relationship in the report and stated that its researchers retained full control over the methodology, analysis and conclusions.

The findings arrive as Massachusetts lawmakers continue negotiating competing legislative proposals. Healey’s Great Divide Act would generally prevent concert tickets from being resold for more than 110% of their original price, while separately limiting resale marketplace fees to 10%. A Senate-approved version includes a similar resale-price ceiling but does not impose the same fee restrictions.

However, as [TicketNews previously reported](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/10/massachusetts-house-ticket-resale-cap-consumer-concerns/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), the Massachusetts House omitted the restrictions from an October supplemental budget, and the legislation has faced growing scrutiny over its potential effects on consumers and marketplace competition.

## A Potential Competitive Advantage for Ticketmaster

One of the study’s most significant findings concerns the information needed to enforce a resale price ceiling.

Independent secondary marketplaces such as StubHub and SeatGeek typically facilitate transactions between ticket holders and buyers without controlling the initial sale. As a result, they may not have reliable access to the original purchase price of every ticket listed on their platforms.

That creates a potential compliance problem under a law requiring resale prices to remain within 10% of the original ticket price. Marketplaces would need sellers to provide accurate purchase information, while regulators would need some way to verify that information.

Ticketmaster occupies a different position because its primary ticketing business gives it access to original ticket prices for transactions processed through its system. The Tufts analysis argues that this could make complying with resale restrictions easier for Ticketmaster than for platforms that operate primarily in the secondary market.

Horowitz writes that such a restriction could “give Ticketmaster a compliance advantage, which could allow it to broaden its reach into the secondary market.”

The potential consequence is significant because Ticketmaster already operates in both primary and secondary ticketing. A regulation that increases compliance costs for competing marketplaces could therefore affect the competitive balance without imposing comparable restrictions on the company’s primary-market operations.

The study does not conclude that Ticketmaster would necessarily gain market share. Rather, it identifies an information advantage that could become more valuable if resale platforms are required to verify and enforce original-price limits.

The analysis also raises unresolved legal questions about whether online marketplaces can be held responsible for policing prices established by third-party sellers, including the extent to which existing protections for online platforms might apply.

For individual ticket holders, the challenge is different. Many electronic tickets do not display their original purchase prices, and consumers may have difficulty locating receipts or determining which fees and taxes should count toward the resale ceiling.

The report additionally warns that purchase receipts and other digital records can be altered using image-editing software or artificial intelligence, potentially complicating efforts to establish whether a resale price violates the law.

## Fee Cap Raises Additional Competition Questions

The Tufts report also examines the potential impact of Healey’s proposed 10% limit on resale marketplace fees, a restriction that would apply to concert and sporting event tickets but would not impose equivalent limits on primary ticketing fees.

According to the analysis, fees collected by major resale platforms currently amount to approximately 25% to 35% of ticket prices when buyer and seller fees are considered together.

If the governor’s proposal were interpreted as limiting all combined marketplace fees to 10%, the report estimates that it could reduce fee-based revenue at established resale platforms by approximately two-thirds.

A narrower interpretation, allowing separate 10% fees for buyers and sellers, would have a smaller but still substantial effect.

The distinction matters because the governor’s proposal does not clearly establish whether the cap applies to fees charged to buyers, sellers or both, according to the study.

The analysis further questions whether vertically integrated companies such as Live Nation could gain a competitive advantage by distributing fee revenue across primary and secondary ticketing operations, while independent resale marketplaces lack the same flexibility.

The report does not establish that such changes would occur, but identifies the potential competitive consequences as an area requiring additional examination.

The Senate proposal creates a different concern. By limiting resale ticket prices without imposing a corresponding fee ceiling, the legislation could allow marketplaces to increase service fees to offset reductions in ticket prices.

In that scenario, buyers might see lower listed ticket prices without experiencing an equivalent reduction in their total purchase costs.

The analysis also acknowledges that appropriately designed fee caps could reduce consumer costs. Its concern is that the current proposals leave important questions unresolved about how the limits would operate and how companies and consumers might respond.

## Enforcement Challenges and Potential Fraud Risks

Beyond the competitive implications, the Tufts analysis argues that implementing a resale price ceiling could be unusually difficult in a ticketing marketplace dominated by electronic transactions and sellers operating across state and national borders.

Even if regulators establish clear definitions for original purchase prices, verifying those amounts across multiple independent platforms could require extensive recordkeeping and cooperation from sellers.

The report examines experiences in Ontario, Ireland, Australia and the United Kingdom, identifying difficulties ranging from limited enforcement activity to ticket transactions shifting toward channels beyond the reach of domestic regulators.

One concern is that buyers and sellers seeking to transact above the legal ceiling could move away from established resale marketplaces and toward informal arrangements through social media, private messaging or less-regulated websites.

Those transactions may offer fewer consumer protections, including less reliable ticket verification, payment safeguards and refund mechanisms.

The concern echoes [warnings previously raised by 16 current and former law enforcement officials](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/11/massachusetts-law-enforcement-ticket-resale-cap-fraud/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), who urged Massachusetts lawmakers to reconsider the proposed restrictions because of potential fraud risks.

However, neither the officials’ warnings nor the Tufts analysis establishes that Massachusetts consumers would necessarily experience higher fraud rates under a price cap. The report identifies displacement as a potential consequence based on economic incentives and experiences elsewhere.

The study also points to a distinction between high-demand events and the broader ticketing marketplace.

Citing earlier academic research, Horowitz notes that approximately half of resold concert tickets trade either below their original price or at a markup of less than 5%. The report also references Live Nation investor disclosures indicating that most concerts do not sell out.

Those figures suggest a 110% resale ceiling would have limited direct impact on many ordinary events where secondary-market prices are already close to or below their original values.

The largest intended effects would instead occur at high-demand concerts, where resale prices can substantially exceed the original purchase price and buyers may compete for limited inventory.

## Study Acknowledges Potential Benefits of Targeted Regulation

Although the report questions the effectiveness of broad resale price and fee caps, it does not reject all regulatory interventions.

Horowitz acknowledges that restrictions could reduce prices on compliant resale platforms and that appropriately structured fee limits could produce consumer savings.

The study also characterizes prohibitions on speculative ticket sales as a comparatively low-risk policy intervention, despite potential enforcement challenges.

Speculative sales involve offering tickets that a seller does not yet possess. Such transactions can create problems when sellers fail to obtain and deliver tickets as promised, particularly when prices rise sharply before an event.

Both the governor’s proposal and the Senate legislation include restrictions on speculative sales, alongside measures addressing misleading ticket advertisements and representations of affiliation with artists or venues.

The report concludes that the potential benefits of resale restrictions must be weighed against their possible effects on ticket availability, marketplace competition, fraud and enforcement costs.

Its findings add an academic perspective to a debate that has increasingly focused on whether regulating the secondary market can meaningfully improve affordability without addressing the original ticket prices and fees established by artists, promoters, venues and primary ticketing providers.

For now, the Massachusetts Legislature has not reached a final agreement on the proposed resale restrictions. The Senate’s economic development package, H.5576/S.3228, remains before a House-Senate conference committee.

Any eventual compromise would determine whether Massachusetts adopts a 110% resale ceiling, incorporates limits on marketplace fees, or pursues narrower consumer-protection measures.
