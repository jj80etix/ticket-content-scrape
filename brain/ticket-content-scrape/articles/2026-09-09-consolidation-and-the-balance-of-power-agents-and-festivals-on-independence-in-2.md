# Consolidation and the ‘balance of power’: Agents and festivals on independence in 2026

## Summary

Speaking at London's International Festival Forum, agents and independent festival operators said industry consolidation has "changed the balance of power," leaving fewer independent decision-makers and driving lineup homogenization as festivals get "mass-booked." Panelists said preserving independence while growing scale comes down to choosing investors and partners who share a festival's artistic vision rather than dictating booking, pricing or dates.

### Key Points
- Panelists: Roam Artists' Jess Kinn (moderator), Solo's Charly Beedell-Tuck, Hellfest's Kem Lalot, Ejekt Festival's Giannis Paltoglou.
- Beedell-Tuck: consolidation means "fewer independent decision makers at the top," producing similar, mass-booked lineups across festivals.
- Paltoglou: the key risk is investors dictating artist bookings, ticket pricing or dates — "if investors jump in... the game is lost."
- Panel also flagged fee competition from the boom in arena/stadium touring, which makes it harder for festivals to compete for artist fees.

### Highlights
- Discussion held this week at the International Festival Forum, London.
- Named risk: investor control over booking/pricing/dates as consolidation's sharpest threat to independence.
- Growing arena/stadium touring economics cited as squeezing festivals' ability to compete on artist fees.

### Industry Problem
Consolidation is narrowing the number of independent decision-makers in festival booking and investment, homogenizing lineups and squeezing independent festivals' negotiating leverage on both artist fees and creative direction — a structural pain point for the "clients" (festivals, promoters) Etix serves as well as a market-structure trend to track.

### Proposed Solution
Panelists' own prescription is partner/investor selection discipline — take capital only from investors who share creative vision and don't dictate booking, pricing or dates — rather than any product fix. For Etix, the actionable signal is to monitor which independent festivals stay independent vs. get absorbed, since client concentration under a few promoter groups affects the addressable independent-venue/festival market; the trigger to watch is any acceleration in investor-driven festival acquisitions following this consolidation trend.

## Consolidation and the ‘balance of power’: Agents and festivals on independence in 2026

Speaking at the International Festival Forum this week, leading sector figures discussed how festivals can grow in scale while preserving independence

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/smush-webp/2026/09/IFF-Conference-Day-One-Panel-Highlights-@mosdrew-00129-1-scaled.jpg.webp)

R-L: Jess Kinn, Giannis Paltoglou, Charly Beedell-Tuck, Kem Lalot © @mosdrew

Consolidation in the festival sector has “changed the balance of power” in the industry, according to leading agents and festivals speaking at the International Festival Forum in London this week.

Speaking on a panel titled ‘Beyond Consolidation: How Festivals, Artists and Independent Agencies Can Thrive Together’, hosted by Roam Artist’s Jess Kinn, Solo agent Charly Beedell-Tuck, Hellfest’s Kem Lalot, and Ejekt Festival’s Giannis Paltoglou told industry figures that the question of whether festivals can grow and preserve their independence comes down to their choice of partners at a time when consolidation is squeezing the level of choice available for acts and agents.

“I think that consolidation has definitely changed the balance of power,” Beedell-Tuck said, adding: “I think there are simply fewer independent decision makers at the top of the industry. The danger is that if one party becomes too powerful, we end up with fewer choices for our artists. On the festival side, I think you see the lineups are all similar because they’re all being mass‑booked, and I think that’s where independence is more important”.

**Investors at the core  
**For Ejekt Festival’s Paltoglou, the central question of whether festivals in 2026 can grow in scale while preserving independence comes down to investor partners.

“I believe you can grow your audience, grow your reputation, grow your festival. But the most important thing is that if you have investors, you have the same vision with them. The problem is if investors jump in and say which artists you need to book, or what tickets you need to charge, or change the dates, then the game is lost,” he said.

It is crucial for independents looking to grow their footprint that they “decide everything according to their vision from the start”, he argued, adding: “Yes, you can grow, but you need to put the lines and decide what you’re going to do in your festival.”

And Beedell-Tuck noted that from an agent’s point of view, if independents can manage this growth, the rewards are great, saying: “Independents have a point of difference because they’re fighting for a place at the table. They go above and beyond”.

> “if investors jump in and say which artists you need to book, or what tickets you need to charge, the game is lost”

**Fee competition  
**The panel also said the increasing popularity of arena and stadium touring is creating a landscape where festivals cannot compete on artist fees, with Beedell-Tuck saying this “makes it more difficult for festivals to have some headliners sometimes”.

The race against stadium touring does not stop there, however, as Beedell-Tuck said that the production “constraints” often necessary at festivals can make stadium or arena shows preferable for artists. She added that this matter of how much control the artist has is increasingly coming up in festival booking discussions.

But the situation can be different for genre-specific festivals, however, said Hellfest’s Lalot. He admits it is sometimes “easier” for Hellfest because “every artist from the punk and metal scene wants to play Hellfest now”.

“With an attendance of 204,000 people, we can announce one artist and we are sold out before announcing any more artists, which is cool. We have resources to make big offers. For the metal and punk scene, we are the same as Graspop and Copenhell, and not far from Download, Southside, Hurricane. So we can talk together and make offers together, arrange some days to make it easier,” he said.

> “After all these years, I need full transparency between agent and promoter”

**The role of artists  
**When asked by Kinn if artists have an obligation to support independent festivals, Beedell-Tuck emphasised that when doing this, acts must still choose the plays that fit into their “long-term strategy”.

While fees might be an area of disadvantage for independents, however, Paltoglou said that their ability to curate communities is their greatest strength, but once again this is all the more the case in genre-specific festivals.

“Certain styles of music, artists prefer to be with the community,” he said.

**‘A relationship busin** **ess’  
**This community aspect also has a parallel in the heightened personability of the booking process in the independent sector, a strength which Paltoglou said is key for festivals to capitalise on.

“After all these years, I need full transparency between agent and promoter. The best is when an artist leaves the stage and says: ‘we were in Greece, we had great hospitality, the show was great, the audience was amazing; please book me again next year’,” he said.

Lalot added that there is “definitely more pressure” on the independent sector when booking their lineups, including “more information, more data, more artists, more promoters”. As a result, he said, “anyone can send an offer now; the most important thing is who will deliver. Relationships built between promoter and agent are important”.

Beedell-Tuck agreed, adding that the independent landscape is “much more of a partnership now. You want to work with good people. Transparency is essential.”
