# Music City Milestone, Smashville Solidified: Bridgestone Arena Celebrates 30th Anniversary

## Summary

A Pollstar feature marks Bridgestone Arena's 30th anniversary, tracing how Mayor Phil Bredesen's early-1990s decision to build the arena on then-seedy Lower Broadway — against conventional wisdom to locate arenas near highways and parking, not downtown — catalyzed Nashville's transformation into a major tourist and entertainment district. Nashville Predators/Bridgestone Arena CEO Sean Henry and SVP Britt Kincheloe recount the venue's near-relocation scare in 2007 and the cultural shift under Henry's 2010 arrival that reconnected the arena with the surrounding neighborhood.

### Key Points
- Bridgestone Arena opened Dec. 18, 1996 with Amy Grant's "Tennessee Christmas" concert; has had four names over 30 years.
- Built at Fifth & Broadway on Mayor Phil Bredesen's vision, breaking from the era's norm of siting arenas away from downtowns.
- In 2007, a "Save The Team" rally helped keep the Nashville Predators from relocating to Hamilton, Ontario amid low attendance and lease concerns.
- Sean Henry (arrived as COO in 2010, now CEO) and then-CEO Jeff Cogen unified previously siloed hockey/non-hockey operations and repositioned the arena to engage with its now-thriving Lower Broadway surroundings.

Nashville’s Lower Broadway has, over the last decade and a half, become one of the country’s most iconic tourist strips.

A five-block ribbon of neon gently sloping down to the banks of the Cumberland River. Trendy bars and clubs sit aside stalwart honky tonks and star-branded nightspots. The Ryman’s iconic facade is illuminated by its modern neighbors. Crowds throng the sidewalks nearly every night of the year, merrymakers on the ubiquitous pedal taverns whoop and holler as they wind the streets.

That scene is a fairly new one, truly kickstarted with a shift in Nashville’s tourism and broader marketing strategy made after the 2010 flood devastated the city, leading to increased attention from coastal newspapers selling Music City as a fun — if then, underrated — place to visit.

But the change really started in the mid- to late 1990s. Phil Bredesen became mayor in 1991 — the first non-native Tennessean elected as Nashville’s mayor in decades — and set to transform his adopted hometown.

Nashville, like many cities, had suffered through decades of downtown decline. Broadway, once the beating heart of the city’s business district, remained home to a few venerable honky tonks, but even the Ryman was largely unused. The rest of the street was a mishmash of dodgy massage parlors, adult bookstores and strip clubs. In short, it wasn’t a place to go after dark unless you were up to something.

But Bredesen had a vision to transform Nashville into a major league city. He wooed the Houston Oilers to town to become the Tennessee Titans, shepherding an effort to build what is now Nissan Stadium on the East Bank across from downtown. And he pushed for an arena to replace the aging Municipal Auditorium.

At the time, the conventional wisdom was to put arenas far from city centers, to surround them with parking and interstate access and that was true even in cities with downtowns that had remained vibrant.

So when Bredesen said he wanted the arena at Fifth & Broadway, smack dab at the head of what was then a thoroughfare of less-than-sterling reputation, eyebrows shot up.

Needless to say, that assessment has aged like stale beer flowing into a sewer grate.

“Mayor Bredesen was a visionary,” Sean Henry, CEO of the Nashville Predators and Bridgestone Arena, says. “The man should get royalties from every city in the world that replicated what he did. … Look at all those buildings built in that window: Tampa, Nashville, Los Angeles. They were intentionally built in areas that weren’t great with the desire of changing it.”

Britt Kincheloe, Senior Vice President, Guest Services & Event Operations, started out as a temp at what was then generically known as Nashville Arena — the facility has been through four names — before the doors officially opened with Amy Grant’s Tennessee Christmas concert December 18, 1996.

“Our temporary offices were down on Second Avenue and they said ‘Hey to get to the arena, you need to jump in your car and drive there.’ And I was like ‘I’m a good walker and it’s only Second Avenue to Fifth,’” she remembers. “They said that’s probably not the best idea for your safety.”

Oh, how times have changed.

Early photos of the arena include a sign for the then-neighboring Adult World (you can imagine). There was a giant vacant lot just south of the arena that’s now the Music City Center convention center. People come and go on foot. At all hours.

There are still remnants of that era etched in the building — some corners still have the extremely ‘90s purple-and-yellow branding, for example, and Henry notes that several sides of the building address the outside world in a very “introverted” way.

“Whoever the salesman was for gray paint was the best salesman in the world,” he says, saying his initial impression when he arrived as COO in 2010 was that it “wasn’t so dissimilar from a lot of buildings.

“The mindset was you don’t want to let the outside in. This building could have been in Tacoma, Washington, or St. Louis or Providence, Rhode Island,” he said. “Broadway then wasn’t what it is now, but there was a great history. I thought we had an opportunity to tell a slightly different story and connect with it a little.”

Henry’s arrival — along with then CEO Jeff Cogen — marked a distinct turning point for Bridgestone Arena and its main tenant, the NHL’s Nashville Predators. Part of the late-‘90s league expansion, the Preds had long been treated as a pariah by much of the league, threatened with relocation almost from the start. In 2007, the moving trucks were all but backed up and Hamilton, Ontario, was taking pre-orders for season tickets in anticipation the Preds would head north of the border.

A “Save The Team” rally in June of that year boosted season tickets enough to give confidence to a group of local businessmen — mostly health care executives — to combine their wealth and keep the Preds in Nashville, but a tense relationship with the city persisted, attendance was still a challenge (a grim parlor game for Preds fans at the time was keeping track of average attendance, hoping the number would stay above the minimum required to prevent lease-breaking) and fears of losing the team never faded.

Non-hockey event traffic was similarly below par. At the time, hockey operations and non-hockey operations were siloed. One of the earliest changes Henry and Cogen made was to bring everyone together on the same team and change the mindset of merely trying to compete with New York, Detroit and Montreal (in both hockey and events) and trying to beat them. Then chairman of the Predators, Tom Ciggaran, removed the word “no” from the vocabulary.

And everyone got to work.

“We changed our business when the new ownership group came in,” David Kells, Chief Venue Officer, said. “Prior to that, we were in silos. There was an arena operations group and there was a hockey sales and operations group. We brought those things together. If there are people who want to spend their entertainment dollars with us, how do we make sure that they’re aware of all the offering, that, my gosh, I can come with my kids to Disney On Ice part of the year and then come to date night with my significant other to a concert and ome with my bros to a hockey game. It’s just a different way of doing business and the businesses grew together.”

The Predators became more competitive, winning a playoff series for the first time in 2011, becoming aggressive with ticket sales and promotions to build a persistent (and famously rowdy) fanbase.

Henry tasked his staff with finding out why major country acts often skipped Nashville on tour and how to make the arena a destination for tours of all kinds.

A true — forgive the turn of phrase — high-water mark came when Garth Brooks played nine sold-out benefit shows after the 2010 flood.

“That totally made people think of this market differently,” Kells said. “We were able to sell out nine concerts in a market that hadn’t had that level of success … It just kept growing and growing and was one of the most exciting on-sales I’ve ever been a part of.”

Henry said at the time, Bridgestone got “very aggressive” with guarantees and ensuring tours they’d get sellouts.

“There was a promoter in Chicago and he said you’re not going to sell this show out and I said, what do you care? You’re getting the money because we’re buying the tickets if it’s not sold out, and the worst thing that happens is we give them to our season ticket holders, and that’s a win, too,” Henry says. “Once you show the world you can get top grosses and that it’s a wonderful building with a great staff, things start changing.”

Things picked up. Bridgestone Arena was, by some metrics, the world’s busiest in 2015. The NHL awarded Bridgestone Arena its All-Star Game in 2016 and it was a rousing success — though Henry said he guaranteed the league beautiful weather and, in fact, Nashville was hit with a rare major snowstorm the week before — and introduced the concept of giant watch parties for hockey events  
on Broadway. Now commonplace in the NHL from Vegas to Montreal, it was unheard of then. The Preds came within a game of lifting the Stanley Cup with a shock run to the finals in 2017. Those two events brought wide exposure to the changes in attitude and presentation both for the team and the building. Despite playing fellow smaller-market team Pittsburgh in that Cup Final, it set ratings records. Perceptions changed leaguewide and Nashville was suddenly seen as a top destination for hockey media, not just a have-to-do-it stop.

Similarly, the building has become a must-do for major tours, the stop now written in pen on the first draft of routing instead of pencilled in on the second leg.

“The staff backstage, front of house, everything makes it easy,” says Britt Kincheloe, the arena’s senior vice president of guest services and event operations. “When we have a security meeting for an event, it’s not uncommon for the security person to say a Nashville show is right up with your LA or your New York shows because there’s so many people that want to see it. A Nashville show is seen as a positive thing now.”

There’s also been a change in the way the arena is seen by those working in the industry. Whereas it was once seen as a stepping-stone job in a secondary market, it’s  
now a career goal, which has led to long-term staffers — there’s a handful of Day One employees like Kincheloe and dozens of people with 25 years of service or more; Kells worked at the arena in the late ‘90s before coming back as overnight conversion manager in 2005. But the desire to work in Nashville also means plenty of new employees. Kincheloe says that keeps the ideas fresh while also allowing for the benefit of institutional knowledge.

Bridgestone Arena is, in short, an established player and the gateway to Nashville’s glittering gulch. It’s 30 years old and, as Henry notes, in many ways still reflects the era in which it was built. That means it’s time for a major renovation.

There was no thought put into relocating or asking for a new building — the current lease with the city, announced just before the pandemic, puts the onus for renovation on the Predators as the lessee, funded by captured sales tax; it was a far cry from the era when the arena would come to Nashville’s Sports Authority hat in hand, needing the city to help it stay afloat.

Bridgestone Arena plans to be at Fifth and Broadway for decades more.

“I believe in multi-generational memories in a building,” Henry says. “Music and sports are shared memories and it binds communities to teams.”

With that mindset, Henry and his team looked at what would keep the arena safe, modern and efficient for the next 30 years and what it would take to do it in a building from 1996.

The outcome is the Broadway 2030 project, adding 170,000 square feet, transforming the two entrance plazas and replacing much of the arena grey paint with glass. New bars and restaurants will come to towers on what is now the “back” of the arena on Sixth Avenue and Demonbreun. The surrounding campus will have an “open-air” feel (while still being protected from the elements to allow for year-round use in Nashville’s famously fickle weather) that more deeply connects the arena with the street it helped transform.

Inside, the project will add 600 to 700 additional seats to Bridgestone Arena’s capacity, wider concourses, club spaces and gathering areas, along with amenity upgrades.

A separate hotel project for Fifth Avenue (now John Lewis Way) is also part of the plan.

“Our approach to renovation is never to do $7 million and then nothing for seven years. We’ve done $120 million over the past 10, 12 years and we’re going to do a $400 million arena renovation and a $200 million development addition and a hotel as well,” Henry says.

It’s going to take some summer shutdowns over the next five years to get it all done, but Henry says there’s more to come.

“We also have a plan for 2038 and ‘43 and ‘49 to make sure it stays as modern as possible,” he says. “There’s something unique about going to a game in the same seat with your grandparents and your grandkids and that’s what we want to bridge.”

In 1996, Nashville was on the cusp of transformation and a mayor from New Jersey had the foresight to see it, to shepherd it, to will it to happen. It came in fits and starts and took more radical reimagining and, honestly, a natural disaster to catalyse it. And it took people working to move the city into the realm of the top destinations for concert tours and tourists alike.

Now that vision is a bustling, flickering Broadway. And at the top of the hill: the building that truly started the change is ready to change once again.
