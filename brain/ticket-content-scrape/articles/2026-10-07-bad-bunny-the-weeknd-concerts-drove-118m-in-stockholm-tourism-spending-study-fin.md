# Bad Bunny, The Weeknd Concerts Drove $118M in Stockholm Tourism Spending, Study Finds

## Summary

Five Bad Bunny and The Weeknd concerts in Stockholm this summer generated an estimated $118 million (SEK 1.18 billion) in direct tourism spending, drawing roughly 195,000 visitors, half of Bad Bunny's audience traveling from outside Sweden (from 135 countries), according to a Live Nation-commissioned analysis. Live Nation Sweden said it promoted 60-plus concerts and sold 300,000-plus tickets in Stockholm over the summer overall.

### Key Points
- 5 shows (2 Bad Bunny, 3 The Weeknd) drew ~195,000 visitors and an estimated $118M in direct tourism spending.
- Half of Bad Bunny's concertgoers traveled from outside Sweden — the highest international share the Experience Institute has recorded for a Stockholm music event.
- Concerts were the primary trip reason for 9 of 10 visitors; average overnight stays of 3.6 days (Bad Bunny) and 2.9 days (The Weeknd).
- Analysis commissioned by Live Nation, Stockholm Business Region and Stockholm Live; measures direct spending only, not full economic-impact multipliers.
- Comparable to Taylor Swift's 2024 Stockholm shows (SEK 848M in tourism impact), per earlier Stockholm Business Region data.

Five concerts by Bad Bunny and The Weeknd generated an estimated SEK 1.18 billion, or about $118 million, in direct tourism-related spending in Stockholm this summer, according to [an analysis released by Live Nation](https://news.livenationentertainment.com/news/bad-bunny-and-the-weeknd-concerts-give-stockholms-economy-a-sek-1-18-billion-boost?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content).

The figure covers two Bad Bunny shows in July and three The Weeknd concerts in August. Together, the five performances brought around 195,000 people to Stockholm, including accompanying travelers, according to the underlying [Swedish release](https://www.mynewsdesk.com/se/livenation/pressreleases/bad-bunny-och-the-weeknd-boostade-stockholms-ekonomi-med-oever-en-miljard-3470385?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content). Visitor spending extended beyond the venues to hotels, restaurants and cafes, shopping, entertainment, local transportation and other consumption around the city.

The Swedish release put what it calls specific tourism turnover at SEK 1.1806 billion, representing spending tied to trips where the concerts were the primary reason for visiting Stockholm. Broader general tourism turnover connected with the visitors exceeded SEK 1.25 billion. Overnight visitors spent an average of just over SEK 2,000, or roughly $200, per day.

The international draw was especially pronounced around Bad Bunny’s shows. Half of his concertgoers traveled from outside Sweden, which the Experience Institute identified as the highest international share it has recorded for a music event in Stockholm. His visitors came from 135 countries, with Spain the largest foreign source market, while The Weeknd’s audiences included visitors from 105 countries.

The study also found that the concerts were the main reason for the trip for nine out of 10 visitors. Those staying overnight spent an average of 3.6 days in Stockholm around Bad Bunny’s concerts and 2.9 days around The Weeknd’s dates.

Live Nation Sweden CEO Jon Bergsjö said the figures demonstrated the scale of the activity associated with just five major shows. He also noted that Live Nation promoted more than 60 concerts and sold more than 300,000 tickets in Stockholm over the summer.

Caroline Strand, deputy CEO of Stockholm Business Region, said major events are an important growth driver for Stockholm, with effects on both local spending and the city’s international competitiveness. Stockholm Live executive Kristofer Åkesson similarly pointed to the city’s arena infrastructure as a factor in attracting and hosting large international acts.

The analysis was conducted by the Experience Institute on behalf of Live Nation, Stockholm Business Region and Stockholm Live. The organizations said it measures direct visitor spending only and does not include indirect economic effects, an important limitation when interpreting the SEK 1.18 billion figure as an economic-impact estimate.
