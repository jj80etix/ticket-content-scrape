# ‘Made in the USA’ Claims for ENDS Products: Navigating FTC Standards and New State Origin Requirements

## Summary

A legal analysis of FTC "Made in USA" labeling standards and new state-level domestic-origin requirements (e.g., Alabama, Texas) affecting electronic nicotine delivery system (vaping) manufacturers. The topic is unrelated to ticketing or live events.

### Key Points
- Covers the FTC's Section 5 authority, 1997 Enforcement Policy Statement, and the Made in USA Labeling Rule (16 C.F.R. Part 323).
- Executive Order 14392 (Mar. 2026) directed the FTC to prioritize "Made in America" enforcement; the FTC sent warning letters to 7 companies in July 2026.
- Alabama and Texas now tie ENDS product directory/market access to domestic-origin manufacturing.
- Topic is vaping-industry regulatory compliance, with no ticketing/live-events relevance.

For electronic nicotine delivery system (ENDS) manufacturers and sellers, a product’s ability to claim domestic origin now carries real commercial weight: it can differentiate a brand from imported products and, in a growing number of states, determine whether a product may be sold at all. At the same time, federal scrutiny of U.S.-origin claims has intensified over the past year. In March 2026, the President issued Executive Order 14392, “ [Ensuring Truthful Advertising of Products Claiming To Be Made in America](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/03/18/2026-05383/ensuring-truthful-advertising-of-products-claiming-to-be-made-in-america),” directing the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) to prioritize enforcement against unlawful “Made in America” claims. On July 6, 2026, the FTC followed through with [warning letters](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/07/ftc-warns-companies-making-questionable-made-usa-claims) to seven companies, three of which sell e-cigarettes.

Under [Section 5 of the FTC Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/USCODE-2024-title15/USCODE-2024-title15-chap2-subchapI-sec45/summary) and the [FTC’s 1997 Enforcement Policy Statement on U.S. Origin Claims](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/enforcement-policy-statement-us-origin-claims), an unqualified “Made in USA” claim must be substantiated by evidence that the product is “all or virtually all” made in the United States. The Made in USA Labeling Rule, 16 C.F.R. Part 323, codified that standard for labels in 2021: it is an unfair or deceptive practice to label a product “Made in the United States” unless (1) final assembly or processing occurs in the United States, (2) all significant processing occurs in the United States, and (3) all or virtually all ingredients or components are made and sourced in the United States. [16 C.F.R. § 323.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-323). The rule reaches not only “made,” but also “manufactured,” “built,” “produced,” “created,” or “crafted” in the U.S., and any other unqualified, express or implied, U.S.-origin claim. 16 C.F.R. § 323.1(a).

That standard is demanding for a product category built on a global supply chain. A typical ENDS device combines a battery, coil, chipset, housing, and e-liquid, many of which are commonly sourced overseas. The FTC weighs the share of total manufacturing costs attributable to U.S. parts and processing, how far removed any foreign content is from the finished product, and the importance of that content to the product’s function. Domestic filling or final assembly, standing alone, will rarely carry an unqualified claim if the battery or atomizer is imported.

### State-Level Requirements

Some states now tie ENDS market access to where a product is made. Alabama and Texas offer two recent examples of the different forms these requirements can take; other states may have adopted, or may be considering, similar measures, and companies should confirm the requirements in each state where they sell.

##### Alabama

Effective June 1, 2025, [Alabama Code § 28-11-17.2](https://law.justia.com/codes/alabama/title-28/chapter-11/section-28-11-17-2/) provides that, beginning October 1, 2025, no e-liquid, ENDS, or alternative nicotine product may be added to the state’s ENDS Directory unless either (1) “\[t\]he product and its components are made, packaged, labeled, and manufactured in the United States,” or (2) the manufacturer has received an FDA marketing order under 21 U.S.C. § 387j(c)(1)(A)(i). The Department of Revenue may require manufacturers, wholesalers, or distributors to certify compliance with the domestic-manufacturing prong under penalty of perjury. Listing matters: under [Alabama Code § 28-11-17.1](https://law.justia.com/codes/alabama/title-28/chapter-11/section-28-11-17-1/), a manufacturer selling an unlisted product faces a $1,000 daily fine per product, and unlisted products are treated as contraband.

##### Texas

Texas takes a different approach. As amended effective September 1, 2025, [Texas Health & Safety Code § 161.0876](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/?tab=1&code=HS&chapter=HS.161&artSec=) (b)(3) makes it a Class A misdemeanor to market, advertise, sell, or offer for sale an e-cigarette product “that is wholly or partially manufactured in or marketed as being manufactured in” China or a country designated as a foreign adversary under 15 C.F.R. § 791.4. Notably, “e-cigarette product” here means the consumable liquid or other material that is vaporized, so the provision targets e-liquids and consumables rather than hardware. Texas does not require U.S. manufacture; it prohibits manufacture in specified countries.

### Where State and Federal Requirements Intersect

These regimes measure different things, and satisfying one does not establish compliance with the other. A product eligible for sale under a state origin requirement may still fail the FTC’s “all or virtually all” test. That gap creates three risks. First, a company that certifies domestic manufacture to a state may be tempted to repeat that position on packaging, converting a regulatory certification into a consumer-facing claim that the FTC will evaluate on its own terms. Second, a company may describe a product’s origin one way in a sworn state certification and another way in its FTC substantiation or marketing; any inconsistencies can undermine the company’s credibility and prompt questions from both state and federal regulators. Third, marketing that touts “not made in China or other foreign adversary countries” to reassure retailers in states like Texas can itself imply U.S. origin depending on context.

### Practical Tips for Manufacturers and Sellers

**1.****Substantiate First, Then Choose Qualified or Unqualified Language**

An unqualified claim requires a reasonable basis, at the time the claim is made, that the product is all or virtually all domestic. Where it is not, the FTC permits qualified claims, such as “Made in USA of U.S. and imported parts” or “Assembled in USA,” provided they are truthful, substantiated, and clear and prominent. Even qualified claims require meaningful U.S. content. Claims about a specific process, such as “e-liquid blended in the USA,” can be appropriate if accurate and not overstated.

**2\. Audit the Supply Chain to the Component Level**

The FTC expects marketers to look far enough back in the manufacturing process to account for any significant foreign content, and buying from a U.S. supplier does not make a part U.S.-made. Obtain written origin certifications for batteries, coils, chipsets, housings, and nicotine; document cost-of-goods calculations; and reverify whenever suppliers change.

**3\. Build One Origin Record That Serves Both State and Federal Purposes**

Maintain a single, attorney-reviewed origin file for each SKU that supports state directory certifications or eligibility requirements (such as those in Alabama and Texas) and any FTC claim. Do not let state compliance language migrate into advertising without separate FTC review.

**4\. Review Labels, Websites, and Social Media Together**

The July 2026 warning letters targeted claims across websites, social media posts, and product labeling, including “AMERICAN MADE,” “Made in America,” “Built in the USA,” and “#MadeinUSA.” Hashtags, flags, and references to American workers can convey implied claims. Imported products must also bear a country-of-origin marking under the Tariff Act, [19 U.S.C. § 1304](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/USCODE-2024-title19/USCODE-2024-title19-chap4-subtitleII-partI-sec1304), and when a label references a U.S. city or address, U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) regulations require the foreign country of origin to appear in close proximity and in at least comparable size, See [19 C.F.R. § 134.46](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-134/subpart-E/section-13446). CBP applies a “same side” rule to the proximity requirement, so a U.S. distributor address on one panel and “Product of \[Country\]” on another is a common pitfall.

**5\. Respond Promptly and Substantively to FTC Contact**

The July 2026 letters asked recipients to contact FTC staff within five business days to discuss a compliance plan and warned that Labeling Rule violations could result in consumer redress or civil penalties of up to $53,088 per violation. Although the letters state that they are not formal determinations of a violation, they may precede an investigation. Recipients should engage counsel immediately, preserve documents, assemble substantiation, suspend or qualify challenged claims across all channels, and present a concrete remediation plan. Recipients should also consider whether the same facts affect state certifications on file.

### Why It Matters

For ENDS companies, domestic-origin claims now sit at the intersection of federal consumer-protection law, customs marking rules, and state market-access statutes. The FTC’s “all or virtually all” standard remains the benchmark for any consumer-facing U.S.-origin claim, and the agency has made clear that vaping products are within its sights. The Alabama and Texas examples show that state origin rules can be stricter, looser, or simply different, and companies should monitor developments in every state where they sell. The safest course is one well-documented origin analysis, applied consistently to state certifications and federal claims. Companies should consult experienced regulatory counsel before making or certifying any domestic-origin representation to ensure their labeling and marketing are defensible under applicable law.
