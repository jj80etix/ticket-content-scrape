# Mexico Is The Futur: European Electronic Music Festival Successfully Expands In Monterrey

## Summary

Kappa FuturFestival's Italian electronic-music brand successfully expanded outside Turin for the first time, staging the inaugural FuturFestival Mexico at Parque Fundidora in Monterrey on Nov. 13–14 to a crowd of more than 18,000 across both days. The debut drew a stacked techno/house lineup despite the absence of Diplo and Skream (grounded U.S. flights), and organizers have already confirmed a 2027 edition.

### Key Points
- First FuturFestival event held outside Turin, Italy, hosted at Parque Fundidora, Monterrey.
- More than 18,000 attendees across the Nov. 13–14 debut weekend.
- Diplo and Skream missed the event due to grounded U.S. flights; lineup continued with other headliners.
- Organizers cite cultural/postindustrial-venue similarities between Turin and Monterrey as the draw.
- A 2027 Mexico edition is already confirmed.

*Parque Fundidora, a large urban park in the heart of Monterrey, has hosted a variety of major events, from one of Mexico’s largest music festivals in Pa’l Norte to Grand Prix races to United Nations summits, and the 280-acre site added another feather to its cap last weekend.*

*It hosted the inaugural FuturFestival Mexico, marking the first time the Italian electronic music festival was held outside of its home in Turin, and with more than 18,000 fans across both days, Nov. 13 and 14, according to [Rave Control](https://ravecontrol.com/blog/futur-festival-mexico-reune-a-mas-de-18-mil-personas-en-su-debut-monte), it’s safe to say the festival organizers found their segunda casa.**It was such a hit that a 2027 edition is officially in the works.*

*“Some moments feel like memories even before they’re over. Maybe it’s the music, the people, or the feeling of being exactly where you’re meant to be. Maybe we’ve done this before. Maybe we never really stopped,” FUTUR wrote in an Instagram caption following the final night of the festival. “Monterrey, thank you for being part of our first chapter. What we shared on those dancefloors will stay with us long after the music fades. This is only the beginning. See you at FuturFestival Mexico 2027.”*

*Despite not having Diplo and Skream—who were unable to travel due to the U.S. flights being grounded on Nov. 13—the festival carried on with a stacked lineup that included Âme, Marcel Dettmann, ANNA, Deer Jade, Carl Craig, Moodymann, Chloé Caillet, Cloonee, Damian Lazarus, Dennis Cruz, DJ Tennis, Dixon, DJ Holographic, FJAAK, Francis Mercier, Mahmut Orhan, KI/KI, Lilly Palmer, Maceo Plex, Tiga, Paco Osuna, Tripolism and Vintage Culture.*

*A successful event in Monterrey is not only a testament to the festival, which launched 17 years ago and has developed a loyal following with its post-industrial aesthetic at Parco Dora, but also to the growing demand for electronic music events in Mexico.*

*Gianluca Brignone, partner and chief innovation officer at Kappa FuturFestival, and Luca Pastorello of Lost Nights, who is co-producing the event in Mexico, took some time to chat with* Pollstar *about the concept of the festival, selecting Monterrey* as *the best home away from home for Futur and the bridging of Mexican and Italian cultures.*

***Pollstar*: What can you tell me about the inception of FuturFestival and how it’s evolved over the years?  
Gianluca Brignone**: The event in Italy started in 2009 as an ode to futurism, and the first big event at Parco Dura was in 2012. It’s my two partners and I, and we’re all from Turin. We’re just passionate about having a good time and giving people a safe and pleasant environment to have a good time with amazing music and great service.

The location speaks for itself, and the reason we’re doing the addition of Monterrey is that it’s the first place we found that has a very similar vibe. We want to try and replicate that culture of having a good time in an underground techno environment in Mexico. Turin had evolved, and after we went for the three-day format, that dramatically changed the outreach of the festival and allowed people from abroad to come for the long weekend. And here we are, doing something special in a new country that is very close to my heart. Luca has been living there for nearly 20 years now, and I have family in Mexico, so it is a country that is dear to my heart.

**Luca, what can you tell me about your connection to FuturFestival and how you started working with Gianluca?  
Luca Pastorello**: I have actually known one of the partners of Futur since primary school, and he was a best man at the wedding of one of my best friends; his girlfriend was one of my best friends; his adopted father was one of the best friends of my father. So it’s a long story.

We met officially about 14 years ago during New Year’s Eve, and we all became friends and started to talk. We started looking for a place to do a festival, but we couldn’t find it, so we stayed in touch and figured that when the time was right, something would happen. And then, the idea of Monterrey came up. We started to see the similarities between Turin and Monterrey, and we believed that we were meant to do this together. It’s going to happen.

**I imagine most try to pursue something in Mexico City, which is one of the most important markets in the live business. What attracted you guys to Monterrey?  
**Brignone: Monterrey, for us, was very driven by the brand. The location is another steel foundry, just like the location in Turin. Our site in Turin is in a park in the middle of the city called Parco Dora, and in Monterrey it’s called Fundidora. They’re both industrial cities, and we love the fact that it’s not a main market. It ticked a lot of boxes in a lot of ways, and the park is well-rounded and has hosted other kinds of events.

There was also a hole in the market to do something like Futur in Monterrey.

**Pastorello**: The most important thing for us was the venue, and the postindustrial setting was the closest we could find in Mexico that defined FuturFestival because the Turin event is defined by the postindustrial towers seen in videos and photos. This is why we decided to call Parque Fundidora our home. It’s a great choice.

Monterrey is known for its regional music, some rock and a little bit of pop, but it’s not a cradle of electronic music…until now. Monterrey is also very close to Mexican states like Coahuila, Tamaulipas, and the southern part of the U.S. We thought, logistically, it would allow us to bring our kind of music and culture.

We established a relationship with TEC de Monterrey, which is one of the most important universities in Latin America. The city really received us with their arms wide open, and we needed to show them what we do and explain our magic, our music, preparations and our activities so they could better understand the feeling of Futur because we were trying to appeal to young students, professionals and family people.

Parque Fundidora in Monterrey, Mexico, was an ideal site for the postindustrial technomusic festival because it resembled the venue in Italy. (Courtesy FuturFestival)

**Leading up to the event, how was the response?  
Pastorello**: We saw everybody in the university community and the administration of Monterrey embrace the movement, and we offered a wide selection of activities. We associated ourselves with a lot of studios, gyms, and other activities because we believe that techno is part of the world and is becoming a very important part of training and a wealth of related activities. We pushed a lot, and we found a lot of acceptance, with people getting to know about techno from a health and wellness perspective.

**There’s also a cultural significance to the event and a sense of responsibility, bridging Italian and Mexican cultures and celebrating their similarities.  
Brignone**: I think the Italian dolce vita \[sweet life\] and the way Mexicans do it are very similar. Both cultures enjoy good times, and there are a lot of parallels that go from food culture to music culture. It just feels natural for us \[to expand in Mexico\] and bring how we do things in Italy as much as possible in Monterrey. We want to cross-pollinate the cultures as much as possible, whether it’s through working with the Italian chamber of commerce or including the Mexican DJs in the lineup, and there’s a good number of them.

We’re trying to follow the same playbook we used at the beginning of our adventure in Turin, where we really focused on up-and-coming and local DJs because we wanted to get the local crowd to participate and get them excited for these artists.

**Pastorello**: Besides the Italian and Mexican culture, we have something else that’s very central to the project, which is the environmental culture. It’s important that everybody we are talking to, from every age, knows that Futur is the first festival in the world that has, on each ticket—paid for by us, the promoters, and not the fans—environmental credits for projects that are happening in Mexico. You may have heard of carbon credits, which are credits that offset carbon emissions from a company, but environmental credits are a new way of thinking about not only mitigating the impact that an industrial and economic activity can have but also regenerating the environment through real projects like reforestation, regenerating the soil, and cleaning the water. We have a positive reaction from our fans and people going to the festival because of the actions we are doing. And Gianluca is on the board of directors for a company we partner with that works on these projects.

**Brignone**: For many years, I’ve been on the board of Global Inheritance, a nonprofit based in L.A. I known the founder, Eric Ritz, for years and we worked on a lot of projects together, and they do a lot of sustainability programs at Coachella, and we’ve been running TRASHed \[which does art installations made out of recycled items and gives fans prizes for bringing items to recycle\] for 13 years, so we decided to get them involved in Mexico. We’ll be running TRASHed and getting some local artists involved to include these action-driven programs that make an impact. It also allows for more engagement.

**I’m sure there’s a challenge in finding the right balance for the festival when it comes to its offerings and identity because you’re expanding an Italian brand in Mexico, which happens to be very close to the United States. How do you overcome such challenges to ensure you create something that caters to everyone?  
Brignone**: We’re really trying to bring the same level of production value, service, and quality that we’ve worked hard to establish for many years because, at the end of the day, it’s one of the fundamentals of any business. We do have a good number of Mexicans and Americans coming from Texas and whatnot, so you’re going to see a trickle-down effect if we do things right. We have to do it right this year, and keep doing it right to build up what we believe is going to be the little brother of Turin.

**Pastorello**: One important cultural characteristic of Monterrey is the carne asada, which is essentially barbecue. So we welcome our artists with a surprise in the green room with a huge barbecue from Toro Negro. It’s an industrial barbecue with a chef from Monterrey giving them a carnita asada. We got artists from the U.S., Europe, Brazil, and areas all over the world, so we’re going to give them a piece of Mexican carne asada.

**Brignone**: Italian and Mexican hospitality go hand in hand, so when you put the two together, it’s going to be pretty good.

**Is there something you’re able to do in Mexico that you can’t in Italy?  
Brignone**: We can go a little later, which is good for our fans. Italy has a curfew at 11:59 p.m. because we have buildings less than 250 feet away. We’re in the middle of a neighborhood, so we have very strict limits on the decibels.

Mexico is a little more lenient, so our sound system is one of a kind.

**Pastorello**: Also, French audio system producer L-Acoustics is a partner, and we’re using a system that is comparable to IMAX systems in cinemas. It’s a 360-degree immersive system.

**How do you build on this event?  
Brignone**: Our focus is Mexico and Turn, and also next year because we have already started that process.

**Luca, with your experience in Mexico, how has the electronic music culture in the country grown over the years?  
Pastorello**: There are areas like Mexico City that have a huge underground community, and there are festivals out there, but also small clubs opening in different areas.

What we did was produce something called Way To Futur, organizing smaller events leading up to the festival. We had 1,000 people in the community of San Pedro in Monterrey, pumping and dancing. We had another in Tulum and Mexico City. The idea was to show people what we’re doing, the music we like to bring to the market.

We see the industry growing a lot, and you can see this in the U.S. today, and part of it is the exercise in listening to electronic entertainment. People raving burn more calories than people running. The music is expanding because of the social interactions, too.
