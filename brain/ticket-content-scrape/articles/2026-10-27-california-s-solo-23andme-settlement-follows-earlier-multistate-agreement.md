# California’s Solo 23andMe Settlement Follows Earlier Multistate Agreement

## Summary

California Attorney General Rob Bonta announced a $6 million settlement with Chrome Holding Co. (23andMe's successor) resolving claims tied to the 2023 data breach and the company's bankruptcy-related sale of genetic data, following a separate multistate settlement with 42 other AGs.

### Key Points
- $6M settlement announced Oct. 19, 2026; resolves California's May 2026 lawsuit.
- Stems from a 2023 data breach affecting ~6.9 million customers.
- Follows a prior multistate settlement with 42 other state AGs.
- Data-privacy/bankruptcy matter unrelated to ticketing.

On October 19, 2026, California Attorney General (AG) Rob Bonta announced a $6 million [settlement](https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/attachments/press-docs/chrome-2760-joint-stipulation.pdf) with Chrome Holding Co., the successor to the genetic testing company 23andMe. The settlement resolves a [lawsuit](https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/attachments/press-docs/People%20v%20Chrome%20Holding%20fka%2023andMe%20et%20al.%20-%20Stamped%20Complaint.pdf) that California filed against the company in May 2026 arising from a 2023 data breach as well as California’s objections to 23andMe’s sale of consumers’ genetic data as part of the company’s bankruptcy proceedings. California’s settlement comes on the heels of a recent settlement with the 42 other state AGs who filed proofs of claim in the bankruptcy proceedings. (See our coverage of the July 2026 multistate settlement [here](https://www.regulatoryoversight.com/2026/07/state-ags-reach-novel-multistate-settlement-with-genetic-testing-company-23andme/).)

### Background

In 2023, 23andMe announced a data breach that exposed the private data of approximately 6.9 million customers. The breach resulted in class-action litigation and investigations by state AGs.

23andMe filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 2025. Forty-three state AGs eventually filed proofs of claim, reportedly seeking approximately $100 billion in damages.

The bankruptcy court ultimately approved the sale of 23andMe’s assets to a nonprofit formed by 23andMe’s founder and former CEO. The court approved the sale notwithstanding [concerns from state AGs](https://www.regulatoryoversight.com/2026/10/state-ags-set-child-safety-and-privacy-expectations-for-any-sale-of-character-ai/) regarding the sale of consumers’ genetic information, but the terms of the sale agreement ultimately addressed many of their concerns.

In May 2026, after the bankruptcy plan had been approved, California filed a state court lawsuit against Chrome Holding. The complaint asserted violations of California’s Genetic Information Privacy Act, California Consumer Privacy Act, and false advertising and unfair competition laws.

On July 10, 2026, the bankruptcy court ruled that California was barred from seeking monetary relief against Chrome Holding in the state court case.

### The Multistate and California Settlements

On July 14, 2026, 42 state AGs announced the resolution of their claims against Chrome Holding. Though the multistate settlement allowed $150 million in claims, it capped the total monetary payout from the company’s bankruptcy funds at $18 million. That recovery was divided among the participating states, with recoveries ranging from $1.27 million for Texas to $149,339 for South Dakota.

Some states — including Connecticut and Indiana, which co-led the multistate group — received awards that appear to reflect their investment of resources in the 23andMe investigation and litigation, rather than merely reflecting the number of affected consumers in their states.

Meanwhile, California continued to litigate various issues relating to 23andMe’s bankruptcy and the remainder of its state-court lawsuit until the California-only settlement was announced on October 19, 2026. The bankruptcy judge approved that settlement on October 26, 2026.

California’s settlement includes a $6 million payment to the state. This amount likely exceeds what California would have received if it had participated in the earlier multistate settlement. Unlike the multistate settlement, under which states generally have broad discretion in how they use their recoveries, California’s settlement commits its recovery to providing consumer relief from the 2023 breach and related costs, with any funds that are unexpended after one year reverting to the state for purposes of consumer protection enforcement. California obtained no additional injunctive relief as part of its settlement.

### Takeaways

California’s decision to resolve its claims separately from the multistate settlement with Chrome Holding (f/k/a 23andMe) highlights one of the main reasons states opt out of multistate resolutions: to hold out for the prospect of a larger financial recovery. In this case, that strategy appears to have paid off, with California securing a payment that likely exceeds what it could have obtained through participation in the multistate resolution.

Larger, well-resourced states like California are among the most likely to pursue this strategy. Here, California’s ability to secure its own settlement appears to have depended in part on its decision to file a separate state-court lawsuit against the company and the prospect of California pursuing further appeals of decisions by the bankruptcy court. Other states may not have been able to commit the litigation resources necessary to justify a stand-alone settlement like California’s.

For companies facing multistate investigations, the 23andMe resolution is a reminder that a multistate settlement may not resolve exposure to every state. Companies should assess early whether any state is likely to pursue its own litigation and factor that risk into negotiation strategy and settlement timing.
