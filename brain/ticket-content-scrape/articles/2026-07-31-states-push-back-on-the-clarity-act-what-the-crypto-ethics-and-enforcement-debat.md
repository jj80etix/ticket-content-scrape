# States Push Back on the Clarity Act: What the Crypto Ethics and Enforcement Debate Means for Compliance Teams

## Summary

NY AG Letitia James testified July 28 urging Congress to amend the pending federal CLARITY Act so it doesn't preempt state authority to prosecute crypto fraud, pushing for a two-year post-office ethics ban enforceable by state regulators, application of AML/money-transmission rules to platforms claiming decentralization, and preserved state securities/antifraud enforcement. Bill sponsors defend the current ethics provisions as historic, and the 600+ page bill needs Democratic votes to pass before the August recess — support that is currently in doubt.

### Key Points
- NY AG Letitia James (July 28 testimony) wants the Clarity Act amended to preserve state enforcement of securities, antifraud, and consumer-protection laws.
- She's pushing to extend the post-office ethics ban to two years and let state/local regulators (not just the federal AG) enforce it.
- Bill sponsor Sen. Cynthia Lummis (R-WY) and a White House official defend the current ethics language as the "most comprehensive... in history."
- The 600+ page bill merges Senate Banking/Agriculture drafts and needs Democratic support to pass before the August recess, which is in doubt.
- As drafted, ethics-provision enforcement rests solely with the (presidentially appointed) US Attorney General — no state AG or private right of action.
- James also wants AML/money-transmission rules applied to platforms claiming decentralized/autonomous governance, not just centrally-controlled ones.

### Highlights
- NY AG James's July 28 testimony seeks state-enforcement carve-outs from federal preemption.
- Bill must pass before the August 2026 recess; Democratic support currently short.
- Ethics-provision enforcement, as drafted, rests solely with the US Attorney General — no state or private enforcement route.

### Industry Problem
The Clarity Act's federal-preemption fight determines whether state regulators keep authority over crypto-asset fraud and registration — directly relevant to Q4/Q5 because unresolved preemption and enforcement-structure questions are exactly the kind of regulatory uncertainty that keeps credible blockchain-ticketing use cases (provenance, resale royalties) in pilot/hype limbo rather than production.

### Proposed Solution
No ticketing-specific solution is proposed in the piece; the appropriate posture is Monitor — watch whether the Act passes before recess and whether the final preemption scope clarifies or further clouds the regulatory footing for any blockchain-based ticketing features Etix might evaluate long-term.

On July 28, 2026, New York Attorney General (AG) Letitia James submitted written testimony in connection with the pending federal Clarity Act, which aims to regulate the crypto market. In her testimony, she urged federal lawmakers to amend the Clarity Act to preserve the authority of the states to protect consumers, prosecute fraud, and hold government officials accountable for profiting from unlawful crypto-related activities. Her testimony came as the subcommittee’s ranking member, Senator Richard Blumenthal (D-Conn.), convened a public hearing examining alleged gaps in the bill, with particular focus on its ethics and enforcement provisions.

James argued that the Clarity Act, as currently written, “seeks to interfere with and preempt states’ investor protection laws as well as dilute our ability to prosecute fraud,” even as crypto fraud complaints continue to climb. She called on Congress to extend the ethics ban to cover a two-year period after officials leave public service, permit state and local regulators to enforce ethics provisions, apply anti-money laundering and money-transmission frameworks to all crypto platforms including those claiming to be decentralized, require exchanges to maintain surveillance programs and consumer protections comparable to those under the Electronic Fund Transfer Act (EFTA), and refrain from preempting state securities, commodities registration, and antifraud laws. Supporters of the bill, including bill sponsor Senator Cynthia Lummis (R-WY) and a White House official, defended the ethics provision as “the most comprehensive and wide-ranging ethics provision in history” and warned that Democratic opposition risks blocking this historic legislation.

The Clarity Act, now a 600-plus-page bill that was recently amended and rereleased by Senate Republicans last week, merges the legislation drafting of the Senate Banking and Agriculture Committees and adds new law enforcement and ethics provisions. The bill requires support from Democratic senators to pass before the August recess, which may be challenging because those Democrats who supported earlier versions of the bill have said the latest text falls short of satisfactorily addressing their concerns. By way of example, the new ethics provision at issue would ban public officials, their employees, and their spouses from issuing or sponsoring digital assets, but enforcement would rest exclusively with the U.S. AG (an appointed executive department official) with no enforcement by the state AGs and no private right of action. This is a substantial obstacle because Senate Republicans are unlikely to amend the bill in this way out of concern that Democratic states would attempt to prosecute the President.

### Why It Matters

This debate carries implications that extend well beyond its political framing, across at least three dimensions that regulatory counsel and compliance teams operating in the crypto space should track closely.

- **Federal preemption of state law remains an obstacle to new federal regulatory frameworks.** The most consequential unresolved question in the Clarity Act is not the ethics language — it is the scope of federal preemption. Federal preemption of state-level enforcement and state law is a concept that has been an obstacle in the negotiations between federal and state regulators in other contexts, such as a federal comprehensive consumer privacy act.
	- On the one hand, uniform federal regulations that preempt state law in the market provide stability and uniformity that reduce compliance costs and create a framework for nationwide enforcement — potentially ensuring all citizens have legal protection from unlawful crypto-related activity.
	- On the other hand, James and other state officials are pushing back on provisions that would displace state securities laws, commodities registration requirements, and antifraud statutes. As currently drafted, preemption is not absolute and states retain some enforcement authority.
	- Assuming the partial/hybrid preemption provisions survive as a compromise position, companies operating in the crypto space may face narrower regulated activity at the state level, but they should not assume that means reduced compliance risk.
- **Decentralized platforms should not assume they are outside the regulatory perimeter.** James specifically urged Congress to apply anti-money laundering and money-transmission frameworks to platforms that claim to operate through autonomous software, not just those effectively controlled by a person or small group. That position reflects a broader regulatory trend of pursuing function over form by looking at who benefits rather than the entity that engages in the activity. Many platforms that have designed their governance structures to avoid regulatory classification should treat James’ testimony as a signal that state and federal regulators are attempting to close that gap.
- **The enforcement structure of the ethics provision may matter more than its substance.** The opposition’s criticism is that enforcement is meaningless if the president’s appointed AG is the only regulator with authority to enforce the law. This is a structural critique that applies beyond the crypto context. For compliance professionals, the legislative battle over enforcement mechanisms, and states’ increasingly viewing federal enforcement as insufficient compared to state-level enforcement, represent a remarkable shift in power sharing between federal and state regulators, giving states significantly more power than in previous decades. Companies that rely on federal preemption to limit their exposure to state enforcement actions should monitor how regulators and Congress evolve in this respect. If federal legislators craft any role for state enforcement, it will be worth watching whether that role functions as a ceiling or a floor.
