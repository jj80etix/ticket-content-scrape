# A 26,000-cap bet on Guadalajara: OCESA opens Coliseo GNP Seguros

## Summary

OCESA opened Coliseo GNP Seguros, a converted 55-year-old university stadium in Guadalajara now booked to hold 14,000–26,000 depending on configuration, with Michael Bublé as its debut headliner. International events director Memo Parra says calendar control — not raw capacity — is the venue's main advantage over Mexico's larger, club-controlled football stadiums.

### Key Points
- Venue can flex from ~15,000 (arena config) to 26,000 (floor standing), versus a publicized "30,000" figure.
- OCESA books directly with the owning university rather than negotiating around football-club and federation calendars.
- Guadalajara previously lacked a venue at this scale; existing rooms topped out around 11,500.
- Parra rules out treating the venue as Corona Capital festival overflow, citing risk to the festival's draw.

## Transcript

The converted 55-year-old university ground now allows a 14,000 to 26,000 capacity but OCESA says availability matters more than size

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/smush-webp/2026/09/MICHAELBUBLE-FOTOLILIANAESTRADA1-scaled.jpg.webp)

Coliseo GNP Seguros © OCESA

Michael Bublé opened Coliseo GNP Seguros on Tuesday in front of 19,400 people, bringing into service a 55-year-old university football ground in Guadalajara that OCESA has spent the past year converting into a music venue.

For Memo Parra, OCESA’s international events director, the most valuable thing about it is not how many people it holds. It is that nobody else controls the calendar.

Mexico’s large football stadiums hold between 48,000 and 55,000, but availability runs through the clubs and the national team. “You don’t have to negotiate with the club and with the Mexican Football Federation to get dates,” says Parra. The Coliseo, owned by the Universidad Autónoma de Guadalajara and operated by OCESA, works to a calendar agreed directly with the university.

Another result was that Bublé played a stage that, for the first time in the building’s 55 years, did not have to be built on the grass.

**A range, not a number**  
The figure attached to the venue since its announcement is 30,000. Parra describes something more granular: around 15,000 in an arena-style configuration that still reads as full; 20,000 with the floor fully seated, and up to 26,000 with the floor standing.

The range he books for is 14,000 to 26,000. “You can play with the capacities,” he says. “The stadium doesn’t feel big, and that’s a huge advantage.”

That intimacy is the pitch. “You can have a big show and the stadium still feels cosy,” Parra says. “Every one of those artists left here saying how good it feels to have the audience so close.”

In its previous form, as Estadio 3 de Marzo, the ground has hosted the likes of Roger Waters, Coldplay, Metallica, Lady Gaga and Shakira.

The design supports the point. Mexico City firm TaAU, which drew up the master plan, cut back a section of terrace and built a permanent stage slab 60 metres wide by 25 metres deep, moving the stage off the pitch for the first time.

Previously every show meant building on the grass, losing roughly a quarter of the field and blanking out the stand behind it. Architect Óscar Rodríguez Castañeda says seated capacity did not grow much in the renovation; what changed was comfort, circulation, and the ability to leave the stage standing between shows.

OCESA has made this move before. Its Estadio GNP Seguros in Mexico City, featured in [*IQ* ’s Stadium Stars](https://www.iqmagazine.com/featured-list/stadium-stars-2026/estadio-gnp-seguros/) 2026, was rebuilt around permanently integrated infrastructure — stages, dressing rooms, and press facilities — that removed the need for temporary builds. Guadalajara is the same approach at a different scale.

> “Tours that play multiple arenas in the United States could fit here”

**What fits**  
“Tours that play multiple arenas in the United States could fit here,” says Parra, also mentioning that the venue is also targeting some acts “that are bigger than an arena.” But he is equally clear about the limit. “There are artists who, however much capacity we’d like, have an arena show. It won’t work here. And there are artists who will want to do it here.”

On the balance between English and Spanish-language programming, there is no quota. “It’s what the market is asking for and what the artists on offer make possible,” he says. In a given year it can tilt either way, depending on who is touring and where Mexico falls in their routing.

The routing logic is follows too. Though only a 30,000-cap space, he explains the capacity of the venue suits the typical population-adjusted crowd sizes expected in Guadalajara for even the biggest tours.

“If you play Estadio GNP in Mexico City and sell 60,000, you can sell 20,000 or 25,000 in Guadalajara,” Parra says.

That turns a single Mexican date into two, but the caveats are commercial rather than physical; some artists only want Mexico City, some want all three cities, and some cannot be paid in Guadalajara what they are paid in the capital.

Parra resists the idea that the venue exists because the capital is saturated. “Every artist is a world of its own,” he says. “It’s not a response to anything. It’s having one more arm.”

> “If you play Estadio GNP in Mexico City and sell 60,000, you can sell 20,000 or 25,000 in Guadalajara”

**The festival stays separate**  
One thing the venue is not, Parra says, is an overflow room for Corona Capital, the Mexico City festival he founded. Gorillaz, who headline Corona Capital in November, play the Coliseo on 9 December, and the show sold well. But Parra rejects the pattern as a model. If festival headliners are routinely spread across the country, audiences who currently travel stop travelling.

“Then the people who travel from Guadalajara won’t come to Corona Capital, because they’ll have them in Guadalajara,” he says. Some artists, he adds, are booked to appear only at the festival, precisely to keep it singular. Festivals depend on visitors, not only on their host city.

He also rules out a Guadalajara edition. “The festival doesn’t fit,” he says. A festival needs green space and room to move, which a stadium does not provide, and an earlier attempt in the city did not take because the festival’s personality was not the city’s.

**The missing tier**  
At a formal opening earlier in the day, Jalisco governor Pablo Lemus framed the venue as the gap in the state’s inventory. Guadalajara already had rooms at 2,000, 7,000, 10,000 and 11,500 capacity; what it lacked was a venue at this scale and standard.

He described the metropolitan area as the entertainment capital of western Mexico, drawing audiences from neighbouring states including Colima, Nayarit, San Luis Potosí and Michoacán, and pointed to direct air links to Madrid, with a Paris route announced.

Parra makes a similar argument about position. “Mexico is one of the most important countries for the live music industry,” he says. “That’s why OCESA is the second or third promoter depending on the year.” Mexico City and its surrounding metropolitan area hold some 25 million people and will always be a different market on size alone. “The idea is for Guadalajara to be a more important market than it was before.”
