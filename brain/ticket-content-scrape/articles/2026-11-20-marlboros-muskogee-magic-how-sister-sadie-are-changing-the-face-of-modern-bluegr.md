# Marlboros, Muskogee & Magic: How Sister Sadie Are Changing The Face of Modern Bluegrass & Beyond

## Summary

Feature profile on all-female bluegrass supergroup Sister Sadie, tracing their evolution from a 2012 one-off jam at Nashville's Station Inn to IBMA Group/Entertainer of the Year winners with a new Grammy-nominated album, *No Fear*.

### Key Points
- Sister Sadie won IBMA Entertainer of the Year in 2020, the first all-female band to do so.
- Current lineup spans ages 23-55 and includes Deanie Richardson, Gena Britt, Jaelee Roberts, Dani Flowers, Rainey Miatke and Katie Blomarz-Kimball.
- The band has expanded into festivals like Bourbon and Beyond, Delfest and Grey Fox, and a slot on the Cayamo Cruise.
- A full album addressing themes like misogyny and ageism is due next year.

## Transcript

![sister sadie](https://static.pollstar.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/sister-sadie-scaled.jpg)

Photo by Austin Koontz

When Deanie Richardson and Gena Britt called Dale Ann Bradley, Tina Adair and Beth Lawrence to come play Nashville’s storied The Station Inn, it was all about friends getting together to play some songs, have a few laughs and celebrate the combustive musicality that defines bluegrass. That it was all women made the gig – fourteen years ago – somewhat unique, but none of the musicians thought anything of it.

“We were all available, and we’d never done it,” Richardson recalls. “It seemed like something fun to do. And we did it a few more times, because it was fun. But then we started getting calls, people asking if we wanted to come play. Most of us had other obligations, so when we could, we would.”

For Richardson, 55, those obligations included road work with Bob Seger, the Chieftains, Vince Gill and Patty Loveless, as well as the Grand Ole Opry band’s alternate fiddler. The women didn’t care. In a male dominated genre, they were providing a fresh perspective – and could explore the genre they’d all grown up playing in new ways and with searing chops.

It was important to the women, but – like women everywhere – they had other obligations. Still *Sister Sadie* struck a chord, and they won the International Bluegrass Music Association’s 2019 Group of the Year. That same year *Sister Sadie II* was nominated for the Best Bluegrass Album Grammy.

![2X9A0383](https://static.pollstar.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/2X9A0383-scaled.jpeg)

As 2020 rolled in and COVID closed America down, Sister Sadie did the unthinkable. Not only did they repeat the IBMA Vocal Group win, but they became the first all-female band to win the IBMA’s Entertainer of the Year. For something that started out as a one-off for five friends, Sister Sadie was turning into a very real proposition – at a time when touring and festivals, the lifeblood of the bluegrass genre, was impossible.

“We were all sitting at home in our jammies,” Richardson, who became only the second woman in 30 years to win Fiddler of the Year that same night, recalls, “and we’re watching the awards. It didn’t seem real, and it was a lot to take in. Even stranger was the fact we weren’t together, and we weren’t able to celebrate or go play.”

Of course, when you have a stone-cold bluegrass legend like Dale Ann Bradley, then a six-time Female Vocalist of the Year, in your band, or a solo force of nature like Tina Adair, trying to align schedules can be tricky. For the group whose name came from Tony Rice’s “Little Sadie” – with an inspirational assist by Shawn Camp’s “Sis Draper” – ultimately the future came down to who wanted to be in a band, wanted to commit the time and really see if there was a place for a multi-generational, all-female group to not just exist, but thrive.

Now able to laugh about the evolution, Richardson concedes, “Over the last few years, but especially the last two, we went from being a Seldom Scene kind of thing to being an all-in, full-commitment situation. It’s a lot and a big leap, but it’s also a reality we couldn’t have dreamed back then. But the more we played, the more we dreamed, the more this felt like the right thing to do.”

![[VzIUegL9XYg]]

When you consider Sister Sadie’s updated line-up anchored this year’s IBMA finale – sharing the stage with Patty Loveless, Billy Strings, Sam Bush, Sierra Hull and Ronnie McCoury – for a spirited “The Boys Are Back In Town” and “Goldrush” to honor the Nashville Bluegrass Band and Byron Berline going into the Hall of Fame, committing to the band created a whole other dynamic. Now made of Richardson on fiddle, Britt on banjo and vocals, plus Jaelee Roberts on vocals and guitar, Dani Flowers on vocals and guitar, who both joined in 2020 and 2022 respectively, Rainey Miatke on mandolin and vocals in 2024 and newest comer Katie Blomarz-Kimball on bass, who joined in 2025, Sister Sadie represents not just the past, but the future of what progressive bluegrass, Americana and even country can contain.

“I love working with great musicians, because when you hear how they play, it really inspires you to dig deep,” says Country Music Hall of Famer Patty Loveless of the group. “Sharing the stage with great talent—and I’ve been blessed to sing with Vince Gill, George Jones, so many heroes—I can tell you Sister Sadie is as true and as talented as musicians, not just vocalists, *as anyone.*

“When they asked me to come sing ‘The Boys Are Back In Town’ with Sister Sadie, the answer was an absolute yes. It takes me back to Kentucky, to my bluegrass roots – and being women making music is the greatest.”

![IMG 1386](https://static.pollstar.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/IMG_1386.jpg)

Sister Sadie with Patty Loveless at this year’s IBMA Awards in Chattanooga, TN. (Courtesy Sister Sadie)

With their *No Fear* release, Sister Sadie scored their second Best Bluegrass Album Grammy nomination – proving it wasn’t just kudos to a supergroup. Along the way, they’ve also expanded their touring, picking up appearances at the progressive Grey Fox, Rockygrass, Bourbon and Beyond, Merlefest, Delfest and Bristol Rhythm & Roots festivals, as well as a slot on the Adult Alternative-Americana-leaning Cayamo Cruise.

Steve Lassiter, President of Reliant Talent, signed Del McCoury and realized there was a stripe of bluegrass performers who could work across genres. As he says, “It’s not always what you do on that stage, it’s how the audience *responds* to what you do on that stage. Whether it’s DelFest or opening up for Deana Carter at the Ryman Auditorium, these women hit the stage hard – and it’s not just one singer, but multiple singers, the harmonies and the musicianship.

“I am amazed when they get standing ovations before their set is over, but then I think about the caliber of playing, and people just aren’t expecting it. You get a great show, yes, but you get so much more because Sister Sadie comes to play their instruments in a way I could see them in front of Widespread Panic, Tyler Childers or Sturgill Simpson, Daniel Donato’s Cosmic Country or even Cody Jinks.”

That diversity comes organically. With Britt and Richardson being in their 50s, the rest of Sister Sadie’s ages run from 23 to 37. For vocalist Dani Flowers, who wrangled with a major label deal and tutelage from Lee Ann Womack and producer Frank Liddell in the early 2000s, it’s the differences that make the band so powerful. “As a songwriter, there’s something deeply personal about bringing a song to this band and hearing what it becomes in their hands. When I look across the stage, I see women of different generations and vastly different backgrounds making something together none of us could make alone.

![lauraschneiderphoto SisterSadie 068](https://static.pollstar.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/lauraschneiderphoto_SisterSadie_-068-scaled.jpg)

Photo by Laura Schneider

“Every single one of my bandmates is a master at what she does, but we’re all so different, those things press us all in new directions. And we empower each other to speak our truth, to feel safe and confident to go further.”

While bluegrass has always been a genre of murder, cheating or desire denied, as well as dead babies, lost loves and salvation, Sister Sadie has taken on incest, misogyny, agism and the impossible beauty standards leveraged at women. With a full album due next year, the Sadies have put their truths where their musicianship is, writing and finding songs from their friends that reflect the snares and obstacles facing women, as well as songs of hope and reason.

“Marlboros in Muskogee,” which was just released, has the finger-picked ease and lush harmonies that suggests Laurel Canyon’s peaceful evening feelings. Written by Flowers and Richardson with Country Music Association and Academy of Country Music Song of the Year winners Jon Randall and Jessi Alexander, their travelogue of chasing a dream is as accurate a road song as much of Jackson Browne’s *Running On Empty*.

Lassiter marvels at the song’s origins. “When they were writing ‘Marlboros in Muskogee,’ I was looking at their itinerary, thinking, ‘I’m going to kill these girls. This is brutal.’ But instead, they got a song out of it.”

![IMG 1391](https://static.pollstar.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/IMG_1391.jpg)

One Night Of Standards: Sister Sadie Performing at the Grand Ole Opry with Ashley McBryde, who was featured on Sister Sadie’s No Fear album. (Courtesy Sister Sadie)

“When we celebrated the opening of the Bruce Springsteen Center for American Music with our “Music America” concerts in June, we wanted the lineup to reflect the breadth and diversity of American musical traditions, just like the exhibits in the Center,” says Melissa Kozlowski, Director of Curatorial Affairs, where Sister Sadie performed as part of the first night’s all-star concert that included Springsteen, Rosanne Cash, Kenny Chesney, Trombone Shorty, Shemekia Copeland and Dropkick Murphys

“While Bruce Springsteen is at the heart of our institution, our mission extends far beyond any one artist or genre. Sister Sadie helped us make that point, and make it loudly, right from the beginning. Their presence celebrated bluegrass as an essential part of America’s musical heritage, while also demonstrating that these traditions are very much alive and evolving. Having such talented women onstage was also a meaningful reflection of our commitment to highlighting voices and stories that have too often been overlooked in traditional music histories.”

That kind of endorsement follows a tradition built on one generation encouraging the next. For Britt and Richardson, both women played in bluegrass bands with Jaelee Roberts’ parents; Britt with her mother in the all-female Petticoat Junction, Richardson in the New Tradition with her father, who also accompanied the 13-, 14-year old in fiddle contests.

![IMG 1384](https://static.pollstar.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/IMG_1384.jpg)

Sister Sadie were featured in the Country Music Hall of Fame in Nashville as a part of their American Currents series, (Courtesy Sister Sadie)

“I remember when Jaelee was born. She came out of the womb singing,” Richardson says. “And her voice has so much of that Linda Ronstadt tone and emotional expression, it stops you in your tracks. Her gift is so natural, she sang like that as a kid.”

Paying it forward, Richardson helped run the Kids of Bluegrass program at the annual IBMA World of Bluegrass conference. Among the young ‘grassers who passed through their hands: Molly Tuttle, Sarah Jarosz, Sierra Hull and Billy Strings’ mandolin player Jarrod Walker, as well as Jaelee.

“I’m 55. I’ve been on private jets, fancy tour buses. I’ve done it all,” Richardson offers. “We’re out there pulling a trailer, but when we hired Jaelee and Dani, then Katie and Rainy, it’s inspiring every day in ways you can’t imagine. To have lived it is one thing, but to be my age and see it through the eyes of girls in their 20s and 30s, it’s really magical. And they’re such good players, who’re so hungry, they push us in ways that keep me fired up.

“Whether people know bluegrass or not, they recognize the caliber of playing. We’re leaning into it now even more. When someone’s getting a capo or dealing with a broken string, I’ll walk over to Rainy, who just won the IBMA multi-instrumentalist of the year, and start jamming. She’s a badass mandolin player – and people get so caught up in it, they don’t realize we’re fixing something. To me, that’s what this is all about.”

![IMG 1385](https://static.pollstar.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/IMG_1385.jpg)

Sister Sadie with manager Ariel Rosemberg of Rainmaker Management and booking agent Ellen Larson from Reliant Talent Agency. (Courtesy Sister Sadie)

Rainy, 23, whose mandolin is a catalyst, is just as effusive. “Being in Sister Sadie has immersed me in experiences that’ve not only shaped the way I view the world, but also the way I view myself. One of the most profound self-realizations I’ve had is that I’ve become a woman that I used to look up to.

“There have been countless times at the merch table after our shows where I’ve met little girls who approach shyly with stars in their eyes, exactly the way I did. In a world still largely and tragically governed by systemic patriarchal values, this is an invaluable thing. I feel incredibly grateful to be alive at a time when some of the most skilled musicians in this genre are women, and I get to be in a band with some of the most talented and creative.”

Blomarz-Kimball, the bass player who comes from the improvisational world of jazz, concurs. “This is being part of bluegrass history. As someone who doesn’t come from this, I am very aware of how special this group of women is. We come from different influences, but come together to represent the change and growth in the genre. Our music continues the innovation every iteration of this band has brought, and we continue to create space for young bluegrassers to thrive for years to come.”

![[70NUAFMTLH8]]

As Britt, the other founder, marvels, “We all have our own experience and influences, and there’s something about putting six women who all have their own musical identity together that creates something so unique. Those differences have made this something so much bigger, and it’s exciting watching the music and the possibilities grow.”

“Sadie is her own entity,” Richardson admits. “We *all* work for Sadie, and she’s a great boss. It’s all about the music with her; how we play it, grow it, present it. Every single one of us comes in here with our best – and when one of us doesn’t quite have it, we lift each other up – but our best isn’t anything like what happens when we come together.

“To hear Dani, who’s the biggest Emmylou and Lee Ann Womack person, or Jaelee, who’s one of the three best singers I’ve ever worked with, get to sing the kinds of songs we’re seeking? To me, we’ve got a body of work that’s starting to speak for all women anywhere, about stuff that matters – and it’s not that we’re looking to lock men out, but maybe this is where we share the reality of what it means to be a woman in today’s America.”
