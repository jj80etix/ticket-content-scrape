# Massachusetts House Omits Ticket Resale Cap as Consumer Concerns Mount

## Summary

Massachusetts' House stripped Gov. Healey's proposed 110%-of-price ticket resale cap and 10% marketplace-fee cap out of a $2.35B supplemental budget (152-0 vote, Oct. 21), though a similar Senate-passed version remains alive in a House-Senate conference committee on a separate economic development bill. Consumer advocates and a resale-industry trade group are raising concerns about enforcement gaps and legal challenges, citing California's shelved AB 1720 and early compliance friction in Ontario's resale-cap regime.

### Key Points
- House-approved H.5700 omits Healey's "Great Divide Act" resale restrictions; House Ways & Means chair calls including it elsewhere "a little duplicative," not a policy rejection.
- Competing Senate version (H.5576/S.3228, passed July) shares the 110% resale ceiling but lacks Healey's 10% marketplace-fee cap and differs on how "original price" is defined — differences still being negotiated in conference committee.
- National Consumers League's Breyault warns a resale-only price cap could push transactions to unregulated channels (social media, DMs) while leaving primary-market dynamic pricing untouched — citing a hypothetical $100 ticket facing a $110 resale cap vs. unlimited $300 dynamic primary pricing.
- Ticket Policy Forum (6-marketplace industry group) won't rule out a legal challenge if Massachusetts enacts the cap; it has already sued over Washington D.C.'s resale restrictions.
- Ontario's resale cap (live since April) has produced enforcement actions — C$25,000 penalty against SeatGeek Canada, C$20,000 against StubHub (appealing) — and documented cases of sellers moving listings to Facebook Marketplace to evade the cap.

### Highlights
- Massachusetts House removed the governor's ticket-resale price cap from its budget bill Oct. 21 (152-0), but a similar cap remains alive in House-Senate conference negotiations on a separate bill — no final outcome yet.

### Industry Problem
Legislators are targeting secondary-market resale prices while leaving primary-market fees and dynamic pricing largely untouched, and early real-world experience (Ontario) shows resale caps can push transactions to unregulated channels without necessarily lowering what buyers pay overall.

### Proposed Solution
Monitor the Massachusetts conference-committee outcome and watch for whether any enacted cap defines "original price" in a way that affects primary platforms' own fee/dynamic-pricing structures; no immediate product response needed since Massachusetts has not yet enacted anything.

Massachusetts lawmakers have removed Gov. Maura Healey’s proposed ticket resale price cap from a $2.35 billion supplemental budget bill, dealing a setback to one legislative route for the restrictions even as a similar proposal remains under negotiation elsewhere in the State House.

The [House-approved bill, H.5700](https://malegislature.gov/Bills/194/H5700?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), contains no equivalent of the governor’s proposed restrictions on ticket resale prices and marketplace fees.

The decision does not necessarily signal that Massachusetts lawmakers have abandoned the idea. House Ways and Means Chairman Aaron Michlewitz said the provisions were excluded because a similar proposal is already part of negotiations between the House and Senate over a separate economic development bill.

Michlewitz described including the restrictions in another legislative vehicle as “a little duplicative,” according to [reporting Thursday by MassterList](https://massterlist.com/p/plan-to-cap-ticket-prices-falls-flat-in-the-house-while-resellers-hint-at-a-legal-challenge?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content).

## Massachusetts Price Cap Remains in Legislative Negotiations

Healey’s proposal, known as the Great Divide Act, would generally prevent covered concert and other live-event tickets from being resold for more than 110% of their original price. It would also prohibit speculative ticket listings, restrict deceptive marketplace advertising and cap certain resale marketplace charges at 10% of the ticket’s listed price.

Sporting events would be exempt from the proposed resale-price ceiling, though other provisions would still apply.

As [TicketNews previously reported](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/09/healey-massachusetts-ticket-resale-cap-budget/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), the governor included the proposal in her September supplemental budget, H.5679, after an earlier effort to advance the legislation independently failed to gain sufficient traction.

The state Senate had already approved a version of the restrictions as part of its economic development package, H.5576/S.3228, in July. That legislation remains before a House-Senate conference committee.

The two versions share the proposed 110% resale ceiling but differ in several important respects.

Healey’s version would separately limit marketplace fees to 10% of the resale listing price. The Senate version does not include that fee cap. The proposals also differ in how they define the original ticket price, which venues are covered and what contractual arrangements can authorize resale above the ceiling.

The Senate language expressly includes certain original ticketing fees in its pricing benchmark while excluding taxes, raising additional questions about how the restriction would work in practice.

The governor’s office has maintained that the restrictions would make live events more affordable. In a statement previously provided to TicketNews, Healey press secretary Jacqui Manning said the legislation was intended to lower costs for fans and address excessive resale prices and fees.

Supporters, including artists and music-industry organizations, argue that limiting markups would discourage speculative buying and give more fans an opportunity to purchase tickets at prices closer to their original cost.

But the debate increasingly centers on whether restricting the secondary market, without imposing comparable limits on original sellers, would accomplish that objective.

## Consumer Advocate Warns of Unintended Consequences

John Breyault, vice president of public policy, telecommunications and fraud at the National Consumers League, raised those concerns in an [October 20 opinion article published by CommonWealth Beacon](https://commonwealthbeacon.org/opinion/think-capping-ticket-prices-will-save-you-money-think-again/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) and subsequently republished by Rhode Island Current.

Breyault argued that Massachusetts should address genuine ticketing abuses without adopting restrictions that could strengthen Live Nation and Ticketmaster’s position in the market.

“The Great Divide Act has some smart policies,” Breyault wrote, citing its proposed prohibitions on deceptive websites and speculative ticket listings.

His objection centers on the resale-price ceiling, which he argues could discourage consumers and professional sellers from listing high-demand tickets on established marketplaces such as StubHub and SeatGeek.

Breyault said consumer advocates do not categorically oppose price caps, but argued that such measures require effective enforcement and safeguards against fraud.

The central concern is that limiting what sellers can charge on established resale platforms does not eliminate demand for tickets to sold-out concerts. Instead, buyers and sellers may seek alternative ways to transact, including through social media, direct messaging or private exchanges that offer fewer protections.

Established resale marketplaces generally provide transaction records, customer service and buyer guarantees. Those safeguards may be unavailable when a consumer purchases a ticket directly from an unfamiliar seller.

The argument also highlights an important distinction between original and secondary ticket prices.

A ticket initially offered for $100 could be subject to a $110 resale ceiling under the Massachusetts proposal. But the legislation would not generally prevent the original seller from offering comparable inventory at $300 through premium or dynamically priced ticketing.

That pricing distinction matters because Live Nation-owned Ticketmaster participates in both primary ticketing and resale, while independent resale marketplaces compete primarily for transactions after tickets have already been distributed.

The Ticket Policy Forum, an industry group representing six online ticket marketplaces, has raised similar objections.

Executive Director Brian Berry told MassterList that the organization would not rule out a legal challenge if Massachusetts enacts the restrictions. The group recently filed litigation challenging Washington, D.C.’s new resale-price restrictions.

No comparable Massachusetts lawsuit has been filed. Berry’s comments indicate a possible response if legislation is adopted, rather than an existing legal dispute.

## California and Ontario Offer Lessons for Lawmakers

The Massachusetts debate follows a significant legislative development in California, where lawmakers declined to advance a comparable price-cap proposal during the final weeks of the state’s 2026 legislative session.

Assembly Bill 1720 would have generally limited covered ticket resales to 10% above the original purchase price, with additional restrictions on marketplace fees.

The legislation was substantially narrowed during consideration to focus primarily on qualifying independent venues with capacities of 3,000 or fewer, along with certain nonprofit venues.

California’s Senate Appropriations Committee ultimately held the bill on its suspense file August 13, preventing it from advancing.

As [TicketNews detailed in its coverage of AB 1720](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/08/california-shelves-ticket-resale-price-cap-after-months-of-lawmaker-skepticism/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), legislative analyses raised questions about the economic justification for selecting a 10% threshold, whether the restriction would address the underlying causes of expensive tickets and how independent resale marketplaces could reliably verify original purchase prices.

The legislation also faced fiscal scrutiny. The California Department of Justice estimated that enforcement would cost approximately $1.6 million in the first fiscal year, with substantial continuing expenses.

The Department of Finance opposed the measure based on those costs and potential pressure on the court system.

California’s action does not establish that resale caps inevitably harm consumers. AB 1720 had advanced through several legislative committees and retained support from artists, venues and other industry organizations. Its failure to advance reflected a combination of unresolved policy and fiscal concerns rather than a definitive finding about the effectiveness of price controls.

Ontario, meanwhile, provides a more immediate example of what happens after a resale cap takes effect.

The Canadian province implemented restrictions in April prohibiting the resale of event tickets above their original total purchase price, subject to permitted resale fees and applicable taxes.

Ontario officials have defended the measure as a way to prevent excessive markups and make tickets more affordable for fans.

The law has also created enforcement and compliance challenges.

The province assessed C$25,000 in administrative penalties against SeatGeek Canada over violations involving resale prices, original-price disclosures and proof-of-purchase requirements. StubHub separately received C$20,000 in penalties and said it intended to appeal.

Another issue involves ticket sellers moving toward less-regulated channels.

In July, [Global News documented](https://globalnews.ca/news/12004007/ticket-sellers-moving-lesser-regulated-marketplaces-ontario-government-ticket-resale-legislation/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) Toronto Blue Jays season-ticket holders using Facebook Marketplace to advertise tickets, including listings showing nominal $1 asking prices before actual prices were negotiated privately.

Some sellers said the price cap prevented them from recovering what they could otherwise obtain for high-demand games, even as teams continued to use dynamic pricing for their original inventory.

Those accounts support concerns that some transactions may migrate away from established marketplaces. They do not, however, establish how widespread the shift has become or demonstrate that ticket fraud has increased across Ontario.

The restrictions have also left substantial primary-market charges untouched.

Earlier this month, [TicketNews examined complaints about C$111 in service fees](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/10/backstreet-boys-fees-ontario-resale-cap/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) associated with a pair of Backstreet Boys tickets at Rogers Centre in Toronto.

Those charges were imposed during the original sale, meaning Ontario’s resale-price ceiling did not restrict them.

The distinction illustrates how a government can impose strict limitations on the prices consumers receive when reselling tickets while allowing original sellers to establish prices and collect substantial fees.

## Competition and Consumer Protection Remain Central Questions

For Massachusetts lawmakers, the unresolved issue is whether a resale-price ceiling can deliver meaningful savings without reducing the availability of tickets through regulated marketplaces or giving original ticket issuers additional advantages over independent competitors.

A buyer who successfully obtains a high-demand ticket at the legally permitted resale price could benefit directly from the restriction. But a cap does not create additional tickets, guarantee that holders will resell them or prevent original sellers from charging higher prices at the outset.

The distinction is particularly relevant when tickets are sold at varying original prices, including through dynamic pricing and premium inventory programs.

Independent resale platforms may not have direct access to all original transaction records, complicating efforts to establish the maximum lawful resale price for individual tickets. Primary ticketing platforms are often better positioned to access that information for tickets they originally sold.

Prohibitions on speculative listings, deceptive advertising and misleading marketplace practices have generally attracted broader agreement across competing ticketing interests than resale-price limits.

For now, the Massachusetts House’s decision has removed Healey’s proposal from the supplemental budget without resolving those larger questions.

The economic development conference committee still has the authority to negotiate ticketing provisions, including some form of the Senate’s resale-price restrictions, before sending a final bill to lawmakers.

That leaves the proposal’s future uncertain, with legislators still deciding whether Massachusetts should join states and provinces imposing direct limits on ticket resale prices — and what consumer protections would be needed if it does.
