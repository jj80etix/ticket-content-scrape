# Play One We Know: New platform eyes greater data control for grassroots sector

## Summary

A new UK grassroots platform, Play One We Know (POWK), gives independent artists, venues, and promoters a searchable database of connections plus dashboards on fan, streaming, and transaction data — pitched as a direct answer to musicians' "hand-to-mouth" existence and to fears that streaming services (e.g., Spotify) are ring-fencing fan-relationship data away from artists and their teams. It already counts 100+ artists and 15 UK venues, charges £5/month for premium features (a "health check" career-diagnostic tool plus streaming-data access), and pairs a venue/promoter-matching database (196,000 artists, 9,300 venues, 6,400 other contacts) with free QR-code fan-engagement tools.

### Key Points
- POWK combines a geographically sortable venue/promoter database (196,000 artists, 9,300 venues, 6,400 industry contacts) with QR-code tools for fan follow/donate/purchase actions.
- Core pitch is data ownership: "to have control they've got to have all the data" — fan location, streaming performance, and booking analytics surfaced directly to artists rather than gatekept by labels/managers/streaming platforms.
- Paid tier (£5/month) unlocks a "health check" career-diagnostic and streaming-platform data access; one ambassador used it to book a self-sustaining mini-tour.
- Framed explicitly against industry concern that platforms like Spotify may restrict artist/manager access to fan and transaction data.
- Currently used by 100+ artists and 15 UK venues; founders plan continued feature expansion.

### Highlights
- Scale so far: 100+ artists, 15 UK venues on the platform; underlying database spans 196,000 artists, 9,300 venues, 6,400 other contacts.
- Pricing: £5/month for the premium "health check" + streaming-data tier; core matching/database features are free.
- Positioned as a direct response to the data-access dispute over Spotify potentially restricting fan/transaction data to artist teams.

### Industry Problem
Grassroots artists, venues, and promoters lack affordable, self-serve tools to see their own fan, transaction, and streaming data or to find booking connections — leaving early-career acts dependent on managers or larger platforms and vulnerable to "hand-to-mouth" income cycles.

### Proposed Solution
POWK's answer is a low-cost (£5/mo) data-and-connections layer purpose-built for the grassroots tier. For a primary ticketing platform, the signal to watch is whether client demand (venues/promoters, especially smaller ones) shifts toward wanting first-party fan/transaction data and career-planning tooling bundled with ticketing — a segment currently outside Etix's typical client tier but worth monitoring as a whitespace/adjacency signal.

The UK-based digital platform emphasises "self-sufficiency" for live professionals, saying "to have control they've got to have the data"

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Crowd-in-Liverpool-©-Day-Fever-2-scaled.jpg)

© Day Fever

A new UK-based digital grassroots platform called Play One We Know (POWK) aims to give independent artists, venues, and promoters greater control over key fan, streaming, and transaction data, its founders say, as it sets its sights on combatting the “hand-to-mouth” existence of live music careers.

The platform has two sides, one which gives users access to a database of venues and promoters, which can be geographically sorted to match acts with connections in desired locations, and another which provides QR codes for artists and venues to direct fans to follow, donate, or purchase from them.

Co-founder Gerard Gibbons explains the origins of the project to *IQ*, saying it began as a tool to help grassroots venues manage donations, funding, and memberships, before developing to include data features to help artists manage and plan their live bookings. It emerged as a joint project between “lifelong gig-goer” Gibbons and technology entrepreneur Gerard Toplass.

“A lot of people who run venues are musicians who used to be in bands, and the next step is to become involved in promotion or running venues. But the commerciality of running a venue isn’t necessarily their strength. That’s how the offer came together,” he says

The platform will continue to add features as it grows, Gibbons says, as the service currently counts over 100 artists and 15 UK venues among its users. However, while on a basic level the platform lets venues and artists fundraise and manage followers, Gibbons, who is also director of celebrated grassroots venue [The New Adelphi in Hull](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/04/tributes-paid-to-uk-grassroots-icon-paul-jackson/), says the fundamental mission of the platform is give artists greater “control” over their growing careers. Central to this is data around their fan followings, transactions, and access to promoters and venues.

> “ Artists can set themselves a music business plan for the next 12–24 months. It’s not just hand‑to‑mouth”

“ We want the artist to have control, and to have control they’ve got to have all the data. Fan data is absolutely key. But there’s also more data around where they’re popular. If they can’t find out where their fans are, they might never have played there,” Gibbons tells *IQ*.

“They might say they’re from Bristol and have never played in Barrow‑in‑Furness, but it appears they’ve got a real pocket of interest there. So they go to Barrow‑in‑Furness,” he says. “That’s how scenes build. We want artists to find those opportunities and build things themselves, not rely on management or others in the food chain.”

As part of this, both artists and venues can view central dashboards with access to sortable fan information, earnings targets, promotional campaigns, analytics, and planning tools. On the promoter side, Gibbons says: “If you’re a promoter and want bands from a certain postcode or town, you can filter down to that. And vice versa: if you’re a band from Bristol wanting to play Newcastle, you can draw out all the venues, it’ll give you capacity, emails, all the data you need, and then you can pick out promoters as well.”

The co-founder says the focus on artists’ control of data is timely, coming against a backdrop of [concern amongst artist managers](https://www.iqmagazine.com/2026/07/who-owns-the-fan-relationship-agents-raise-questions-over-spotify-reserved/) and agents about streaming companies like Spotify potentially ring-fencing fan and transaction information seen as key to tour planning.

Contrasting with this, Gibbons says with POWK, “ artists can also get all their information from all the streaming services — where they are, whether they’re growing or not — and see global data about themselves”.

“In a given place, they can pull all the stuff they might get from streaming services and radio stations,” he explains. And while the fallout around access to fan data circulates around international acts, Gibbons argues control over the process can be all the more important at grassroots level as artists try to grow in their early careers.

He concedes that putting artists at the helm in terms of this minutiae “might be unpopular with some people in the industry but very popular with others”, but that ultimately, POWK’s goal is for artists to be “more self-sufficient”.

> “ We want the artist to have control, and to have control they’ve got to have all the data”

But he adds using the service need not mean taking on all the duties associated with management teams for artists, and notes POWK can be used “as much or as little as you want”.

“The more you’re minded towards being serious about a career in music, the more you’ll use it, the more questions you’ll ask, and the better POWK will get,” he says.

Some of these more “serious” features include what POWK calls a “health check”. By filling out a set of questions around artist style, level of establishment, and existing following, the platform identifies “where your strengths are, where you might not realise you’ve got a gap,” Gibbons says.

“One of our ambassadors, Brooke Ellen, an artist from Manchester, filled it in as she was setting up. It basically said she needed to do more gigs. On the back of that, she got in touch with a few promoters and other artists she knows, and got herself a mini‑tour in July and August. It’s going to cover her for about six months’ worth of living, just from realising that’s what she needed to do.”

With the feature, artists can also set earnings targets to make a sustainable living, which prompts the platform to produce insights from their career analytics to advise artists how to meet them.

“Only \[artists\] know how much they need to be sustainable,” Gibbons says. “They can set their own targets. We can show them, for example, how many streams they’d need to earn minimum wage. But if they did 50 gigs a year and set a target of getting £100 of donations and support per gig, that could replace all that streaming and make them sustainable at the number they want.”

“ It’s about giving them a target. It’s exciting — they can set themselves a music business plan for the next 12–24 months. It’s not just hand‑to‑mouth,” he adds.

The ‘health check’ comes as part of a paid membership of £5 per month, which also gives artists and venues access to streaming platform data and POWK’s full database, known as Mr IQ, while other features are free to use. Explaining the function of the full database, Gibbons says: “Different people in the sector will have different questions and require different information, and they can select that from the database.”

The database currently includes 196,000 artists, 9,300, and 6,400 other industry contacts, aimed at connecting artists, promoters, and venues to facilitate live shows.

Summing up the goals of the project, Gibbons says the key word is “sust ainability” for those making a living in live music, whether artist, promoter or venue. “How do they make themselves sustainable if they’re serious about a life in music? They’ve got to do it differently. And we feel that Play One We Know is a really big cog to help artists and venues keep that sustainability,” Gibbons says.
