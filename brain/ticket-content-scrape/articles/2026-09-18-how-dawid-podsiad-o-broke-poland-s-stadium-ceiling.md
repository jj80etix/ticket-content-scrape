# How Dawid Podsiadło broke Poland’s stadium ceiling

## Summary

Polish singer Dawid Podsiadło has sold over one million tickets on his three-year 360° tour without playing outside Poland, a milestone his manager frames as proof of the domestic market's maturation from a scene once reliant on sponsor/state-funded free shows to one supporting genuine stadium-level acts.

### Key Points
- 1M+ tickets sold across a three-year, all-domestic touring cycle.
- Poland's major-venue infrastructure only began scaling after 2012 (tied to UEFA Euro 2012 venue upgrades).
- Less than seven years ago, no domestic act had reached stadium level in Poland.
- Manager describes a "reverse-engineered residency" touring strategy behind the growth.

## Transcript

Selling over a million tickets on his 360° tour, the Polish-language star has demonstrated the market's maturation as a touring destination

![Dawid Podsiadło's 360° tour](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Dawid-Podsiadlos-360°-tour-©-Michal-Murawski.png)

Dawid Podsiadło's 360° tour © Michał Murawski

Having sold over one million tickets on his 360° tour, Polish superstar Dawid Podsiadło has broken through the ceiling of the country’s live market – and done so without ever leaving it. His manager Maciej Woć unpacks the pop singer’s rapid rise and ‘reverse-engineered residency’ strategy in an in-depth interview with *IQ*.

Podsiadło recently wrapped another six-city run of his ongoing trek, which has run for three years and taken the artist around his native country and back again. The achievement is made even more impressive by the fact that, less than seven years ago, no domestic act had reached stadium level, with the major venues largely reserved for international stars.

“The Polish concert scene has changed dramatically over the past 10 years – the growth is crazy,” Muzk Management CEO Woć tells *IQ*.

Large-scale Polish events, he explains, once relied heavily on financing by sponsors or the state and were offered free of charge to audiences, while acts made their money from clubs and smaller arena shows. Major concert infrastructure only really began to take shape after 2012, when Poland’s venue network was updated to host the UEFA European Championship.

“Before 2019, due to the way the market was structured, it was unheard of for a local act to sell out a stadium,” he says. But that year, Podsiadło and fellow Polish star Taco Hemingway rose to the challenge, selling out a co-headlining show at Warsaw’s National Stadium in two hours and breaking the barrier for domestic artists.

Over a little more than a decade, Podsiadło has progressed from clubs to stadiums, becoming the first Polish-language act to complete a solo headlining stadium tour in 2021. His concept has evolved in recent years, with the team developing a 360° production, with which he has now played to over a million fans at 14 sold-out shows.

For a market like Poland, the scale is “unprecedented and definitely record-breaking,” Woć says. It is made all the more remarkable by the fact that Podsiadło has never toured outside his home country.

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/smush-webp/2026/09/Dawid-Podsiadlo-©-Michal-Murawski-430x269.png.webp)

The infrastructure required to operate a 360° show at this scale also reveals how much Poland’s top tier of touring has matured. On a fundamental level, going in-the-round changes almost every aspect of touring productions, with complexity rising dramatically for every party involved.

Unlike other major 360° productions that are transported from country to country, like those of Ed Sheeran or BTS, Podsiadło’s team has been able to take advantage of a domestic-only route, allowing them to bump up the footprint and intricacy of the production.

“Because we knew we were touring only across the local market, we could afford to do it a bit bigger and a bit more complex,” Woć says.

By creating a Polish-built spectacle, his management describes the tour as a “reverse-engineered” residency, somewhat akin to the music tourism trend seen as a result of other residency-style runs.

With over 37 million people, the population is concentrated enough to make most major cities a viable touring stop, while the country’s size allows each audience to be distinct and each stop to be accessible for travelling fans. The balance is attractive for the country’s publicly operated venues, with Woć saying their partnership is an important piece of the equation.

“Our input and the outcome of our activities, measured in the economic impact, often helps us to establish this great partnership because we can prove that we’re bringing tens of thousands of people moving across Poland and visiting cities,” Woć says, who says the shows’ audiences are split down the middle in terms of residents versus visiting fans.

The scale within their native market forms the core of why the Polish-language artist and his team haven’t looked to take the show outside of the country, instead relying on fans from the Polish diaspora to travel into the market. Around 5-7% of the shows’ audience, Woć estimates, come from abroad.

> “We’ve been completely redesigning it and offering a completely different experience”

“Due to the scale of the domestic touring, it would be very difficult for us to arrange a comparable scale of touring outside of the domestic market, and scaling it completely down wasn’t really interesting,” he says, citing his Polish discography as a reason why they haven’t looked outside the country.

In order to keep attracting fans, both inside and outside of Poland, the production has vastly evolved in its three-year run.

“Even though we played only three shows and were coming back a year or two years after, we’ve been completely redesigning it and offering a completely different experience,” Woć says.

The first 360° production was entirely locally produced, something the team regards as an important milestone for the Polish live industry, while fresher editions introduced new technology – including the debut of a new PixMob LED wristband – and new repertoire. The regular reinvention is an important part of the strategy, with the greatest threat to commercial success coming from oversaturation.

“You can’t grow forever,” the manager says. “It needs to be interesting. It needs to be surprising. It needs to be fresh all the time.”

When considering what the next form is, the team is looking beyond scale. Podsiadło has already sold out back-to-back shows at Poland’s biggest stadiums, shifting around 100,000 in Chorzów and another 95,000 in Warsaw.

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/smush-webp/2026/09/Dawid-Podsiadlos-tour-©-Michal-Murawski-430x269.png.webp)

Their strategy, instead, is to move laterally, with the team already plotting the return of Podsiadło’s touring music festival ZORZA, powered by T-Mobile, next year.

“When it comes to scaling, we will look for different ways because, in our case, it wouldn’t be about scaling; it’s more about development of the concept,” Woć says. “It might mean that we will scale it down and up again after some time, because you can’t grow forever.”

Behind the scenes, Podsiadło’s rapid rise has helped his management team build a full-service live business, one capable of applying the same expertise to other artists and tours as they scale both domestically and, potentially, abroad.

For a market that has historically operated according to a unique live model, his rise represents a fundamental shift in the Polish concert business. More significantly, it has been achieved almost entirely in-house: a locally developed production, a national touring strategy, alliances with publicly operated venues, and a model built around keeping audiences moving.

In the meantime, Podsiadło and his team will stay the course.

“There’s a saturation point somewhere in the distance – but we haven’t found it yet,” Woć concludes.
