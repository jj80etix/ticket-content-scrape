# Festival Cordillera on Latin America’s evolving currency-resilient playbook

## Summary

Colombia's Festival Cordillera and its parent company Páramo Presenta describe a currency-resilient playbook for Latin American festival promoters, including multi-festival "consortium" block booking, dollar-denominated artist contracts, financial hedges, and a deliberate policy of not relying on early-bird ticket sales as a financing tool. Co-founder Sergio Pabón discusses how promoters are grouping together across the region to negotiate lineups and offset peso/dollar volatility ahead of the festival's September 12–13 dates in Bogotá. The piece is a promoter-economics feature focused on booking and treasury strategy rather than ticketing technology or platforms.

### Key Points
- Latin American promoters are forming informal "circuits" to jointly block-book higher-tier artists and share costs across festivals.
- Most Spanish-language headline artists now charge in US dollars, adding to currency exposure for regional promoters.
- Páramo Presenta uses financial hedges and predetermined target exchange rates in artist contracts to lock in costs.
- The company explicitly avoids using early-bird ticket sales as a cash-flow/financing mechanism for artist deposits.
- Rising global logistics costs (transport, hotels, equipment) are compounding currency-driven cost pressure.
- Festival Cordillera runs September 12–13, 2026 at Simón Bolívar Metropolitan Park in Bogotá, with a lineup including Ricky Martin and Sean Paul.

## Festival Cordillera on Latin America’s evolving currency-resilient playbook

Páramo Presenta says "circles are being shaped" to bring a consortium approach to booking as part of the region's growing financial toolkit

![](https://www.iqmagazine.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Sergio-Pabon.png)

Sergio Pabón © Páramo Presenta

Promoters across Latin America are increasingly using multi-festival booking leverage in their toolkit to mitigate exchange rate fluctuations, according to Colombia’s Festival Cordillera, as the event tells *IQ* about honing its strategies for currency resilience in the region.

The festival’s co-founder and lead promoter of Páramo Presenta, Sergio Pabón, tells *IQ* “circles are being shaped” across the region to offset fluctuations and volatility of local currencies against the US dollar, which remains one of the steepest financial hurdles for mass-event promoters.

The promoter says Páramo is actively coordinating with other South American companies to unify artist routes and offer block booking in a bid to lower costs collectively, with Pabón saying: “More and more, we manage to offer artists a block of concerts, and gradually, circuits are being shaped.”

He explains this consortium approach is targeted primarily at a higher echelon of artist which typically give a festival its raison d’être, knowing that this provides an event with leeway to play with when filling out its bill.

“They are artists who undoubtedly guarantee a significant number of ticket sales and, above all, guarantee the quality and identity of the festival. The success of the festival lies in combining these legacy artists with younger artists who are trending and who, on many occasions, also guarantee a relevant number of ticket sales.”

Being part of a structure like the Páramo Presenta holding company grants advantages withint this strategy, Pabón explains. When asked how his negotiation stance shifts in the international market when securing artist packages for multiple regional festivals simultaneously, Pabón highlights the value of loyalty.

Explaining the approach, he says: “Páramo Presenta has allowed us to build very long-term relationships with artists. We don’t just look for them to be part of the festival once. Most of the time, we look to develop their careers in Colombia, and gradually, we have earned their trust by betting on the long term and showing them that we truly care.”

Ahead of Festival Cordillera’s gathering on 12-13 September at Simón Bolívar Metropolitan Park in Bogotá, with a lineup including Andrés Calamaro, Ricky Martin, Caifanes, Sean Paul, and Beéle, Pabón adds that strict artists curation is also growing as a focus across the regional sector as a tactic to shield against exposure to exchange rates.

Pabón tells *IQ* that while the curation team aims to “sit down to build \[lineups\] in the same way we always do”, currency instability often means “the financial team works hard on strategies that allow us to protect ourselves as much as possible against this volatility.”

Typical strategies to combat this include tying ticket prices to US dollars or agreeing a predetermined target exchange rate in artist contracts which trigger organisers buying the required amount of the appropriate currency when the rate is reached.

> “The community is everything to us. We do not view early birds as a financing tool”

But with more Spanish language artists, especially headline-level talent, charging festivals in US dollars, booking big names for proudly Spanish language events is becoming more similar to booking Anglophone acts from overseas.

“Most Spanish-language artists charge in dollars. Fortunately, there are also Spanish-speaking giants. It doesn’t work much differently compared to a festival featuring Anglo artists.”

Maintaining the right budget balance without diluting the lineup poses a management challenge. When asked what criteria they apply to safeguard the event’s economic health and continue opening vital showcases for local talent without losing the international prestige that the public demands at the box office, his response points to consistency.

“Cordillera Festival’s approach has always been to combine established artists with new talent, maintaining the identity and quality of the festival”.

Primarily, though, Cordillera is adamant that currency concerns must not “affect the principles under which we curate the festival”, and this means looking to areas outside of artist fees to minimise currency-related liabilities.

When asked about tangible financial tools implemented by Páramo Presenta to mitigate risks, lock in currency values, or secure stable exchange rates before launching the first early-bird ticket window, Pabón simplifies the corporate strategy: “There are financial hedges and other tools that allow us to lock in the exchange rate at which a deal will be executed.”

However, he adds that this need not result in a festival employing extraordinary contractual clauses, praising the Cordillera team for “solid financial planning that protects the festival against a potential devaluation.”

Timing of artist payments is a key determiner of how well a festival can be insulated against any currency instabilities, with early cash flow sometimes being crucial to this. For many festivals, the strategic role of early-bird presale phases are importnat for generating the immediate liquidity demanded by the initial deposits of international agencies, but Pabón says Páramo’s approach to this stands them in good stead in volatile conditions.

“Páramo Presenta is a company that does not rely on early-bird sales to pay the artists’ first deposits. Early birds, both for Cordillera Festival and Estéreo Picnic Festival, are a way to reward fans who attend the festival because they love the experience we offer, regardless of the potential lineup we might have. That community is everything to us. We do not view early birds as a financing tool.”

> “it’s clear that for visitors from countries with stronger currencies than ours, it represents a great opportunity in financial terms.”

Assembling infrastructure for large-scale festivals is also traditionally associated with high-cost technical equipment, either imported at a premium or paid for in a local currency that that is susceptible to exchange rate fluctuations.

However, when asked how negotiation dynamics with local sponsors and banking entities have transformed to turn them into capital partners capable of cushioning inflation, Pabón forcefully advocates for the domestic market: “I can say with great pride that practically nothing is imported. Colombia is a country with an impressive technical infrastructure, featuring premium-quality equipment managed by top-tier professionals.”

“On the other hand, conversations with sponsors haven’t changed. We have always seen them as allies, and they are part of the festival’s identity. Most sponsors have been with us for several years and are part of the team,” he adds.

But with rising costs in global supply chains, he is frank about the operation’s susceptibility to macro factors, even down to specific trade agreements, with elements such as transport, hotel, and logistics partnerships presenting the risk of excessively using crucial cash reserves.

“It’s true that logistics costs have skyrocketed. Unfortunately, there isn’t much we can do about that. Artist cargo, airfare, hotels, and general festival expenses have been rising year after year. All agreements we make with suppliers to find pricing that makes the festival viable are essential. They are also part of the team that makes this festival possible.”

Music fans’ enthusiasm is undeniable, but purchasing power has clear ceilings, he notes.

“It’s a very complex situation. We do our best to ensure that ticket prices don’t rise beyond the CPI year after year, although it’s not always possible to do so given the escalation of costs.”

> “solid financial planning that protects the festival against a potential devaluation”

This inflationary pressure inevitably carries over into the venue, with these repercussions felt across the festival’s micro-economy, specifically when it comes to bars and food courts. A cashless consumption ecosystem is one measure designed to safeguard the profitability of this part of the festival, he says.

“Everything has an impact. As costs become more expensive, the prices of products sold at the festival and the overall attendee experience inevitably become more expensive. We fight to keep it from being that way and to ensure prices make sense for attendees, but it is undoubtedly becoming increasingly difficult,” he explains.

However, Pabón stresses the currency situation is a double-edged sword, and that the ‘playbook’ also includes marketing to overseas visitors whose currencies convert favourably to the Colombian Peso, thus drawing in fans with elevated spending power.

“We run many campaigns outside of Colombia,” he says. “The festival is highly loved across Latin America, and each year we see more visitors from other countries. Coming to Cordillera is incredible for many reasons. However, it’s clear that for visitors from countries with stronger currencies than ours, it represents a great opportunity in financial terms.”

In efforts to battle the perennial problem of currency volatility, a balancing act as delicate as this can still be maintained without compromising the artistic mission at the heart of Cordillera, the promoter says. If anything, he adds, the region’s events are forced into a more careful and self-assured approach.

“It doesn’t slow us down. It forces us to plan better and be more careful,” he says. “Cordillera Festival has the essential purpose of presenting the greatest Spanish-language artists in the world, as well as a showcase of talents of all sizes that represent our culture and our roots. That responsibility takes precedence over everything else.”
