# Study: Resellers, Online Ticketers Add $11B To Cost Of Live Events Annually

## Summary

A Vanderbilt Policy Accelerator white paper estimates ticket resellers and online ticketing platforms together add $11 billion a year to live-event costs: roughly $4.5-5.2B from broker resale markups and $6.8B from platform service fees exceeding a proposed 10% cap. The paper argues resale brokers add no production value and that primary platforms hold show-level monopoly power (venues pick one exclusive platform), so fees escape competitive pressure. It calls for banning above-face-value resale and capping platform fees at 10%; NIVA's executive director endorsed the findings as ammunition for resale price-gouging legislation.

### Key Points
- Total estimated harm: $11B/year, split ~$4.5-5.2B from broker resale markups and ~$6.8B from platform fees above a 10% benchmark.
- Methodology for the reseller estimate uses StubHub markup data and a New York state study, net of the ~$414M/year brokers reportedly save consumers on mispriced (below-face) resales.
- Paper's core argument: brokers add no production value; platforms operate as per-show monopolies since venues select one exclusive ticketing provider, insulating fees from competition.
- Recommends an outright ban on above-face-value resale and a 10% cap on platform fees.
- NIVA (National Independent Venue Association) executive director Stephen Parker publicly endorsed the paper as evidence for state/federal resale price-gouging legislation.

### Highlights
- $11B annual total; $4.5-5.2B broker markup, $6.8B platform fees above 10%.
- Proposed policy: ban resale above face value + 10% fee cap.
- NIVA is actively using this study to push legislators, governors, and state AGs toward resale-cap and fee-cap laws.

### Industry Problem
The paper frames platform service fees themselves — not just resale markups — as a regulatory target, arguing per-show exclusivity makes fee levels a monopoly-pricing problem rather than a competitive market outcome; this directly targets the fee structure primary platforms like Etix depend on.

### Proposed Solution
No product fix is proposed in the source; it's an advocacy paper aimed at legislation (10% fee cap, resale ban above face value). For Etix: treat this as a leading indicator of state-level fee-cap bills (already trending per Q9/Q11) — worth monitoring which legislators cite this study, and worth having transparent, defensible fee breakdowns ready ahead of any all-in-pricing or fee-cap mandate.

A new study says that large-scale ticket resellers and online ticketing platforms inflate the price of live events by $11 billion annually.

[The white paper](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-URL/wp-content/uploads/sites/412/2026/08/03190151/How-to-Save-Audiences-11-Billion-on-Live-Events.pdf) from the Vanderbilt Policy Accelerator argues that in tandem, those two factors have a push-and-pull effect that creates wildly inflated prices.

“The core problem with the modern ticket broker industry is that it inflates the price of live events without providing any value to either customers or event producers. The brokers contribute nothing to the actual production of live events. And unlike other ‘brokers,’ like a mortgage or real estate broker for example, they do not help consumers navigate and make decisions in a competitive market of providers. Their sole economic function is to profit by increasing the price of a good that someone else is already producing, promoting, and selling at retail,” the paper reads. “Conversely, the main problem with the online ticketing platforms’ service charges is that they are charged in a monopoly environment, and as a predictable consequence, they are far too high. Customers cannot choose between platforms because the venue, not the customer, selects a single platform that is exclusive for practical reasons. Regardless of how competitive the primary ticket platform market is across multiple shows, it is usually a monopoly as to a particular show. Therefore, platforms do not face competitive pressure and are able to charge monopoly prices.”

The paper’s author estimates that resellers alone add $4.5 billion to the cost of tickets, using data from StubHub and a study from the state of New York on average above-face mark-up.

“Brokers do sometimes sell below face value when they misread demand. Protect Ticket Rights, a ticket-resale advocacy group, claims this happens about 10 million times a year and saves consumers $414 million. Because the markup averages cited above already net out those sales, brokers upsold by roughly $4.6 to $5.2 billion. In other words, for every $1 consumers save from the broker market, they lose about $12,” the paper says.

The paper further estimates the average fees charged by ticketing platforms adds another 20% to the cost. The study concedes that some fees are necessary to operate the platforms and generate a profit, but says that because Ticketmaster and its competitors have continued to operate in states that have already implemented 10% fee caps anything above 10% is unnecessary rent seeking and adds $6.8 billion.

The paper calls for an outright ban on “ticket profiteering” – that is a bar on any sale above face value — and a fee cap of 10%.

“Vanderbilt has given Congress, governors, state legislators, and attorneys general the most convincing evidence we’ve seen that resale price gouging has to be reined in. This report uncovers that fans and consumers lose $12 on the resale market for every $1 that scalper advocacy groups claim they save,” NIVA executive director Stephen Parker says, “That is shocking, and it calls for action. Protecting fans with ticket resale price gouging regulations, allowing for resale at the original ticket price or less, is the future of resale in America.”
