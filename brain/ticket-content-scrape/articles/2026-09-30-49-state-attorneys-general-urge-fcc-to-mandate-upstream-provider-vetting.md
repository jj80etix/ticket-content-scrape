# 49 State Attorneys General Urge FCC to Mandate Upstream Provider Vetting

## Summary

A bipartisan 49-state/territory AG coalition, led by North Carolina AG Jeff Jackson, filed FCC reply comments pushing to make "Know Your Upstream Provider" vetting rules mandatory (rather than discretionary) for voice providers, arguing current flexibility hasn't stopped illegal robocall traffic.

### Key Points
- Filed Sept. 8, 2026, in response to a May 20, 2026 FCC Further Notice proposing five mandatory KYUP baseline categories.
- Coalition endorses all five categories and asks the FCC to go further on enforcement.
- Current rules (since 2022) leave "reasonable steps" to vet upstream providers undefined; AGs say that's failed.
- Telecom/robocall regulatory story; no direct ticketing-industry application.

## Transcript

On September 8, 2026, a bipartisan coalition of 49 state and territorial attorneys general filed [reply comments with the Federal Communications Commission (FCC)](https://ncdoj.gov/attorney-general-jeff-jackson-leads-bipartisan-coalition-pushing-federal-government-to-strengthen-know-your-upstream-provider-rules-to-combat-illegal-robocalls/), urging the agency to make its “Know Your Upstream Provider” (KYUP) requirements mandatory for voice service providers. The coalition is led by North Carolina Attorney General Jeff Jackson, who also chairs the Anti-Robocall Multistate Litigation Task Force. The filing follows the coalition’s July 27 letter to the FCC seeking stronger vetting obligations for originating voice service providers and extends that position to providers that accept and route traffic further down the call path.

### Background

A call typically passes through several providers before it reaches a consumer. Each provider accepts traffic from the provider before it in the chain, referred to as its upstream provider. Since 2022, FCC rules have required a voice service provider to take reasonable steps to ensure that its upstream providers are not using its network to carry high volumes of illegal calls. The current rules do not specify what those steps are, leaving each provider to determine what diligence is reasonable.

In a Further Notice adopted May 20, 2026, the FCC proposed replacing that discretion with a prescribed baseline covering five categories: collecting information about upstream providers, reviewing their compliance, verifying the information collected, monitoring them on an ongoing basis, and taking responsive action, including refusing or discontinuing service to providers that are not following the law. Comments closed August 10, with reply comments due September 8.

In their reply comments, the attorneys general argue that flexibility has not worked. Providers have been subject to these obligations since 2022 — and to potential liability under the Telemarketing Sales Rule and the Telephone Consumer Protection Act before that — but illegal traffic continues to reach consumers at scale. “That’s why we’re asking the FCC to go further,” Jackson said in announcing the filing, “and put in place requirements that actually stop these calls from passing through the network to begin with.”

### Where the Coalition Agrees and Asks for More

The coalition endorses all five proposed categories, and its additions are largely about specificity — replacing standards a provider can satisfy on paper with requirements it must actually perform.

- **Verified addresses, not corporate formalities.** The comments ask providers to collect verified physical places of business and treat registered agents, virtual addresses, P.O. boxes, and shared suites as insufficient. They also seek identification of human principals, owners, parents, and affiliates, aimed at shell entities and nominal executives.
- **Public filings are not proof of compliance****.** The attorneys general urge the FCC to direct providers not to treat a Robocall Mitigation Database entry or a Form 499-A filing as establishing compliance, citing undeliverable subpoenas sent to database addresses and facially defective mitigation plans. They also ask that state attorney general enforcement actions be added to the data points a provider must weigh.
- **Fixed intervals, not event-driven review.** A provider today generally reviews an upstream customer when entering or renewing an agreement, or when there is evidence that the customer may be breaking the law. The coalition instead asks for recurring monitoring on annual timelines, with a 10-day window for upstream providers to report changes.
- **Consequences that attach.** The comments seek per-call base forfeitures, retention of vetting records for the full limitations period, and no exemption for small providers, on the theory that bad actors will route traffic wherever scrutiny is lightest.

The coalition makes a parallel request regarding STIR/SHAKEN, the caller ID authentication framework, arguing that the entities issuing and overseeing its credentials should face the same vetting requirements.

### Where the Coalition Parts With the FCC

The reply is not a blanket endorsement. The FCC proposed a safe harbor that would shield a provider from enforcement when its upstream provider holds a Service Provider Code token under STIR/SHAKEN. The attorneys general oppose it, arguing that holding a credential is not a substitute for diligence. They do support a separate safe harbor for good-faith decisions to refuse or discontinue service.

### Why It Matters

There is no rule yet. The comment cycle has closed, but no order has been circulated and no vote has been scheduled. Providers are evaluating their vetting practices against a proposal, not a requirement. Whatever the FCC ultimately adopts will be built on the record that closed September 8.

Paper compliance is the specific target. The through line in the coalition’s additions is a distrust of documentation that can be produced without being true. A provider whose onboarding file consists of a database entry, a certification, and a corporate registration is describing the type of practice the attorneys general are asking the FCC to declare insufficient.

The comments accompany an active enforcement effort. The Anti-Robocall Multistate Litigation Task Force launched Operation Robocall Roundup in 2025. In its first phase, it sent warning letters to 37 smaller voice providers; the FCC has since ordered six of them to address their robocall mitigation practices or risk losing the ability to route calls. A second phase extended the effort to four of the largest intermediate providers. Positions advanced in these comments should be expected to surface in enforcement theories, whether or not the FCC adopts them as rules.
