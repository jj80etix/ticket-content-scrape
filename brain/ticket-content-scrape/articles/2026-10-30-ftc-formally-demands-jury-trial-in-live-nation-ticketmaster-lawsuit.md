# FTC Formally Demands Jury Trial in Live Nation, Ticketmaster Lawsuit

## Summary

The FTC formally demanded a jury trial (filed October 27, Doc. 146 in Case No. 2:25-cv-08884, C.D. Cal.) in its lawsuit against Live Nation and Ticketmaster, formalizing a position the agency and seven participating states had outlined in an October 13 scheduling report. The FTC/states want an April 24, 2028 jury trial; Live Nation/Ticketmaster had proposed an October 2, 2028 bench trial before a judge alone.

### Key Points
- Filed October 27 in U.S. District Court, Central District of California
- FTC's September 2025 suit alleges Live Nation/Ticketmaster let brokers evade purchase limits via large account networks, then collected extra fees on resulting resales despite having detection technology
- Suit also challenges historical pricing disclosures — alleging advertised prices excluded mandatory fees and the companies misrepresented purchase-restriction effectiveness
- Government alleges violations of the BOTS Act plus federal/state consumer-protection law; Live Nation/Ticketmaster deny wrongdoing and argue they shouldn't bear responsibility for third-party broker abuse
- Filing is procedural only — doesn't itself set a jury trial or trial date

### Highlights
- Core allegations combine two of the industry's most consequential compliance threads: BOTS Act bot/broker enforcement and all-in/junk-fee pricing disclosure
- Jury vs. bench trial choice (requested by the plaintiffs) suggests FTC/states expect a jury to be more sympathetic to deceptive-pricing/broker-harm allegations than a judge

### Industry Problem
The suit crystallizes two of the most operationally consequential regulatory threats to a primary ticketing platform: (1) liability exposure when fee-inclusive ("all-in") pricing disclosures are later found misleading, and (2) liability for failing to enforce purchase limits against broker networks despite having the technical means to detect them.

### Proposed Solution
No product fix is proposed in the article itself; the posture here is monitor/compliance-readiness — Etix should treat this as a forcing function to audit its own all-in pricing disclosure language and broker/bot-detection enforcement now, before similar FTC scrutiny could extend beyond Ticketmaster to the broader primary-ticketing industry.

The Federal Trade Commission has formally demanded a jury trial in its ongoing lawsuit against Live Nation and Ticketmaster, advancing a procedural dispute over how the companies will face allegations involving deceptive ticket pricing, ticket purchase limits and resale practices.

The demand was filed October 27 in the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California, according to federal court records. The filing, entered as Document 146 in Case No. 2:25-cv-08884.

The request formalizes a position the FTC and seven participating states had already outlined in an October 13 joint scheduling report. Regulators proposed an April 24, 2028, jury trial, while Live Nation and Ticketmaster proposed an October 2, 2028, bench trial, in which a judge rather than a jury would decide the case.

The filing does not itself establish that the case will be heard by a jury or set a trial date.

The FTC’s September 2025 lawsuit accuses Live Nation and Ticketmaster of knowingly allowing professional ticket brokers to circumvent purchasing restrictions by operating large networks of accounts, then facilitating the resale of tickets acquired through those practices.

Regulators allege that Ticketmaster collected additional fees on those transactions despite possessing technology capable of identifying brokers exceeding purchase limits. The complaint also challenges the company’s historical pricing disclosures, alleging that advertised ticket prices excluded mandatory fees and that the company misrepresented the effectiveness of its purchase restrictions.

The government argues that the conduct violated the Better Online Ticket Sales Act, commonly known as the BOTS Act, along with federal and state consumer-protection laws.

Live Nation and Ticketmaster deny the allegations and have argued that regulators are improperly attempting to hold ticketing platforms responsible for purchasing abuses committed by third parties.

Earlier this month, the companies contend that the [FTC’s interpretation of the BOTS Act](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/10/live-nation-ftc-lawsuit-anti-bot-protections/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content) could discourage ticketing platforms from implementing stronger anti-bot protections by exposing them to liability whenever brokers successfully circumvent those measures.

The FTC, meanwhile, maintains that its case concerns more than Ticketmaster’s inability to prevent every purchasing violation. Regulators allege that the company knowingly tolerated certain brokers’ activities and financially benefited when tickets obtained beyond stated limits were subsequently resold.

In September, U.S. District Judge Maame Ewusi-Mensah Frimpong [rejected Live Nation and Ticketmaster’s motion to dismiss the lawsuit](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/09/ticketmaster-ftc-bots-act-lawsuit-dismissal-denied/?utm_source=TicketNews&utm_medium=content), allowing the government’s claims to proceed. That ruling determined that the complaint was legally sufficient to move forward, not that the companies had violated the law.

The California proceeding is separate from the Justice Department’s antitrust litigation against Live Nation and Ticketmaster in New York.

The parties must still navigate discovery, pretrial proceedings and the disagreement over whether the case should ultimately be decided by a jury or a judge.
